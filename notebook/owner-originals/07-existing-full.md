# The Man Who Made The Necklace


The shopkeeper explained how he created the necklace for a lady some time ago. She wanted to stay hidden from the world, and remain undetectable.

He said it plainly, the way a man reads a receipt. Then he stopped, and the plainness went out of his voice.

"I know my own work," he said. "I made this string. I carved that stone. I have not seen it in a very long time."

Felix looked at Addie. Addie looked at Felix. Neither of them said anything.

The man came around the counter. He was older than he had looked from across the room, with white in his beard and grease worked into the lines of his hands, and he moved like the shop was a thing he had to be careful in. He put the necklace down on a square of felt and turned the little lamp on it.

"Sit," he said, and pointed at two stools. "You are going to want to sit."

They sat. Felix had his hands in his lap. Addie had hers on the counter, and she kept looking at the door.

"A woman came in here a long time ago," the man said. "Near closing, when the light was going. She did not want a ring fixed or a watch sized. She wanted to be gone." He turned the obsidian bead so the little fork-shaped mark caught the lamp. "So I made her a thing that would do it. The beads are ordinary. The stone is not, and neither is what I put in it."

"What did you put in it?" Addie asked.

"A protection," the man said. "The old kind. My grandmother's kind." He looked at Addie for a second like he was deciding something about her, and then he looked back at the stone. "You want the short of it. As long as this is on you, you are hard to find. A quiet thing in a loud room. Take it off, and you are a candle in a dark house, and anyone who is looking will see you burning."

Felix touched the beads at his throat. He had put the necklace back on at the coffee shop, joking, and he had not taken it off since. It felt heavier now than it had this morning.

"Who's looking?" Felix asked.

"I do not know," the man said. "She did not tell me, and I did not ask. People who come to me at closing do not want questions." He straightened up. "But I will tell you what I told her. Do not take it off. Not to shower. Not to sleep. Not because a girl at a party thinks it is ugly." He almost smiled. "You keep it on."

"I have been," Felix said. "There was a note. It said the same thing."

"Then whoever gave it to you did right by you." The man's eyes went to Felix's face and held there a moment too long. To the shape of his ears, and away again, fast, like a man who has looked at the sun. He did not say anything about it. Felix felt his neck go hot anyway.

Addie reached into the folder Felix had brought and pulled out the envelopes. "There was more," she said. "These came with it."

She spread them on the counter. Three of them, thin, the paper gone soft at the corners, and each one closed with a circle of red wax pressed with a mark none of them could read.

The man went still.

He did not pick them up. He looked at the seal the way you look at a snake you were not expecting on a path, and for a second Felix thought he was going to tell them to put the letters away and get out. His hand came up halfway and stopped.

"Where did you get these," the man said. It was not a question this time.

"Same box," Felix said. "My parents left me a box when I moved out. The necklace was in it. So were the letters. I can't read them. They're not in any language I know."

"No," the man said quietly. "They would not be." He touched the edge of one envelope with one finger, just the paper, not the wax, and drew his hand back. "I have seen that seal one other time in my life. On a paper the woman signed before she left. I did not read that one either." He looked up, and there was something in his face that had not been there when they walked in. Not curiosity. Closer to fear. "You are the boy who had the necklace, and you have her letters, and you have her look. Do you understand what I am telling you? You are hers."

"Whose?" Addie said. "You keep saying her. Her who?"

"I do not have a name to give you," the man said, "and if I did I would not say it in this room with the door unlocked." He gathered the letters into a stack, careful, and pushed them back across the felt to Felix. "Take these. Keep them with the stone. Do not show them around like you showed me."

Felix folded them into the folder. His hands were not quite steady. "You have to give us something," he said. "You can't just tell me I'm in danger and send me home."

The man was quiet for a moment. Then he wiped his hands on the rag at his belt, the way he had when they came in, and Felix understood it was a thing he did when he was thinking.

"She told me where she had been," he said. "Not where she was going. Where she had been. She said it like it still had a hold on her." He looked at Felix. "There was a place in this town for children who had no people. It is not there anymore. They tore it down and built apartments on the lot, on the east side, off the river road. If she left anything, she left it there. That is all I have, and it is more than I should have said."

Felix knew the place. He had a folder full of paper that started with the words ALEXANDRIA ORPHANAGE in bold at the top. He had been trying not to think about it since the phone call at his kitchen table, and now a stranger in a pawn shop was pointing him straight at it.

"We know it," Addie said. "It's the Riverbend complex now. My mom looked at a unit there once."

Something crossed the man's face when she said mom, and it was gone before Felix could name it.

"Then you know the way," the man said. "Go in the daytime. Do not go making a lot of noise. And you." He looked at Felix. "The necklace stays on you the whole time. Do you hear me? If you feel like taking it off, that is the exact moment you do not."

"I hear you," Felix said.

The man nodded once, like a thing was settled, and started turning off the little lamps along the counter. It was the middle of the afternoon. The shop was suddenly a place that wanted them out of it.

They stood. Addie already had a hand on the door.

"Thank you," Felix said, because he did not know what else to say to a man who had just told him his whole life was a lie built around a rock.

The man did not answer. He was looking past them, out the front window, at Main Street, at nothing Felix could see. His jaw had gone tight.

"Go home a different way than you came," the man said. "Both of you."

Addie pushed the door open and the bell over it rang, too loud. Felix followed her out into the light. The heat came down on them like a hand. Behind him he heard the lock turn, and then the second lock, and then a chain.

The little unicorn mobile in the window turned slowly on its string. There was no wind. Not a breath of it, not a car passing, not an open door. The street was still as a held breath.

But the unicorn turned, once all the way around, and then it stopped, pointing at Felix.

"Get on," Addie said. She was already on the bike. "I don't like this street."

Felix got on. He grabbed her around the waist, and this time he did not think about how she drove. He was thinking about a lot next to a river with apartments on it, and a woman he had never met who had his look, and a rock at his throat he was never supposed to take off.

He did not take it off.
