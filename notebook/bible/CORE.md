The continuity contract. Every writer on this book reads this file completely
before drafting a brief or a chapter, and reads it again before handing a chapter
in. Where this file and a later brief disagree, this file wins. Where this file
and the owner's text disagree, the owner's text wins and this file is wrong —
say so in `DECISIONS.md` and fix it here.

The reasoning behind every ruling below lives in `DECISIONS.md` (same folder),
written for the owner to read and veto. This file is the *what*; that one is the
*why*.

Absolute rules (the owner's, restated so nobody has to go looking):

1. `book-1/02` to `06` are sacred. Every sentence survives, verbatim, in order.
   New prose goes *around* them, never in place of them. Typos, spelling and
   punctuation may be fixed silently; nothing else. `07` may be extended, never
   trimmed. `00` and `01` are not touched. Section (m) below lists every
   sentence, numbered, so a chapter can be checked line by line.
2. Names are canon: Felix Knight, Addie **Walker**, Leila Chattox (the
   Mistress), Darneil Delvaux, Janet Walker, Robert and Diane Knight. Three
   people named in this bible for the first time: **Alcide Fontenot** (the
   craftsman), **Anatole Bordelon** (the leader of the ruins), **Armand
   Bordelon** (his brother).
3. The necklace never leaves Felix except where its removal is the whole point
   of a scene. The scenes where it comes off are listed in (b), and there are
   exactly five of them in Book One.
4. Only the Hilt is decided in Book One. The Blade of Tyr is *named* on the last
   page and never found.
5. Present-day Alexandria, Louisiana. The ruins are under the real city. No
   portal, no realm, no other world.
6. Voice: overworld chapters in the owner's plain contemporary close-third;
   Mistress/Darneil chapters may carry the prologue's dread; nobody anywhere
   writes chronicle. See (l).
7. Chapter 1 is a cool fall Thursday a week before Thanksgiving break. Days
   chain. See (i).
8. POV per chapter is fixed. See (i) and (n).

## (a) Canon digest

**Felix Knight**, eighteen, short, heavy, long brown hair, green eyes, pointed
ears he has never once asked about. A travel blogger, good at it; home from
Belfast three weeks before the book opens; just moved out of his parents' house
into a small second-floor apartment in the Garden District. Adopted at about
four by **Robert and Diane Knight**, two accountants in the Garden District, the
kindest and most orderly people Addie has ever met; they alphabetize the spice
rack. Felix is kind, impatient, a little rude when scared, and would rather not
know. Christian. Straight. Loves history and other people's countries.

**Addie Walker**, eighteen, psychology student finishing her first college
semester, pale, freckled, copper-haired, brown-eyed, fit, asymmetrical ears.
Rides a motorcycle like she has no regard for her own safety or his. Gardens,
birds, photography, bonsai, an orchid cactus Felix smuggled home from Belize in
a sock. Pessimistic, careful in one thing only (the garden), untidy, loyal,
bisexual, Christian. Lives at home with her mother. Has known Felix four years,
dated him three. She is the one who pushes.

**Janet Walker**, Addie's mother. Fourteen years ago a caseworker at the
Alexandria Orphanage in its last years; now a 911 dispatcher for the parish,
twelve-hour shifts, mostly nights. The protectors' contact up top. She chose the
Knights. She keeps a ward on her house (sage, butter, honey). She tracks the
Mistress's nights on a map in her closet. She put Addie and Felix in the same
room once; the rest was Addie's.

**Leila Chattox**, the Mistress. Tall, dark-skinned, long black hair that takes
the light and gives nothing back, brown eyes, red eyes when the shadow is up.
Older than she looks; her hands tell the truth. Last of a bloodline of voodoo
queens whose voodoo is lost and whose shadow and poisons remain. A master
assassin who goes up top at night to practice. Atheist. Does not play well with
others. Felix's birth mother. Darneil's wife. She has been looking for her son
for fourteen years and for the last five days she has been able to feel him.

**Darneil Delvaux**, Felix's birth father, a protector's son who went over to
the hunter, lived years with her under the city, and on a night of flood carried
their four-year-old out of her bed with her own concealment charm around his
neck and gave him to strangers. Fourteen years in the protectors' hold with the
Hilt of Tyr. Gaunt, watchful, strange, tender only about the boy.

**Alcide Fontenot**, seventy-one, the Gold and Silver Pawn Shop on Main Street.
Made the necklace for Leila twenty-odd years ago. His grandmother was a
*traiteuse*; the necklace is her craft's older, darker cousin. Afraid of Leila
ever since. A friend of the protectors, not hers.

**Anatole Bordelon**, about fifty, leader of the buried town under Alexandria —
forty-some people living in the brick vaults and cellars of the old river town
the city was built on top of. Keeps the wooden door. Keeps the truce. Feeds the
hold. Decent, tired, frightened, honest about being frightened.

**Armand Bordelon**, about forty-two, his brother. Wants the sun. Has been
running the Mistress's errands up top for a year and taking people for her for
six weeks. Her lever.

**The necklace**: hand-carved wooden beads on a waxed cord, one bead of black
obsidian cut with a mark like a little two-tined fork. A concealment charm.
Alcide made it for Leila; Darneil took it off her while she slept and put it on
their son. The cord has a knot in it that Alcide did not tie. It hid Felix for
fourteen years and it is the reason no one found him.

**The Hilt of Tyr**: grip and crossguard of an old sword, no blade. Carried to
the New World by the French. Lost. Found by the protectors of the light. Within
a stone's throw of it the shadow will not come. The first of three pieces.

**Under the city**: orphanage lot → Riverbend apartments → Building C basement →
the wooden door → the bell-stair → the buried town → (riverward) the hold, and
(downward, by her own cracks) the shrouded room.

**The book**: Felix finds adoption papers (Thu). The necklace comes out of a wax
envelope at a coffee shop table under the eyes of a waitress who started today.
Addie carries it home by accident. The waitress sends them to the pawn shop; the
craftsman tells them what it is and points them at the old orphanage lot.
Riverbend; the unlocked basement; the wooden door. The Mistress practices; the
father learns his boy has surfaced. Down. Taken. The leader and his brother. The
shrouded room and the barista's face. Bloodline. What Janet knew. The Hilt and
the father. Red eyes. The brother's gambit. The necklace off, on purpose, at a
cost. Mother. The Hilt passes to Felix. Thanksgiving: she is across the street.

## (b) THE RULES OF THE CHARM

> The charm hides the one it was set on, and it was set on Felix at four. It
> hides by **keeping**: worn against the skin it is silence — she cannot feel
> him, see him, or find him; kept for him but not worn — in his box, under his
> roof, in a friend's pocket, on a felt square within his reach — it is a
> smudge, a warmth in a dark room that tells her he is up and roughly where, but
> shows her no face and no door; **out of keeping** — off his neck and out of
> anyone's hands, or in the hands of someone who does not hold it as his — he
> is a candle in a dark house and she feels it like a hand on the back of her
> neck across the whole parish. A warded house (Janet's craft) dims a kept
> charm further; the Knights' house was warded, which is how eighteen years of
> a sealed envelope in a box passed for silence. Distance matters: a flare she
> feels anywhere; a smudge only when she is near. The charm also keeps the
> shadow *down* in him — it hides him from his own blood as well as from hers.
> Off is off: the moment it leaves him and no one holds it for him, he is seen.
> And once she has seen his face unhidden, the charm can dim him from her but
> never again hide him — a charm hides from those who have not yet seen. That
> is the cost of Chapter 21 and the last page of the book.

What this rule does to the existing text:

- **Prologue.** Cord breaks, necklace in the water — *out of keeping* — the
  flare she feels for one breath. Darneil's fist closes on it with the boy
  against his chest — *kept* — snuffed. Then worn — silence.
- **Fourteen years.** Sealed in wax, in a folder, in a box, in a warded house,
  with the boy sleeping under the same roof: kept, dim, and dimmed further.
  She never felt it.
- **Five days before Chapter 1 (Sat D−5).** Robert Knight drives the last box
  to Felix's apartment. Forty minutes in a car trunk, out of the ward, between
  roofs. Not a flare — Robert is keeping it for the boy — but the first warmth
  in fourteen years. Enough to tell her: *up, and moving, and near.* She has
  been up top every night since.
- **Chapter 3.** Felix opens the envelope at a public table and puts the
  necklace on, joking. Then, self-conscious, he takes it off and hands it
  across the table for Addie to look at (see (n), Ch03). Off his neck and in
  Addie's hand within reach: kept, but barely — the smudge sharpens to a
  *shape* for the first time. She is at the counter. She now knows which table.
- **Chapter 4 (overnight).** Addie walks off with it in her jacket pocket and
  does not know she has it until the store. Addie keeps it for him from the
  moment she realizes; even before that it is in a friend's pocket. Kept — but
  two miles from him, in a warded house. Felix, alone in an unwarded apartment
  with no curtains, is a smudge with a shape. The Mistress, after her shift,
  finds his *block*. Not his door, not his face. She stands under a row of
  windows and cannot say which is his. She could kill a shape. She does not
  want him dead. She goes to the Walkers' street and stands across from the
  sage-smelling house where the charm's own small quiet sits, and learns
  nothing more. **That is what the overnight does: it gets her closer than she
  has been in fourteen years and still not close enough to see him plainly —
  so on Friday she whispers instead of taking him.**
- **Chapter 5.** Addie returns it; he puts it on (Friday, "back on," joking
  again). Silence. She serves him twice at arm's length and cannot hold his
  face in her eye. She can see Addie perfectly.
- **Chapter 6–7.** Felix hands it to Alcide; it lies on felt under a lamp for
  the length of Alcide's first speech (kept, arm's reach). Alcide pushes it
  back and makes him put it on before he says another word (the beat inserted
  between 07 lines 22 and 24). She is across Main Street. The unicorn turns.
- **Chapter 21.** Felix takes it off himself, outside the Hilt's quiet, to
  reach the shadow. She sees him. It goes back on in Chapter 22, in front of
  her, as his answer. It never hides him from her again.

**The five removals in Book One** (the only ones permitted): Ch03 (handed to
Addie at the table), Ch06/07 (handed to Alcide), Ch18 (Darneil asks to see the
knot; it does not leave Felix's hand), Ch21 (off, on purpose), Ch22 (back on, in
front of her). Anything else is a defect.

## (c) The shadow and her tools — what each looks like, and its limits

**The shadow.** Comes up off the floor to meet her. Slow. Patient the way water
is patient. Pools at the wrists, lies along the forearms like a cat that has
decided she will do. It comes for the ones who can take it or leave it and it
smells need on a person the way a dog does. On the page: never a "bolt" or a
"blast." It *rises*, *pools*, *lies*, *closes over*. It thins in real light —
sun, headlights, a bare bulb — and is nearly nothing at noon on Main Street,
which is why she stands in doorways by day. **Limits:** dark or dim only for
anything but the not-being-seen; touch range for the taking; one person at a
time; it will not go where the Hilt is; it does not work on the ones who feel
weather (below); it costs her — the ache — and it will not come to a person who
*needs* it, which is the whole trouble it has with Felix in Chapter 21.

**The not-being-seen.** Her first art. The eye slides off her. In a crowd she is
the person you did not notice. Up top by day she is a tall woman nobody
remembers. It is not invisibility; a camera would show her; Addie *sees* her
across the street in Chapter 2 (and then does not — "in that moment, they were
gone"). It fails against anyone who is looking *for* her and knows what they are
looking for: the protectors survive because they know.

**The shadow's lie.** A small gray suggestion laid over a person a half-second
before she reaches them: everything is fine, the street is empty, the cold at
the back of the neck is only the night. It is why nobody turns. **Limit:** it
does not work on the ones who feel weather — the buried town's blood, up top or
down. On them it inverts: instead of calm they get sick. Addie's gut-wrench in
Ch2, her stomach in knots in Ch3, are the lie failing on her. Janet feels it as
cold. It also weakens on anyone who already *knows* she is coming — which is
the only reason the fight in Chapter 21 is a fight.

**The taking of the light.** Her hand on a shoulder, gentle; eye contact; she
lets them see the red; the shadow closes over them like still water over a
dropped stone. They sit down as if to rest. No wound. The coroner writes
*consistent with cardiac event*. The paper runs it small. **Limits:** touch;
eyes; one at a time; she must *see* the person — she cannot take what the charm
hides, which is why she never simply kills Felix; and every taking costs her a
little of the ache's quiet and gives back a little more.

**Shadow-speed.** A slight boost, not a power. In the dark she covers ground
faster than a fit person should — the two miles from the Walkers' street to the
alley behind Third Street in ten minutes, by culvert and drain and grate, while
Addie's bike does it in seven. She cannot outrun a car. She cannot outrun the
bike. She does not need to; she knows where the bike is going.

**Red eyes.** The whites go; a red so deep it is nearly black, the red at the
center of a coal deciding whether to die; lit from somewhere the lamp cannot
answer for. It is the tell. She cannot use the shadow without it and she cannot
hide it while the shadow is up. Felix's go the same red in Chapter 21 and cool
back to green with a rim that stays.

**The paralytic.** The stoppered vial the color of tea. On the folding knife
with the bone handle worn smooth by a hundred years of thumbs; on her arrows in
the ruins. Slows and sedates. A scratch: minutes of slowing, the limbs heavy,
the tongue thick. A cut: hours down and awake. The *holding* — her newest brew,
perfected on the kept ones over the last year — days, and something does not
come back. **Limits:** the skin must be broken; it needs fresh brewing; there is
no antidote but time. It is what she uses on the ones she wants to suffer, or to
keep.

**The belt of forgotten poisons.** Leather, a dozen stoppered vials, each a brew
whose name is gone with the voodoo; she has never named them and the book never
lists more than three at once. Tea (the paralytic). Milk (the sleeper). Black
(the mercy). Everything else is color and silence.

**Dagger and arrows.** The folding knife, up top. A short bow, black-fletched,
in the long dark galleries below; she does not carry the bow up top. She is a
knife-and-hands killer in a city and an archer in a tunnel.

**Felix's shadow.** The same. Latent under the charm for eighteen years. In
Chapter 16, under her floor and angry, it strains against the charm: the candle
in Anatole's hall bends toward him, cold pools at his wrist, and he understands
it is *his*. In Chapter 21, with the charm off, it comes — and because he needs
it, it comes badly, late, and takes payment. What he can do with it in Book One:
the not-being-seen laid over Addie (he hides her from his mother); and one shove
— shadow on shadow, hers and his, which are the same shadow, so that hers
*hesitates*. That hesitation is the whole use. **Costs (permanent, Book Two
carries them):** she has seen him; his hands stay cold; a rim of red at the
edge of the green in low light.

**The ones who feel weather.** Janet's line — the buried town's blood that went
up top three generations ago. Not magic. A nausea near the shadow, a cold spot,
a wrongness. The town's people below have it too; the ones who did not are the
ones she took, a hundred and fifty years ago, until the truce.

## (d) The Hilt of Tyr — lightly

Grip, crossguard, and the stub of a tang. Iron gone black, wrapped in silver
wire worn to the color of pewter. No blade. It fits a hand exactly and it is
heavier than it should be. It sits on the altar stone of the old chapel's crypt
under the buried town.

What it does: **within a stone's throw of it the shadow will not come.** Not
cannot — will not; the shadow leaves a room the Hilt is in the way a dog leaves
a room. A person of the bloodline who holds it feels the shadow in them go
still; held long enough it *binds* — the shadow sleeps in them and does not
wake. That is what it is for. The protectors say Tyr gave his hand to bind the
wolf, once, in dialogue, in a crypt, as a thing they say; it is never narrated.
Whole, the sword would bind the line for good. In three pieces it holds one
room.

Limits: it does nothing to ordinary people. It is not a weapon; it has no edge.
It must be *held* to bind; on a stone it only quiets. Its quiet has an edge —
the iron chancel rail of the crypt — and Armand's men, being ordinary, walk
through it. It cannot be carried by the bloodline without cost: the Mistress
cannot touch it without her shadow dying in her, which would leave her an
ordinary woman with old hands, and that is the one thing she fears. Felix can
hold it. He is of the line; it goes still in him; that is why custody can pass
to him and why, in Book Two, it can also be turned on him.

## (e) The ruins

**The buried town.** Alexandria grew up around a French post at the rapids of
the Red River. The first town stood on the low ground by the water: brick
cellars, cypress beams, a street, a market square, a chapel, a warehouse vault,
a convent. The great floods silted it in; the fire of 1864 finished the top;
the city rebuilt on the higher ground and on the levee and, in time, right over
the old town's roofs. The cellars connected. Some families did not leave. Over
a hundred and fifty years they became a people: forty-odd souls, the Bordelons
and the Lemoines and the Dauzats, lamps and cisterns and mushrooms and stolen
current, a school of six children, a hall in the warehouse vault, a smell of
sage at every threshold. They are not monsters and not a court. They are small
and frightened and they have kept a door for a very long time. In prose they are
"the town," "the people down here," "Anatole's people." Nobody says *ruins*
except Felix, once, and Anatole corrects him: "We live here."

**Levels.** Reading from the surface down:
1. The orphanage lot → Riverbend, Building C, on the old convent's foundation.
2. The Building C basement: the old convent cellar, concrete over brick. The
   wooden door in the far wall.
3. The bell-stair: ninety-four steps, brick then stone, the shaft of the
   convent's old bell tower, going down instead of up. Sage at the bottom.
4. The buried street ("the old street"): the town. The hall (warehouse vault),
   the cisterns, the sleeping rooms, the school.
5. Riverward, the river way: four hundred yards of brick sewer, knee-wet in
   rain, to the old chapel. The chancel, sunk; the crypt below it: **the
   hold.** An iron rail marks the quiet's edge.
6. Downward, off the far end of the old street and off the market square, her
   fissures — cracks that were cracks before anyone built over them. Below the
   water table, below the old brick sewers: **the shrouded room.**

**The wooden door.** Cypress, old, oiled from the inside, the wrong wood for a
concrete basement. Kept by the Bordelons for a hundred and fifty years. It is
*unlocked* because it locks from below and the town's people go up through it
at night for what they need; the "MAINTENANCE" door at the top is their
convenience, not the developer's. Janet has a right to it. It is the town's
door. **She never uses it.** She does not care to be seen using anything they
use.

**Her older ways up.** Three the book names: the grate in the alley behind
Third Street (behind Ugly Mug); the storm drain at the low place on the river
road below Riverbend — the same grate the necklace hung from in the prologue;
the brick culvert under the railroad crossing two blocks from the Walkers'
house. She goes up through the dark of them without a light, hand on wet stone.

**The truce.** Made by Anatole's grandmother with Leila's mother. Five terms,
said aloud in Chapter 14 by Anatole and never written:
1. She does not hunt the town.
2. The town does not use her ways and does not speak of her up top.
3. Whoever comes down the door uninvited is *presented* to her. She takes some
   and lets some go. (The town's terrible price; the source of the old missing.)
4. She does not cross the chapel rail. (She cannot. The town does not know
   that. They think it is the truce.)
5. The town feeds the hold. (Sworn to Darneil's father.)

**The shrouded room.** The Quiet Room of the notebook piece, exactly: below
everything, cloth hung on the walls so old the color has left it, gray on gray,
one fat candle in a dish of black glass, a low table, the tea-colored vial, the
bone-handled knife, a length of cord, a small brass box she does not open. In
the brass box: a lock of dark baby hair, and a scrap with a name on it. Off the
room, a second chamber the notebook did not need and Chapter 19 does: where the
kept ones lie.

**The protectors' hold.** The crypt of the old chapel. Vaulted brick, a stone
altar with the Hilt on it, a cot, a lamp, a shelf of books gone soft, fourteen
years of a man's handwriting on the walls in charcoal — dates, and a boy's
height marked against the stone at every age Darneil could only guess at. The
iron chancel rail across the entry from the river way. Beyond the rail, the
shadow does not come.

## (f) The protectors network, and how word travels

The protectors of the light are a line by *oath*, not blood — which is why the
Hilt can pass to Felix. The oath, said once in Chapter 23, three plain
sentences: *I keep the light. I keep it from her. I keep it until it's whole.*

Who is left:
- **Darneil**, in the hold, sworn fourteen years. Cannot leave the quiet
  without being found. Has not seen the sky since the prologue.
- **Alcide Fontenot**, up top, the maker. Not sworn; a friend of the sworn.
  His shop's back door is where Janet knocks.
- **Janet Walker**, up top, the placer and the watcher. Not sworn; the town's
  blood gone up; the protectors' contact. Chose the Knights. Keeps the ward.
- **Anatole Bordelon**, below, not a protector — the truce-keeper. Feeds the
  hold (Odile carries it). Carries word to Darneil.
- **Robert and Diane Knight**, unknowing. Told three things.
- Dead: Étienne Delvaux, Darneil's father, the protector before him, who died
  in the hold of damp and age the year before the prologue.

How word travels:
- Up top: phones, but never about *this* on a phone. Janet knocks at Alcide's
  back door after closing. Alcide calls Janet's cell and says only "Come by."
- Between up top and below: Janet goes down the wooden door. The watchers at
  the foot of the bell-stair know her. She has a standing right. She used it
  on the Friday night of Chapter 7 (offstage; told in Ch17) and again on the
  Monday of Chapter 17 (onstage).
- Below: runners. Ti-Jean Lemoine, eleven, runs between the hall and the rail
  of the hold. Nobody goes to *her*; she sends for whom she wants, and for a
  year the one she has sent for is Armand.
- To Darneil: only Anatole and Odile cross the rail. Word of the boy reaches
  him on Sunday morning (Ch11) by Anatole's mouth: Janet's Friday-night message
  ("the boy has the box; he has been to Alcide; he is coming to the lot") plus
  Saturday's news ("the door was opened a hand's width yesterday; two of them;
  the watchers heard a girl say *tomorrow*").
- To the Mistress: Armand. And her own senses — she felt the door open Saturday
  and the warmth pass over her head.

## (g) Geography in words

**Alexandria** sits on the west bank of the Red River, Pineville across the
water. Downtown is a grid of a dozen blocks against the levee — Third Street,
Jackson Street, Main Street in this book's map — old brick, a few restaurants,
the Ugly Mug. The Garden District lies just southwest of downtown: live oaks,
big old houses, some cut into apartments. MacArthur Drive rings it all. The
river road runs the levee on the east side, out past the last of downtown, where
the ground dips; the Riverbend complex is there, on the old orphanage lot, off
the river road, the levee at its back.

**Ugly Mug Coffee.** Downtown on Third Street, two blocks from the levee. Big
front windows, a counter with a pastry case, a dozen small tables, latte art,
a back door to the alley. The alley has a grate. Felix works here most mornings
with a laptop. It is Addie's favorite place to come. After Chapter 5 she does
not come back.

**Gold and Silver Pawn Shop.** Main Street, four blocks from Ugly Mug. A
vertical sign on the building. One front window with a little unicorn mobile
hanging in it. Inside: trinkets, books, clothes, a glass counter with squares
of felt and small gooseneck lamps, a curtain to the back workroom, a back door
to the alley. Three locks and a chain. Alcide lives upstairs.

**The Walkers' house.** Two miles from Felix's apartment, southwest off Jackson
Street Extension. Two storeys (Addie's room upstairs, lockable), a kitchen with
a black window over the sink, a back garden with a potting bench, a clip light,
feeders, a birdbath, a crepe myrtle, a trident maple bonsai, an orchid cactus
that spends the whole book half out of its pot until Janet repots it in Ch17.
Houses across the street. A railroad crossing two blocks down; under it, a
brick culvert.

**Felix's apartment.** Garden District, a second-floor apartment cut out of an
old house on a side street off Jackson, streetlight orange through a window with
no curtains. A kitchen table with a folder on it. The Knights' house is four
blocks away; Felix walked to Ugly Mug from here on Thursday and Friday.

**The Knights' house.** Garden District. The lit doorway of the prologue. The
alphabetized spice rack. A dining table that seats six with the leaf in.

**Riverbend.** Three brick-faced three-storey buildings on the old lot, A, B, C,
a leasing office in A with a brass plaque about the orphanage, a landscaped
strip, a parking lot, the levee behind. Building C sits on the old convent's
foundation. Under its stair, a door marked MAINTENANCE. It opens on concrete
steps down into the old cellar.

**Below.** See (e). Distances that matter: the bell-stair is ninety-four steps;
the old street is about two hundred yards end to end; the river way is four
hundred yards from the hall to the rail; her fissures open off the far end of
the old street, past the market square, and nobody in the town has ever gone
down one and come back to say how far.

## (h) Character sheets

Format: name — look — want — fear — how they talk — what they know (at the
start of the book; the tables in (i)/(j) track what changes).

**Felix Knight.** 18. Short, heavy, long brown hair, green eyes, pointed ears
under the hair. Wears a hoodie and the same jeans. *Want:* to make the most of
his life, to help people, to see the world before fifty — and, underneath, to be
left alone by the truth. *Fear:* that the truth about him is ugly; that Addie
will look at his ears and finally say something. *Talk:* plain, quick, a little
rude when scared ("Addie, do we even have an orphanage in Alexandria?"); jokes
at the wrong moment ("How does it look?"); says "I hear you" when he does not.
*Knows at start:* he was adopted (papers, Thursday); Robert and Diane are good
people; nothing about the necklace, the ears, his blood, or the woman at the
counter. *Arc:* from the boy who would rather not know to the one who takes it
off on purpose.

**Addie Walker.** 18. Copper hair coming loose, freckles, pale, brown eyes,
asymmetrical ears (the left sits a little lower; she got it from Janet's
mother). Peak shape; rides a mid-size street bike she kick-starts. Potting soil
on her jeans. *Want:* to know; to keep Felix; the trails. *Fear:* that her
mother has lied to her; that she is not enough to hold on to him. *Talk:*

**Armand Bordelon.** About 42. Anatole's face with the fat gone out of it; a

## (l) Voice rules, the banned register, and three exemplars

**Rules.**
1. Overworld chapters (Felix / Addie / Janet POV): the owner's voice. Short
   declarative sentences. Contemporary and plain. Close third, one focal
   person, that person's senses and fears filtering everything. Dialogue does
   the work; the narration gets out of its way. Atmosphere comes from what is
   in the room — a flour-coated apron, a unicorn mobile, three locks and a
   chain — never from narration telling us to be afraid. A little breathless:
   the sentences run when the character runs.
2. Mistress and Darneil chapters (10, 11, 19 first half, 20, 21 second half,
   22 second half) may carry the prologue's and the Quiet Room's dread
   register: longer sentences, the shadow described slowly, the ache. Still
   close third, still one person, still no narrator standing outside the story
   telling us what it means.
3. Nobody, anywhere, writes chronicle. The narrator never knows what the
   histories said, never looks down from a height, never addresses the reader.
4. Nothing is explained that a character would not say out loud. The rules in
   (b), (c), (d) reach the page as dialogue, as things people do, or as the
   focal character's private inference. Not as narration.
5. Length comes from staying *in* a scene — real time, sensory detail, talk
   that does work — never from summary of history or from telling us what a
   character has already shown us.
6. The owner's dialogue tags and rhythms stand as written. Around them, the
   new dialogue matches them: people say "I hear you" and "Get on" and "Put
   this on." Nobody says "indeed."
7. Tense: the new prose is past tense. The owner's sentences stand in whatever
   tense he wrote them (see `DECISIONS.md`, N).

**Banned.** These do not appear anywhere in Book One:
- "the histories, when they were finally written" and every cousin of it: "it
  is said," "in those days," "the old ones knew," "little did they know,"
