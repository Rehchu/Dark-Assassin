# DECISIONS — Book One: The Hilt

Written for the owner. Every ruling the showrunner had to make to turn the
outline into a bible that four writers can draft against in parallel. Each
entry: **the decision**, **the reasoning**, **what depends on it**. Veto any of
them; the bible (`BIBLE.md`, same folder) is built so that a veto is a
find-and-replace, not a rebuild — the dependencies are listed for exactly that
reason.

Nothing here touches your sentences. Items A–M are the questions the brief
asked; N onward are the ones the text forced.

---

## A. The charm's rules

**Decision.** The charm hides by *keeping*, and hides best when *worn*. Three
states: **worn** (silence — she cannot feel, see or find him); **kept** (in his
possession or held for him by someone who holds it as his: dim — a warmth that
tells her *up, near, roughly where*, but no face and no door); **out of
keeping** (off his neck and in nobody's hands, or in the hands of someone who
does not hold it for him: a candle in a dark house, felt across the parish). A
warded house dims a kept charm further. Once she has seen his face unhidden, the
charm dims him from her but never hides him from her again. The quotable
paragraph is in `BIBLE.md` (b).

**How this reconciles the text.** Prologue: cord breaks → out of keeping →
the flare; Darneil's fist closes → kept → snuffed; then worn. Fourteen years:
sealed in a warded house with the boy under the roof → kept and dimmed →
silence to her. Your Ch3: he finds it *sealed* and has "never seen anything
like this before" — true, because the Knights were told to keep it *with* him,
not on him, and he was four when it was last on his neck. Ch7's "put the
necklace back on at the coffee shop, joking" is *Friday* (your Ch5's return),
so "this morning" is true. Ch4/5's overnight in Addie's room: kept, two miles
off, in a warded house → he is a smudge with a shape → she finds his *block*
and cannot see a door or a face, and so on Friday she whispers instead of
taking him. That is the whole reason the whisper exists.

**The flicker.** The "one breath" the Quiet Room waits for is not one moment;
it is a week of them, each a little brighter: the box leaving the Knights'
ward in Robert's trunk five days before Ch1 (the first warmth in fourteen
years — this is why she is already across Addie's street in Ch2 and already
hired at Ugly Mug in Ch3); the envelope opened at a public table and the
necklace handed to Addie (the smudge sharpens to a shape — she knows which
table); the overnight (she finds his block); the pawn shop counter (arm's
reach). Each one is a removal that is *the point of its scene*, which keeps
Rule 3 honest.

**Also decided here:** the charm keeps the shadow *down* in him too. It hides
him from his own blood as well as from hers. That gives the note's rule a
second edge and makes Chapter 21's removal do two things at once.

**Depends on it:** Ch2's figure, Ch3's waitress, Ch3's handoff (W), Ch4's
empty block, the whisper (B), Ch10, Ch18's full rule, Ch21's cost, Ch24's
last line.

## B. The whisper

**Decision.** The exact words, whispered at Felix's ear in your Ch5, between
"I might know someone who can help you" and "Gold And Silver Pawn shop":

> "He'll tell you never to take it off. He's wrong, baby. Take it off some
> night, out under the sky, and find out what those ears are for. I'll hear
> you."

Felix's reply is yours: "I'll have to check it out."

**Reasoning.** It has to be short — Addie only turns away to sip. It has to
be a thing a hunter-mother says to a son she cannot see clearly: not *I am your
mother* (she does not know his face; she would never show her hand), but an
invitation tied to the one act that lets her see him. "He'll tell you" makes
"I'll have to check it out" a natural answer — she has just hinted at the
pawn-shop man — and it makes your Ch7 land twice: when Alcide says "You keep
it on," Felix hears the whisper's prophecy come true and has already been told
the man is wrong. "Those ears" is the private wound: nobody has ever said the
word *ears* to him, Addie least of all, and that is exactly why he cannot repeat
it to her — to repeat it he would have to say the word. So he keeps it, and the
keeping is his arc's first wrong turn. "I'll hear you" pays in Ch15 ("I said
I'd hear you" — he knows the voice) and Ch22 ("I told you I'd hear you").

**The reader does not hear it in Ch5.** Ch5 is Addie's POV (your original is;
see M), so the whisper is withheld from the reader as well as from Addie, and
the words arrive whole in Ch16, in Felix's head, when Anatole says the word
*ears*. Four writers need the words fixed now; that is why they are here.

**Depends on it:** Ch5 lean (M), Ch7 line 28 landing doubled, Ch15, Ch16, Ch21
(the invitation accepted on his own terms, not hers), Ch22.

## C. Janet Walker

**Decision.** Janet is the protectors' contact up top. Fourteen years ago she
was a caseworker at the Alexandria Orphanage in its last years; the protectors
used her to place the boy with the Knights through papers that look real —
which is why the papers say ALEXANDRIA ORPHANAGE and why the boy was never in
the building more than a night. She chose the Knights. She has watched Felix
since, partly through Addie: she put them in the same church-youth van once,
freshman year; the rest was Addie's. The Walkers are the buried town's blood
gone up three generations ago — Janet's grandmother was born on the old
street — which is why Janet has a standing right to the wooden door, why she
wards her house the way the town wards its thresholds (sage, butter, honey:
sage burned at the sills, a saucer of butter and honey set out — a house that
smells like a kitchen, not like a boy), and why Addie feels sick near the
shadow (see V). Janet is now a 911 dispatcher for the parish, twelve-hour
shifts, mostly nights: that is how "a string of missing persons cases lately"
comes out of her mouth as a fact, and how she has kept a map for fourteen
years with two columns, DEAD and MISSING (see L). Alcide flinches at *mom* in
Ch7 because Jan was at his back door Thursday night.

**Reasoning.** Your Ch2 gives her three tells in one paragraph — the errand,
the cash, the warning — and Ch1 (already written) has Addie push away the
thought that her mother once mentioned the orphanage. Ch7 has "my mom looked
at a unit there once" and the flinch. All of that wants a mother who knows.
Making her the *placer* rather than a bystander is what sharpens Addie's stake
in Ch17: she has to ask whether her love was ever her own, and the answer —
"The rest was yours. All of it." — is the book's second-best line. The
buried-town blood is what ties "how the Walkers are tied in" (outline, Ch17) to
the ground under the city instead of to a coincidence.

**Depends on it:** Ch2, Ch4 (the scrubbed kitchen, the empty drive), Ch7's
flinch, Ch8 ("don't go in the basement"; Diane's "a woman from the
orphanage"), Ch9 (sage at the door), Ch13 ("Miss Jan's girl"), Ch17, Ch23 (she
wards the apartment), Ch24.

## D. The Knights

**Decision.** Robert and Diane Knight are ordinary, kind, and told three things
by the man who handed the boy through the lit doorway: *keep it with him; never
tell him until he leaves home; when he leaves, give him the box.* They knew the
orphanage woman as "Mrs. Walker" and were asked not to keep her name; they
kept the request instead. One short scene: Felix calls Diane from the Riverbend
parking lot in Ch8 (Act II). She cries and apologizes for crying. She says the
three things. She says a man brought him in the rain and a woman from the
orphanage did the papers and "your father thinks she goes to our church." She
does not say Walker. Ch24 seats them at the Thanksgiving table and forgives
them plainly.

**Reasoning.** Ch1 has already made them "the kindest and most orderly people
Addie has ever met." A book that turns them into liars would be a different
book. The half-clue about church lets an attentive reader guess Janet nine
chapters early without Felix guessing — he is reeling — which is the fair kind
of plant.

**Depends on it:** Ch8, Ch17 (Janet confirms "Mrs. Walker"), Ch24.

## E. The craftsman

**Decision.** **Alcide Fontenot**, seventy-one, of the Gold and Silver Pawn
Shop, Main Street. Fontenot is Evangeline-Parish French, ordinary in Central
Louisiana; Alcide is an old Louisiana French given name. His grandmother was a
*traiteuse* (a Cajun folk healer); "the old kind of protection, my
grandmother's kind" is her craft's older, darker cousin, which he learned and
does not use. He made the necklace for Leila twenty-odd years ago, near
closing, and has been afraid of her since. She came to him once after the boy
was taken to ask who could have lifted it off her; he said no one; she let him
live because she may need him to make another and because he did not lie. He
is a friend of the protectors — Darneil came to him after; Janet knocks at his
back door — and not hers. He notices, in your Ch6, that the string has a knot
he did not tie. He comes down the stair in Ch21 with a flashlight and a crowbar
and a bad heart, drives the truck in Ch23, and says "Jan" once.

**Reasoning.** The prologue already says "an old man in a pawn shop on Main
Street" and "his grandmother's kind." The traiteur tradition is real, local,
and lets "the old kind" stay grounded in Louisiana instead of drifting toward
generic magic. Making him afraid rather than compromised keeps your Ch6 anger
("I'll ask again") true — it is fear.

**Depends on it:** Ch6, Ch7, Ch17, Ch18 (the knot), Ch21, Ch23, Ch24.

## F. The ruins

**Decision.** The buried town is the first Alexandria: the low-ground
settlement that grew up around the French post at the rapids — brick cellars,
cypress beams, a street, a market square, a chapel, a warehouse vault, a
convent. The great floods silted it in; the fire of 1864 took what stood; the
city rebuilt on the higher ground and, in time, over the old roofs. The cellars
connected. A few families did not leave. A hundred and fifty years later they
are forty-some people, a school of six, lamps and cisterns and stolen current —
small, frightened, and alive because of a truce. In prose they are "the town"
and they say "We live here."

**The leader:** **Anatole Bordelon**, about fifty. **His brother:** **Armand
Bordelon**, about forty-two. Bordelon is Avoyelles/Rapides French — you will
find it on mailboxes twenty miles from the coffee shop. Anatole keeps the
wooden door (it locks from *below*; the town goes up through it at night for
what it needs; that is why "a basement that wasn't locked"), keeps the truce,
feeds the hold. Armand wants the sun; he has run her errands up top for a year
and taken people for her for six weeks; he is her lever in Ch20.

**The truce** (five terms, said aloud in Ch14 like a lease): she does not
hunt the town; the town does not use her ways and does not speak of her up
top; whoever comes down the door uninvited is *presented* to her (some taken,
some let go — the old missing); she does not cross the chapel rail (she cannot;
the town thinks it is the truce); the town feeds the hold. The Mistress never
uses the door; her ways up are older cracks (the alley grate behind Ugly Mug;
the river-road drain — the prologue's grate; the culvert under the rail
crossing near the Walkers').

**Reasoning.** "As if the city was just built right on top" (storyline) and
"the buried streets of a town that had been standing here when Alexandria was
still a word" (Quiet Room) want a real buried town, not a fantasy kingdom. The
Poste des Rapides gives it a French root and a chapel, and the chapel gives the
Hilt a place to have been lost and found. The truce turns "a leader, his
brother, and the Mistress" from a cast list into an order that can break.

**Depends on it:** Ch9 (sage at the door), Ch12–16, Ch19–21, Ch23; the
protectors' network (G); the missing (L).

## G. Darneil

**Decision.** Darneil hides in the protectors' hold: the crypt of the old
town's chapel, four hundred yards riverward from the hall along a brick sewer,
behind an iron chancel rail. The Hilt sits on the altar stone. **He has hidden
fourteen years within a few hundred yards of her because the shadow will not go
where the Hilt is** — the hold is its own quiet, the way the necklace is
Felix's, and it is also a prison: past the rail he is found. He has not seen the
sky since the prologue. Gaunt, watchful, strange, tender only about the boy. The
wall of the crypt carries fourteen years of charcoal dates and a boy's height
guessed at every age. Her signet ring is on a cord at his throat. He is a
protector's son (Étienne Delvaux, the keeper before him, died in the hold the
year before the prologue) who went over to the hunter, lived with her under the
city, and ran when the boy was born and the Hilt stood unguarded — two reasons,
one night. He is sworn; the protectors are a line by oath, not blood, which is
what lets custody pass to Felix in Ch23.

**How word reaches him for Ch11.** By Anatole's mouth, Sunday morning: Janet's
Friday-night message through the door ("the boy has the box; he has been to
Alcide; he is coming to the lot; tell Delvaux; do not present him") and
Saturday's news from the watchers ("the door opened a hand's width; a girl said
*tomorrow*"). Only Anatole and Odile cross the rail. In parallel, Armand tells
the Mistress that the Walker woman uses the door — so the same news travels
down both channels and the book has two clocks running.

**Reasoning.** The notebook says "hiding for 14 years" and the Quiet Room puts
her a floor below the town. A father hidden *that* close needs a reason she
has never simply walked in and taken him, and the Hilt's quiet is that reason —
it also fixes, early and in action, the one rule the climax turns on (the rail).
Janet as the messenger keeps her a protector and not a bystander.

**Depends on it:** Ch11, Ch14 ("your father is here"), Ch18, Ch19 (the rail is
the whole standoff), Ch20 (Armand's men are ordinary and can cross), Ch21,
Ch23.

## H. What the Mistress wants

**Decision.** Her son — the bloodline's last. The necklace is the obstacle: she
must have it *off* him to see him plainly and bind him. The Hilt is the one
thing that can bind her line (the protectors say Tyr gave his hand to bind the
wolf — once, in dialogue, in Ch18, never narrated), so Felix is also her way to
draw Darneil and the Hilt out from behind the rail: she has watched the father
stand at the rail for a night and knows he will cross it the moment the boy is
in danger. She gets both wants half-met: she sees her son (Ch21) and the Hilt
leaves the hold for a Garden District apartment where an ordinary man could
carry it away (Book Two). Underneath both, the thing she cannot say: the
not-being-seen filled, once, all the way — a person who looks at her and does
not slide off.

**Reasoning.** Your storyline says she wants to overthrow the leader and take
the overworld. Kept, and grounded: with the town hers she walks the old street;
with the door hers she comes and goes by daylight through a leasing office. But
a mother hunting a son is a better engine than a conqueror, and it is the one
the prologue and the Quiet Room already built.

**Depends on it:** Ch10, Ch15 (why she lets them go), Ch19 (the bargain),
Ch20, Ch21 (why Darneil takes the arrow), Ch22, Ch24.

## I. Felix's ability

**Decision.** The same shadow, latent, kept down by the charm. It stirs in
Ch16 with the necklace on — under her floor and angry, the candle bends toward
him, cold pools at his wrist — enough to horrify him. It is used for real in
Ch21 with the necklace off, on purpose, by his own hand, outside the rail: it
comes late and badly because he *needs* it and the shadow smells need. What he
can do with it: lay the not-being-seen over Addie so she walks out of Armand's
hands unseen; and one shove — shadow on shadow, his and hers, which are the
same, so hers *hesitates*. The costs, all permanent and all carried into Book
Two: she has seen his face and the charm will never again hide him from her;
his hands stay cold; a rim of red at the edge of the green in low light. His
eyes go the same red as hers.

**Reasoning.** "Utilize his newfound abilities to help stop the bad guy"
(storyline) and the series note's rule that the necklace coming off must be
"the graduation made physical." The price must be one the reader has known a
whole book: the pawn shop says a candle in a dark house; the cost is that the
candle has been seen.

**Depends on it:** Ch16, Ch21, Ch22 (he puts it back on in front of her),
Ch24 (Addie feels his cold hands at the table; the last line).

## J. The Hilt decided (Ch23)

**Decision.** Darneil survives, badly hurt: her holding poison, taken in the
right shoulder past the rail, leaves his right arm and leg dead and his speech
thick — permanent. He cannot hold the Hilt. There is one other hand of the
line in the room. Custody passes to Felix with the oath (three plain
sentences: *I keep the light. I keep it from her. I keep it until it's whole.*)
The Mistress withdraws unbeaten, with Armand, having seen her son. Darneil is
carried up the stair into the first daylight in fourteen years and admitted to
Rapides Regional as a stroke under his own name. The Hilt goes into the
Knights' box under Felix's bed in a warded apartment; its quiet reaches the
street.

**Reasoning.** The series note says the mentor must be removed before the last
book, on purpose. Half-removing him here — alive, present, unable — keeps the
father-son thread for Books Two and Three and makes Felix the one who stands
between his mother and the sword *now*. A dead right hand echoes Tyr lightly
without a word of narration.

**Depends on it:** Ch21, Ch22, Ch23, Ch24, the Book Two opening.

## K. The last page, and the trilogy

**Decision — the second piece.** **The Blade of Tyr**, rumoured at
**Natchitoches** — the oldest French town in Louisiana, fifty miles north on
the Cane River — "under the old fort," Darneil says in Ch24, thick-tongued,
and that is all he knows. Named on the last page; never found in Book One.
The third piece is the **Pommel** (the peace-stone that caps and balances the
sword), rumoured in New Orleans, where the bloodline came up out of the water.

**The last page.** Thanksgiving at the Knights' table. Felix's hands are cold
when he passes the dish. The window. A tall woman in a gray coat across the
street at the exact edge of the quiet, in daylight, not hiding. He looks back —
the thing the prologue says you never do — and does not take it off. The last
line is hers: she knows where he is.

**Trilogy-ending sketch (for `PLAN.md`; a paragraph, to argue with).** Book
Two, *The Blade*: the Mistress up top with Armand as her seen face; the Hilt
carried off from the apartment by ordinary hands; Felix and Addie to
Natchitoches, where the protectors who kept the Blade are down to one old
woman and the piece is in the river; the second letter opened; Darneil, in a
chair, teaching Felix to let the shadow instead of needing it; the book closes
with two pieces in the Mistress's reach and one in Felix's, and Addie hurt in
a way that is not undone. Book Three, *The Sword*: New Orleans, the
bloodline's ground, the Pommel; the third letter; Darneil's full removal (he
dies past a rail again, for the same reason); the sword made whole in Felix's
two cold hands — and the ending, decided now so the books are written toward
it: **he binds the line, and the line is himself.** The Hilt binds what holds
it. The sword whole binds the shadow in every hand of the line, and Felix, who
has spent three books learning to let it, lets it go: he binds his mother and
himself in one act, and becomes ordinary — the thing she feared most, chosen.
She, ordinary at last, is looked at by her son and does not slide off his eye.
The not-being-seen is filled. Addie's last line closes the trilogy where Ch1
began: something about paper not changing who you are, said back to him.

**Reasoning.** "Get the next piece" is a ladder; each piece must close a human
question (series note §3). Book One: *who is Felix*. Book Two: *can he let it
without becoming her* (the Blade is where that comes due). Book Three: *what
does he owe the line* — and the sword's answer is the one the whole first book
set up: it binds what holds it.

**Depends on it:** Ch24; `PLAN.md` (the owner's to update).

## L. Missing persons vs. "random" deaths

**Decision.** Two things, and Janet's map has two columns. **DEAD** — the
randoms: her practice, one every few months for fourteen years, forty-one on
the map, "consistent with cardiac event," found against walls, no wound. All
hers. **MISSING** — new, four in six weeks: the *kept ones*, taken down by
Armand for her holding-brew (the paralytic she has been perfecting for the day
she needs her son held, not dead). They lie in a second chamber off the
shrouded room. Older than both: the truce's third term, by which the lost who
came down the door were presented and some were taken — the parish's slow
background of vanishings, which is why Janet's grandmother's people left.

**Pays off.** Ch10: she practices (one dead on the river road, found Sunday
morning; Janet takes the call and writes the time on her wrist) and passes the
second chamber. Ch17: Janet's map, the two columns, and the one thing she does
not know — why the missing are new. Ch19: the kept ones carried to the rail on
stretchers, held, eyes open; one taken in front of Felix to show him the
taking; the bargain. Ch22: three given back as "a mother's gift." Ch23: three
left on a bench on the river road; the six o'clock news says "found alive,
confused"; three fewer names on the map.

**Reasoning.** Your Ch2 says *missing*; the Quiet Room says *bodies*. Both
are true if the deaths are hers and the disappearances are her brother-in-arms'
recent work, and the difference is exactly what tells Janet — and the reader —
that something under the city has changed.

**Depends on it:** Ch2, Ch10, Ch14 (third term), Ch17, Ch19, Ch22, Ch23.

## N. Tense and seams in the owner's sentences

**Decision (default; please confirm or veto).** Your sentences stand *exactly*
as written, including tense. The new prose around them is past tense. Where
one of your sentences is present tense ("Addie grabs the cash from her mom"),
the writer places it so it reads as the breathless register, not as an error,
and does not touch it. Dialogue tags stand ("Asks her mom." "Stated Addie.").
Paragraph breaks may be inserted between sentences; sentences may not be split,
merged, or moved. The flagged `[typo?]` items in `BIBLE.md` (m) — the space
before a period, "resembling" for "resembled," the dangling "with" in Ch3
sentence 2, quotation-mark placement — are yours to rule on; the default is to
leave all but the plainest punctuation alone.

**The alternative, if you prefer it:** a single tense-normalizing pass on
02–06, treated as punctuation-level. Say so and it will be done in one commit
you can read line by line.

**Two seams the expansions have to carry, decided here:**

- Ch1 ends on a look Addie does not recognize; your Ch2 has Janet see "such a
  cheerful mood." Ruled: Addie puts the cheer on for her mother on the stairs
  and Janet sees through it and lets her.

## S. Where the door is, and what is under it

**Decision.** Building C of Riverbend stands on the old convent's foundation;
its basement is the convent cellar; the wooden door in the far wall opens on
the convent's bell-tower shaft going *down* — ninety-four steps — to the old
street. The chapel (the hold) is four hundred yards riverward along a brick

## V. Addie feels weather

**Decision.** Addie's gut-wrench in your Ch2 and her stomach in knots in your
Ch3 are the shadow's lie *failing* on her: the buried town's blood (through
Janet) feels the shadow as nausea instead of calm. Not magic; a family
inheritance, like her asymmetrical ears (Janet: "You got that from my mother").
It gives her a use under the city — she feels them coming in Ch13, she feels
the Mistress through the floor in Ch20, and in Ch21 she is the one who sees her
coming when nobody else does — and it gives Janet a line in Ch17: "You feel it
too. I hoped you wouldn't."

**Reasoning.** You wrote the nausea twice, both times next to her. It should
mean something, and it should be hers, not Felix's.

## W. How Addie comes to have the necklace (Ch3)

**Decision.** After your line "What a lovely stone, wherever did you get it?"
and Felix's answer, and after your last sentence (Addie remembers the store),
the expansion adds: Felix, self-conscious — his ears burn; the waitress looked
too long — takes it off and hands it across the table so Addie can see the
mark under the lamp; she is turning it in her fingers when she remembers the
store; she stands with it in her fist, shoves it in her jacket pocket with
Janet's cash, says "I'll text you," and goes; Felix, gathering the folder,
does not see. At the counter the waitress has gone very still and is looking at
the table, not the door.

**Reasoning.** Your Ch4 needs her to have it and not know; your Ch3 has him put
it on. Something has to happen in between, and if the necklace is coming off at
a public table under the Mistress's eyes ten minutes after a note said *at all
times*, that removal is the point of the scene — the Quiet Room's "careless the
way the young always did" — and the reader should watch the counter while it
happens.

## Y. Where the Hilt ends Book One

**Decision.** Up top — in the Knights' box under Felix's bed in a Garden
District apartment, warded by Janet. Its quiet reaches the street, which is why
she is *across* the street on the last page and not at the door. An ordinary
man can carry it away; Armand is up top with her. That is Book Two's first
move.

**Reasoning.** The README closes Book One "on the Hilt's custody being decided
— and on the Mistress knowing where her son is." Both, on one page, need the
Hilt where the son is.

## Z. Armand's fate

**Decision.** He lives. He goes up top with her, her seen face — a name, a gray
car nobody remembers, sunglasses that are finally not a joke. Anatole keeps a
smaller town and a door with fewer keepers. The Mistress does not need to
overthrow the town to win; she has what she came for, and Armand is more use
to her in daylight than in a hall.

**Reasoning.** She "doesn't play well with others," but she is patient, and a
seen face is the one thing she has never had. Book Two needs him.

## AA. The three catastrophes (CRAFT-FICTION asks for them pinned)

**Decision.** Quarter: Ch7 — the pawn shop tells Felix what the necklace is
