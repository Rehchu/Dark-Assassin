# Adoption Papers

It was a cool fall evening, the first real one of the year, and Addie Walker had
just typed the last line of her research paper for her experimental methodology
class and closed the laptop like she was shutting a door on it.

For three weeks the paper had been the only thing in her head. It had followed
her to bed and been there in the morning. It had sat down across from her at
every meal. Now it was done, sent, gone, a week before the Thanksgiving break,
and the quiet it left behind was so sudden and so complete that she just sat
there for a minute in the blue light of the screen, listening to the house tick
and settle around her, not sure what to do with her own hands.

Then she remembered the trails.

That was the thing about finishing. The whole long weekend opened up in front of
her all at once — the state forest an hour north, the loop out past the river
with the pileated woodpeckers, the cool gold light that came into the pines this
time of year and made everything look like it meant something. She had been
promising herself the trails since September. She loved the outdoors the way
some people loved a person, steadily and without needing to explain it, and her
shelves showed it: the field guide to the birds of the central Gulf states, the
one on the flora and fauna of the parish, the folding hand lens she kept meaning
to take with her and always forgot. Six months of school had kept her indoors
and blue-lit and hunched, and her body wanted the woods the way it wanted water
after a run.

But the woods were tomorrow. Tonight there was the garden, and the garden was
where she went to get the work out of her.

She went down the stairs two at a time, which was how she did most things, and
out the back door into the cool. The evening had that first-cold-snap smell to
it, wet leaves and woodsmoke from somebody's yard down the block, and the light
was nearly gone — that deep blue that was not quite dark, when the birds have
mostly quit and the crickets have not yet decided the night is theirs. She
turned on the little clip light over her potting bench and got to work.

There was a rhythm to it that she never had to think about. She refilled the
bird feeders first, the tube one and the platform and the suet cage, scooping
seed from the metal can while a chickadee that had no business being up this
late scolded her from the crepe myrtle for taking so long. She checked the water
in the bath. She turned to the bonsai on the end of the bench, the little
trident maple she had been training for two years, and she pinched back the new
growth with her fingernails, slow, careful, the one part of her life she did
slowly and carefully. The school stress went out of her hands and into the work.
It always did. That was what the garden was for.

Last she moved the orchid cactus.

It had outgrown its pot again — five years old now and still surprising her, a
sprawling green thing with flat trailing arms that would throw out those huge
impossible flowers once a year, deep orange and open all at once and gone by
morning. Felix had given it to her. He had brought it back from Belmopan, on
that trip to Belize the first winter they were together, carried the cutting
home wrapped in a wet paper towel inside his sock like a smuggler, and presented
it to her at the airport with the particular pride of a man who has broken a
minor law for love. *It's a jungle cactus,* he had said. *It's supposed to live
where you'd never expect it.* She had rooted the cutting on her windowsill and it
had never once looked back.

She was thinking about that — about the airport, about the sock, about how young
they had both looked in the picture from that trip — when her phone went off in
the pocket of her jacket, and Felix's name came up on the screen, and she smiled
before she even answered, because that was the effect he had on her, still, after
all this time.

She wiped her hands on her jeans and thumbed the call. "Hello?"

"Hey." Just the one word, and something in it made the smile go careful on her
face. She had known Felix Knight for four years and dated him for three, and she
could read him in a syllable the way she could read weather off a sky.

She kept it light anyway, to see if he would come up to meet it. "How's moving
into your new apartment coming along?"

"It is starting to come together nicely." A pause. She heard him moving through
the place, the particular echo a room has before there is enough in it. "Just a
few more touches, and it'll feel just like home."

Felix loved to travel. It was not a hobby with him, it was the shape of his
whole life — he was a travel blogger, and a genuinely good one, good enough that
the little apartment in the Garden District was mostly a place to keep his things
between flights. He had come home from Belfast three weeks ago, jet-lagged and
happy and full of stories, and gone straight into the move, and she had watched
him build the new place around himself box by box with the same restless energy
he did everything. She loved that about him. She loved the way he was never quite
still.

He was still now. She could hear it.

"Addie," he said, and then he stopped, and the stopping was the loudest thing he
had done since he said hello.

"I'm here," she said.

"I called because I have some news I don't like the feeling about." The words
came out in a rush, the way his words did when he had been sitting on them. "I
was going through some of my old things, packing up the last of what my parents
sent over, and I found some adoption papers. I think they're mine."

The garden went very quiet around her. Or maybe it was only that she had stopped
hearing it. She stood there with the clip light throwing her shadow long across
the yard and the cactus half out of its old pot in her two hands, and for a
moment she could not make the sentence turn into a thing she understood.

"Adoption papers," she said.

"Yeah."

"Felix." She set the cactus down on the bench, gently, because her hands had
started to feel unreliable and she did not trust them with it. "Okay. Slow down.
Do you *know* that they're yours?"

"I don't — I don't know. I don't know what I know."

"Do you think your parents would have informed you about something like this?"
It was the wrong question and the right one at the same time, and she heard it
land in him, heard him take it the way you take a step you thought was there and
find is not.

His parents. Robert and Diane Knight, two accountants in the Garden District,
the kindest and most orderly people she had ever met, the kind of people who
alphabetized the spice rack and filed their receipts by month. If there was one
family in the whole state that did not keep secrets, it was the Knights. That was
the thing that did not fit. That was the thing that was making the back of her
neck go cold in the cooling evening.

On the other end of the line she heard paper. The dry riffle of it, pages turned
too fast by a hand that could not settle, and then turned back, slower, as
something caught his eye.

"There's a header," Felix said. His voice had changed. It had gone flat and
careful, the voice of a person reading out loud so they do not have to think
about the words. "At the top of the first page. It's in bold."

"What does it say?"

"It says—" He stopped. She could picture him exactly, standing in the middle of
his half-unpacked apartment with a box open at his feet and a stack of yellowing
paper in his hand and the streetlight coming orange through a window that did not
have curtains yet. She had spent three years learning that face. She wished, very
much, that she could see it now.

"*Alexandria Orphanage,*" he read. "In big letters. *Alexandria Orphanage.*" A
beat. "Addie, do we even have an orphanage in Alexandria?"

And that, of all of it, was the question that made it real — such a small,
ordinary, practical question, the kind you'd ask about a restaurant or a hardware
store, asked in a voice that was trying to hold itself together by pretending
this was that kind of thing.

"I don't think we do anymore," she said slowly. She was already reaching for it,
the way you reach for a fact to have something to hold. "I don't — no. I think it
had gotten redeveloped. Converted. Into a new apartment complex, out on the east
side, off the river road." She could see it in her head, more or less, the way
you can see a place you have driven past a hundred times and never once looked
at. Her mother had mentioned it once. She pushed that thought away for later.
"They tore the old building down years ago. I don't think there's been an
orphanage in this town since we were kids."

"So there was one," Felix said. "When we were kids."

"I guess there was."

The silence came back and sat between them, the whole four hundred miles of
fiber and air between his apartment and her garden, and it was not an empty
silence. It was full of the thing neither of them was saying, which was that
somewhere in the last ten minutes the floor of Felix's life had turned out to be
a lid, and neither of them yet knew what was under it, only that it was open now,
and would not close.

She looked down at her hands on the bench. Her heart was going harder than the
moment called for. She made herself breathe.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. Here's what we're not going to do. We're not going to
sit two miles apart in the dark spinning out about a piece of paper. You're going
to put that folder down, and you're going to get on your feet, and we are going
to go somewhere with lights and coffee and other people in it and figure out what
this actually is."

She heard him let out a breath she had not known he was holding. It was the
first thing he had done, in the whole call, that sounded like himself.

"Do you want to meet up at Ugly Mug Coffee?" he asked. There was the ghost of the
old Felix in it, the one who always knew the right place to be. "I think we need
to discuss this more in person. And take a walk downtown to help clear my mind."

"I'll meet you there," she said. "Twenty minutes. Bring the folder. Don't read
any more of it without me — promise."

"I promise."

"Felix." She did not know what she was going to say until she said it. "Whatever
this is, you're the same person you were an hour ago. Paper doesn't change who
you are."

"I know," he said, and she could hear that he did not, quite, believe it, and she
could hear that he loved her for saying it. "See you there."

He hung up.

Addie stood in the garden a moment longer with the dead phone in her hand. The
clip light hummed. The chickadee had gone quiet in the crepe myrtle, and the
crickets had not started, and for a strange suspended minute the whole yard was
between sounds, the way the Prelude of a song is between sounds, holding its
breath before the first real note. She became aware, in a way she could not have
explained and did not try to, that the evening had gotten darker than it should
have in the time she had been on the phone. The blue had gone out of the sky. It
was only night, she told herself. It was only fall coming on.

She looked at the orchid cactus, half out of its pot, its roots naked in the
lamplight, five years of steady patient growth exposed all at once to the cold
air. She should finish repotting it. She should not leave it like that.

She left it like that.

She turned off the clip light, and the dark came in around the bench, and she
went inside to get her keys and her helmet. On the way through the kitchen she
caught her own reflection in the black window over the sink — a young woman with
her hair coming loose, potting soil on her jeans, a look on her face she did not
recognize — and she thought, with the small clear part of her mind that never
stopped narrating, *this is the last ordinary night,* and did not know why she
thought it, and could not make herself unthink it.

Then she was out the door and the bike was under her and the engine caught on the
first kick, loud and rude in the quiet street, and she was gone toward downtown
and the lights and the boy she loved, carrying a header in bold type behind her
eyes — ALEXANDRIA ORPHANAGE — down streets that ran, though she would not know
it for a long time yet, directly over the top of a room that no map had ever
held, where a woman with black hair sat in the dark and waited to feel a light
she had been hunting for fourteen years.

The city did not look down. It never did.

Addie leaned into the turn onto Jackson Street and opened the throttle, and the
wind took the cold right through her jacket, and she welcomed it, because it was
real, and because for one more minute, everything still was.
