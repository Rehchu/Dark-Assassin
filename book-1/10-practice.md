# Practice

The door over her head had opened at twenty to five in the afternoon and stayed
open for the length of a held breath, and she had felt it the way you feel a
window opened in a far room of a house you have lived in all your life — not a
sound, not a draft, a change in the pressure of the dark — and she had sat at
the low table with her hands open on the wood and let the breath be held, and
let it go, and she had not moved.

That had been eight hours ago. She had not moved since.

The candle in its dish of black glass had burned down two fingers. The cloth on
the walls, gray on gray on gray, had swallowed the small sounds she made not
moving. And on the table in front of her the things of her evening lay where
they always lay, the vial the color of tea, the folding knife with the bone
handle, the cord, the brass box she did not open, and she looked at none of
them, because she was looking at the week.

Five days. She counted them the way she had once counted years.

Saturday, a warmth. The first in fourteen years — up, and moving, and near, a
thing carried in a car trunk between one roof and another for forty minutes, and
gone under a roof again, but not the old roof, not the one that had held its
silence so long she had come to think of the silence as the shape of the town.
A new roof. A young man's roof. She had gone up that night and every night
since.

Thursday, a girl. Copper hair coming loose under a helmet, a bike, a mouth that
narrated. The girl had come out of a house that smelled of sage from across
the street and had looked straight at her — *looked,* through the
not-being-seen, the way only the old blood looked — and had gone gray in the
face and put her hand on the bike, and had ridden off toward the warmth as if
she were tied to it. She was. That was the whole use of her.

Thursday, a table. A coffee shop on Third Street, a corner table under a lamp,
a boy she could not hold in her eye. She had stood three feet from him with a
carafe of water and her eyes had gone to the folder and the cup and the window
and the top of his head, and slid, and slid, the way water slides off glass.
She had not minded. She had waited fourteen years; she could wait through a
shift. And then he had taken it off.

She closed her hands on the wood, remembering.

He had lifted it over his head, laughing at something the girl said, and handed
it across the table, and for the length of time it lay in the girl's palm under
the lamp the smudge had sharpened, all at once, into a *shape* — a shoulder, a
line of jaw, dark hair falling over the sides of a face — and then the girl had
stood up with it in her fist and walked out the door and the shape had gone
with her, and the boy at the table had been a boy at a table, plain as bread,
and she still could not have said what his eyes were.

But she knew which table. She knew the block his roof was on, now — she had
stood under a row of windows in the Garden District at eleven that night with
the streetlight orange on the wet leaves and the warmth over her head like a
hand, and she had looked at six windows and could not say which was his. She
could have climbed. She could have gone in at any one of them and found a boy
asleep and put her hand on his shoulder. She did not want him asleep. She did
not want him dead. She wanted him *seen,* the way she had seen him for one
half of one second under a coffee-shop lamp, and for that he had to take it
off himself, in the open, under a sky, and be looking at her when he did.

So she had gone to the girl's street instead, and stood across from the
sage-smelling house until the light went off upstairs, and learned nothing but
the smell.

Friday, a whisper. Her mouth at his ear for the length of a sip of coffee, and
the words she had chosen for fourteen years without knowing she was choosing
them, and his face going the color of milk. *I'll have to check it out,* he had
said, the boy, to the woman who had made him. She had nearly laughed. And then
the old man on Main Street, who had taken the necklace in his hands and looked
at the string first, and had sent them not home but to the lot — *to the lot* —
and so had told her, at last, after twenty years, which side of the counter he
stood on.

Saturday, a door.

She opened her hands.

When she opened them again her eyes had changed, and the shadow came up off the
floor to meet her, slow, patient the way water is patient, pooling at her
wrists and lying along her forearms like a thing that had decided, after long
consideration, that she would do. She let it. She had spent forty years learning
to let it. And she stood up from the low table, and took the knife, and the
vial, and left the brass box where it was, and went out through the cloth.

---

She passed the second chamber on her way to the crack.

She did not go in. There was nothing in there that needed her tonight — four
shapes on four low beds under the one lamp Armand kept lit for them, breathing
the slow breathing of the held, their eyes open and seeing nothing she had not
put there. Four in six weeks. They were a thing she was learning, and she was
patient with things she was learning, and they would keep.

Then the crack, and the dark of it, and her hand on wet stone, and up.

She did not use the door. She never had. The door was the town's — the
Bordelons' and the Lemoines' and their frightened forty, who went up through it
at night for batteries and flour and came back down and barred it and told
themselves the bar was what kept them safe. She had her own ways up, and they
were older than the door and older than the town on top and the town below it
both, cracks that had been cracks before anyone thought to build over them, and
she went up through the black of them without a light, the shadow gone close
and quiet around her like a coat, until the stone gave way to brick, and the
brick to iron, and the iron, at last, to air that moved.

The air that moved was wet.

She came up under a grate in the low place on the river road, where the road
dipped under the levee and the storm drains sat in the curb with their mouths
open, and she lay a moment with her cheek against the iron and listened to the
night on the other side of it. Wind. The first real wind in two days, warm
still, pushing the leaves along the gutter over her head with a dry scraping
sound like something being dragged. No rain yet. The rain was coming; she could
smell it in the wind, the river-and-iron smell of a front. Frogs. A dog. And,
close — twenty feet, no more — an engine idling, and a radio turned down low
inside it, and a man's boots on the pavement, and a light.

She went still.

The boots came along the curb. The light came with them, a flashlight, swinging
low, along the gutter, along the leaves, and stopped on the grate over her
face, and held there. A man's voice said, "Yeah, that one's half blocked," to
nobody, or to the radio, and a boot came down on the grate an inch from her
eyes, and she felt the iron take his weight, and did not breathe.

A parish truck. She knew the shape of the words on the door without seeing
them. A man sent out at two in the morning ahead of a rain to check the drains
at the low place on the river road, because the low place flooded, because
everyone in this town knew it flooded, because it had flooded fourteen years
ago on the night a man with a child under his coat had knelt in it up to his
wrists and pulled her light back up out of the dark by one bead.

The boot lifted. The light moved on. She heard him crouch at the next grate
down and swear at it, gently, the way a man swears at a thing that is only
doing its job.

She put her hands flat against the iron over her face and pushed.

---

He never heard her come up.

That was the whole of it. The shadow reached him a half-second before she did,
the small gray suggestion it laid on a person — that everything was fine, that
the street behind him was empty, that the cold at the back of the neck was
only the front coming in — and he believed it, the way they all believed it, the
way a man believes the ground. He was crouched at the second grate with his
flashlight in his teeth and a gloved hand in the leaves, and she came up out of
the first one behind him without a sound that mattered and set the grate back
in its frame, and walked to him along the curb in the wind, and did not hurry.
Hurry was seen.

She put her hand on his shoulder. Gently. The way you turn a person who is
looking the wrong way.

He turned.

He was fifty, or near it. A big man gone soft, with a parish jacket and a
reflective vest over it and a wedding ring on the glove, and a face that had
spent thirty years being sent out in the rain to look at drains and had never
once expected anything to be down there but leaves. The flashlight fell out of
his mouth and rolled and lit the gutter. His eyes found her eyes.

She let him see them.

That was the one mercy in her and it was not for him. It was for her — for
the thing that happened in a face in the half-instant before the shadow took
the light out of it, when the surface broke and the person under the person
looked out, unhidden, the way nobody was ever unhidden up here in the noise and
the sun. He looked out at her. He saw the red. He understood, in the wordless
way they understood, that the thing he had been sent out in the dark to find
was not in the drain and never had been, and that it was looking at him, and
then the shadow closed over him the way still water closes over a dropped stone,
without a splash, without a ring, and the big soft man sat down against the
levee wall at the low place on the river road as though he had only decided to
rest a minute, and his eyes stayed open, and the vial in her pocket stayed
stoppered, because she had not needed it. She almost never needed it.

The wind pushed the leaves along the gutter past his boots.

She stood over him. She had not chosen him; the night had. He had put his boot
on her way up and that was all, and it was enough, and it was also, she found,
standing there with the ache going quiet in her for the first time in a week,
something she had needed more than she had let herself know. Five days of
warmth over her head, and a boy she could not hold in her eye, and a door
opened a hand's width by a girl who could look at her — a person could go a
long time on that, and a person could also want, badly, one plain thing that
went the way things were supposed to go.

They would find him in the morning. A parish worker on a night call, against a
wall, no wound, the truck still running. The coroner would write the thing the
coroner wrote. The paper would run it small. Someone at the parish would have
to make the call to a house where a woman was asleep, and a phone would ring at
a console somewhere in the dark with a dispatcher's flat voice on the other end
of it, and the dispatcher would take the address and the time and write them
down.

She turned from him. The truck idled on. She left it.

---

She went to his block again. She could not have said why.

The Garden District at three in the morning, the live oaks black against a sky
gone the color of wet slate, the wind in them now, the first drops coming — not
rain yet, the front's loose change, a drop on a car roof, a drop on a leaf. She
stood under the row of windows on the side street off Jackson with the shadow
lying thin and flat around her feet and the warmth over her head, exactly over
her head, close enough to climb to, and she looked at six windows and could not
say which one was his.

Six windows. Five dark. One with the blue of a screen behind it, faint, a boy
awake at three in the morning looking at something.

That one, she thought. Or the one beside it. Or none of them.

The charm hid a door as well as a face. She had known that; it was her charm;
she had paid an old man on Main Street for exactly this, once, near closing,
when the light was going, and had worn it fourteen years of her own life and
had never been found. It was doing what she had bought it to do. There was a
justice in that she did not have the patience for tonight.

The rain came.

It came the way it came here in November when a front broke, all at once, warm
and loud and total, the oaks roaring with it, the gutters filling in a minute,
the streetlight over the side street wearing a halo and then nearly going out
behind the sheets of it. She stood in it. She let it come down on her and run
off the black of her hair and go on running, and she looked at the one blue
window, and she thought about a night fourteen years ago when it had rained
like this, exactly like this, and she had sat up in the dark under the city
with her light gone out of the world for one breath, and had not screamed.

Somebody had put out her light and hidden it in a bushel. Somebody clever.
Somebody close.

And she knew where he was, now. Not the boy. The other one. The one who had
loved her light as much as she did, who had lifted the charm off her throat one
bead at a time while she slept, who had knelt in this rain in the low place on
the river road with a child under his coat. He had never left. She had known it
for years the way you know a tooth is bad: a room four hundred yards riverward
from the frightened town, behind an iron rail, where the shadow would not go
and she had never once been able to make it. He had sat behind that rail for
fourteen years with the one thing in the world she could not touch, and waited
for the boy to come, and now the boy was coming, and the man behind the rail
would do what men behind rails always did, in the end, when the thing they
loved was on the wrong side of it.

He would cross.

She turned from the blue window and went back down into the dark to wait for
that.

---

Armand was in the room when she came back through the cloth.

She had sent for him. He was the one she sent for; for a year now, when she
wanted a thing carried up top or a thing carried down, it had been Anatole's
brother who came, quick and charming and hungry, with his sunglasses pushed up
on his head in a place where there was no sun, and he was standing by the low
table now, dripping, because he had come by her crack and not his brother's
door, which was a thing he did to show her he could.

He did not look at the second chamber. He never did.

"Ma'am."

She sat. She put the knife on the table and the vial beside it and folded her
hands, and the shadow went down off her like a tide going out, and her eyes
cooled to brown, and she looked at him with them until he stopped being pleased
with himself.

"The Walker woman uses the door," he said. "Friday. Late. Went down to my
brother and came back up before three. My brother's watchers know her. They let
her by like she's — like she's *family.*"

"She is."

He had not known that. She watched him not know it.

"And the girl," she said. "Copper hair. The one on the bike. She's a Walker
too. She looks at me and gets sick." She said it the way you say the weather.
"That's the old blood. That's what it does when it's near me. They went up top
three generations ago and thought they'd left it under the city and they took
it with them in the girl's mother's mother, and now it's back at the door with a
bike and a boy, and your brother's people will know her the minute they see
her, and they'll take her to the hall and give her tea."

"So what do you —"

"Let them come down."

He waited.

"They're coming tomorrow. The girl said so, at the door, with her hand on the
ring — your men heard her; you told me." She turned the knife a quarter-turn on
the wood. "Let them come. Do not stop them at the top. Do not stop them on the
stair. Let them come all the way down to the old street and be sure they are
down, and then take them to me. To *me,* Armand. Not to your brother. Not to
the hall. Not to be *presented,* the way the truce says, with your brother
reading his five terms off the wall like a man reading a lease. You take them
along the old street to the far end and you bring them down my way, and you
bring them to this room."

"Anatole will —"

"Anatole will keep the truce. That is what Anatole does. He will keep it right
up until there is nothing left to keep it with." She looked at him. "You want
the sun. You have told me so in a hundred ways since the first day I sent for
you, and I have listened, and I have not laughed. Bring me the boy, and I will
show you what the sun is for."

He was quiet a moment. Then he nodded, once, the way a man nods when he has
already decided and is only letting you watch him do it.

"The girl," he said.

"Do not touch the girl."

"If she —"

"Do not touch her. Not a hand on her. Not a rope. You hood the boy if you have
to, you carry him if you have to, you do what you have to do to the boy — he is
mine and I will mend whatever you break. The girl walks. The girl walks beside
him the whole way down with her hands free and not a mark on her, do you hear
me, because she is the one thing near him that I can *see,* and I want her
whole and I want her looking at me when she gets here."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Go."

He went. Out through the cloth, and she heard him in the dark beyond it going
up her crack again, fast, the way the young went, careless, sure of every step
in a place he had walked for a year and she had walked for forty. Then nothing.
Then the rain, a far dim roar through a hundred feet of stone, coming down on
the town and the river and the low place on the road where a big soft man sat
against a wall with his eyes open and the leaves going by his boots.

She sat with her hands open on the wood.

The ache had gone quiet. It was not gone. It never went. But it had loosened
its hold the way it always did, enough, and she had a boy over her head who
could not be seen and a girl beside him who could, and a man behind a rail who
would cross it, and a door that had opened a hand's width and would open all
the way tomorrow, in the rain, with a real light.

Fourteen years she had waited. She could wait one more night.

She said a name, very quietly, and the cloth ate it before it reached the
walls, and she closed her eyes.
