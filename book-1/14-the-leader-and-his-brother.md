# The Leader and His Brother

The hall was a warehouse once. He could see that the way he could see the
bell stair was a bell stair: the vault of it, brick, high enough that the
lamps on the walls did not reach the top, and the long floor, and the iron
rings set into the pillars where something had been tied off a hundred and
fifty years ago, bales or barrels or mules. Somebody's goods had sat in here
waiting for the river. Now there was a table down the middle of it as long as
a church aisle, made of doors laid on trestles, and benches, and lamps, and
at the far end a fire in a hearth cut into the brick with a flue that went up
into the earth and, somewhere, out — because the smoke went somewhere — and
along both walls, on the benches, on the floor, in the doorways of the side
vaults, standing, sitting, holding children, the town.

Forty people. He counted, later, and lost count, and settled on forty.

They were not what he had thought a thing at the bottom of a stair would be.
They were people. Pale, all of them, the pale of skin that had not had sun in
a long time, and thin, and dressed in layers of cloth that had been patched
and patched again and washed, and every one of them looked at him when Odile
walked him in with his knee bandaged and his hand at his collar. Not at Addie.
At him. At his face, and the sides of his face, the way people had been
looking at him all week — and then away, fast, the way you look away from a
thing you have been told not to stare at.

A woman with a baby on her hip. Three old men on a bench with their hands on
their knees. A girl Addie's age with her hair in a cloth. Children, six of
them, in a row on the floor by the fire with a slate between them, and a
woman standing over the slate who had been teaching them something when the
door opened and had stopped.

A school. They had a school.

"Sit," said the man at the head of the table. "Mais, sit. Both of you. Nobody
in this room is going to touch you."

---

His name was Anatole. He said it the way you say a thing you have said in this
room ten thousand times: *I'm Anatole Bordelon. This is my hall.* He was
fifty, or near it, thick through the shoulders, with a beard whose ends had
been singed by a lamp so many times they had given up growing back straight,
and hands like a man who laid brick, and a face that was tired all the way
down and honest about it. He had a French under his English that came up when
he was tired. He was tired now.

He had the backpack.

It was on the table in front of him, Addie's pack, the yellow of it loud in
the lamplight, with the zipper closed and the straps folded neat on top of it
the way you fold the straps of a thing you are going to give back. Felix
looked at it. Addie looked at it. Anatole saw them look, and he put his hand
flat on it, and did not open it.

"You came down my door," he said. "Uninvited. In the rain. With a rope and
two lamps and a boy who limps now and a girl who fights nets." He almost
smiled. "And you came down it a minute ahead of my brother's men, which is
the only reason you're sitting at my table and not somewhere else, and I
want you to understand that before I say anything, because everything I say
after this is going to be about that minute."

"Who are you," Felix said.

"I told you."

"No. Who are — " He looked at the room. At the woman with the baby. At the
slate. "Who *are* you. What is this. This is — there's a *street.* There's a
church under a — there's a stair, there's a lamp, there's a man named Lemoine
cut into a — what is this? What are you *doing* down here in the — in the
ruins of —"

"We live here."

Anatole said it quietly. He did not raise his voice. But the room went still
around it the way a room goes still when somebody has said the one thing in
it that is not up for argument.

"That's the word you want, *ruins,* and it's the wrong word, and I'll thank
you not to use it in my hall. My grandmother was born on that street. Her
grandmother was born on that street, in the house with the swan bottle in the
window, when there was a sky over it. The river came up and the fire came
down and the town on top built itself on our roofs, and some of us went up,
and some of us didn't, and the ones who didn't are in this room. That's the
whole of it. We're not ruins. We're not ghosts. We're not a *court,* whatever
she calls us." A breath. "We live here. We keep a door. We've kept it a hundred
and fifty years. That's who we are."

"Who's *she,*" Addie said.

Anatole looked at her for a long moment. Then he looked at the pack under his
hand. Then he looked, for the first time, down the table — to the far end,
past the fire, where a man was leaning against a pillar with his arms folded
and a pair of sunglasses pushed up on his head like a joke that had stopped
being one.

"That's my brother," Anatole said. "Armand. He's going to want to talk. He's
been wanting to talk since you came through the curtain. I'm going to let
him, in a minute, because this is a hall and not a jail and everybody in it
gets to say their piece. But first I'm going to say mine." He took his hand
off the pack. "Because I don't think he'll say it right."

---

He said it like a lease.

That was the thing Felix would remember. Not the words — he would remember the
words too, all his life, all five of them — but the way the man said them,
standing at the head of a table made of doors with forty people watching, in
the voice of a man reading terms off a paper he did not draw up and could not
change and had been living inside since before he was born.

"There is a truce," Anatole said. "It was made by my grandmother with — with
the mother of the one below. Under this floor. Further down than you've been.
It has five terms and none of them are written and every soul in this room
knows them like they know their own names, and I'm going to say them to you
in order, so that when my brother says the third one at you the way he's
about to, you'll know what it is."

He held up one thick finger.

"One. She does not hunt the town." The finger went down. "That's the one that
matters. That's the one we're alive by. Before the truce she did. Before the
truce, my people went out on the old street for water and didn't come back,
one every few months, for eighty years, and they never knew what it was in the
dark that took them, only that it did. My grandmother knew. My grandmother
went down and found out, and came back up with her hair gone white, and made
the truce."

Two fingers.

"Two. We do not use her ways, and we do not speak of her up top. Her ways are
the cracks. Off the far end of the street, off the square. They go down
further than anybody here has ever gone and come back to say. We don't touch
them. We don't go near them. And when we go up the door for what we need —
batteries, flour, a doctor once, God help us — we don't say her name to
anybody up there, ever, not if they beat us. And we don't know it anyway.
Nobody here knows it. We say *her.*"

Three.

"Three." He stopped. The room stopped with him. He looked at his brother down
the table and his brother looked back, and something went between them that
had been going between them for a long time. "Three. Whoever comes down the
door uninvited is presented to her."

Nobody said anything.

"That's the price," Anatole said. "That's what we paid to be let alone. The
door is ours. It locks from our side. We go up through it for what we need and
we come back down and we bar it. But if somebody comes *down* it — somebody
from up top, uninvited, lost, curious, a drunk who got into a basement, a boy
with a paper in a folder — we don't send them back up. We can't. We take them
to the far end of the street and we send them down her way and she — " He
stopped again. "She takes some. She lets some go. The ones she lets go come
back up the street and go up the door and never come down it again, and don't
remember much, and tell people up top they got lost in a storm drain. The ones
she takes, she takes."

"The missing," Addie said. Her voice came out strange. "My mother said there
was a string of — she said missing persons. She said four in six weeks."

Anatole's eyes went to her and stayed.

"Four in six weeks isn't the truce," he said slowly. "The truce is one in ten
years, maybe. A drunk. A city man with a flashlight looking for the old
sewers. Four in six weeks is — " He did not look at his brother. He very
carefully did not look at his brother. "Four in six weeks is something else."

Four fingers.

"Four. She does not cross the rail." He said it fast, the way you say a thing
that is a relief. "There's a rail. Iron. Across the mouth of the old chapel
crypt at the end of the river way, four hundred yards from where you're
sitting. She doesn't go past it. She's never gone past it. That's a term of
the truce and it's the one she's never broken."

Five.

"Five. The town feeds the hold." He put his hand down flat on the table. "The
crypt behind the rail. There's a man in it. There's been a man in it since
before I was born — a different man, then. His father. We feed him. Odile
carries it. We've fed him every day for fourteen years and his father for
twenty before that, and nobody in this room has ever once asked him why he
sits in a hole behind a piece of iron with a lamp and doesn't come out, because
that was the fifth term, and we swore it, and we keep what we swear."

He looked at Felix.

"That's the truce."

---

"His father," Felix said.

He did not know he had said it until it was said. His hand was at his collar.
The room had gone away — the fire, the slate, the forty faces — and there was
only the table, and the man at the head of it, and the yellow pack.

"Fourteen years," Felix said. "You said — fourteen years. A man behind a rail.
Fourteen years."

"Yes."

"Why fourteen."

Anatole did not answer. He picked up the pack. He came down the table with it,
past the benches and the people on the benches, slowly, with his hand on it,
and he set it down in front of Addie, and stood there.

"I didn't open it," he said. "Nobody did. It's yours."

Addie put both hands on it.

"There's a folder in there," Anatole said. He was not talking to Addie. "And
in the folder there's papers with the boy's name on them, and there's
letters, and there's a note, and there's a mark in red wax that I'd know from
across this hall because it's on a ring that a man behind a rail four hundred
yards from here has worn on a cord around his neck every day of the fourteen
years I've fed him. He told me what's in it. Jan told me. Alcide told Jan."
He looked at Felix. "I know what you are, son. I've known since Friday. I've
known since before that, if I'm honest, since a man came down the river way
one night with a lamp and his knuckles bleeding and asked my father for the
crypt."

Felix could not breathe.

"Your father is here," Anatole said. "He's been here the whole time. He's
sitting behind that rail tonight with the — with a thing in his hand he
hasn't picked up in a year, and he's waiting for you, and I promised him
Friday I'd bring you by the river way, and I meant it when I said it."

"Then *bring* me —"

"And that," said the voice from the pillar, "is where I come in."

---

Armand came up the hall the way water comes up a beach.

Not fast. He had all the time in the world and he wanted the room to see that
he had it. He was his brother's face with the fat gone out of it, a scar
through one eyebrow, the sunglasses pushed up in his hair, and he walked
between the benches with a hand trailing along the shoulders of the people on
them, easy, friendly, a man among his own, and not one of the shoulders he
touched was easy under his hand.

"No worries," he said. To Felix. It came out of him like a thing he had heard
up top and liked the sound of. "No worries, man. Nobody here's going to hurt
you. My brother said. My brother says a lot of things." He stopped at the end
of the table and leaned on it with both hands and looked at Anatole, and he
smiled. "He said the truce, just now. All five. Did you hear him say it,
everybody? In order? Like Grandmère? Like a *lease?*"

Nobody answered him. The children by the fire had gone quiet.

"So here's the thing about a lease," Armand said. "You don't get to read four
lines of it and skip the one you don't like." He straightened. He said it to
the room, turning, so that it reached the back wall. "*Whoever comes down the
door uninvited is presented to her.* That's the third term. That's the term
that's kept every one of you breathing for a hundred and fifty years. They
came down the door. Uninvited. In the rain, with a rope. They're *presented.*
That's not me saying it, that's Grandmère, that's the truce, that's the thing
my brother just stood up there and said he'd swear by."

"They're not —" Anatole began.

"They're not what? Not from up top? Look at them. Not uninvited? Who invited
them?" He turned back. "You did. Friday. When the Walker woman came down and
whispered in your ear and you sent Ti-Jean running to the rail. You invited
them, after the fact, so you could skip the third term, and you did it for a
man in a hole who's never once in fourteen years done a thing for this town
but eat its bread." He put his hand on his chest, mocking the gesture, and
Felix saw Anatole flinch at it. "I'm not the one breaking it, Anatole. You
are. In front of everybody. For two. And I'm asking — I'm just *asking* — how
the truce holds for forty of us and not for you."

Silence. The fire. A child's foot scraping on brick.

Anatole stood at the table with his hands flat on it, and Felix watched him
look for a way out, and watched him not find one, and understood, in the way
you understand a thing you are watching happen to someone else, that this was
a decent man, and that decent was not going to be enough, and that the man
knew it better than anyone in the room.

"Ti-Jean," Anatole said.

The boy was there. He was always there.

"Run to the rail. Tell him — " He stopped. He looked at Felix. "Tell him
they're being taken to her. Under the third term. Tell him I couldn't stop it
without breaking the thing that keeps forty of us alive, and tell him I'm
sorry, and tell him I'll be at the far end of the street when they come back
up it, because they *will* come back up it." He turned to his brother. "She
lets some go. That's the term. She lets some go, and you bring them back.
Both of them. To me. To this hall. That's my instruction, Armand. In front of
everybody. You bring them *back.*"

"Sure," Armand said. "No worries."

Ti-Jean was already gone.

---

Addie had the pack on her back.

She had put it on while the brothers talked, both straps, pulled tight, the way
she had put it on in the rain in the lot, and she stood up from the bench with
it on and her wrist wrapped and her face white and set, and she looked at
Felix, and he got up too, on the knee that did not want him to.

"It stays on," she said. Low. For him.

His hand was at his collar. The stone was under it, warm, against his skin.

"I know."

"Whatever she is. Whatever's down there. Say okay."

"Okay."

Armand had the curtain at the far end held back with one hand and the
sunglasses down over his eyes now, in a hall lit by lamps, and two of his men
were behind him with a lantern, and beyond the curtain the street went off
into the dark toward the end Addie had felt the sickness come from, and the
town watched them go the way you watch a thing you have decided not to be able
to stop.

At the curtain Felix looked back once. Anatole was still standing at the head
of the table with his hands on the doors it was made of. He did not look like a
man who had won anything. He looked like a man who had just told another man's
son that his father was alive, and then sent the son away down a dark street,
and would have to live in this room with the people who had watched him do it.

"Bring them back," Anatole said again, to nobody, to the table.

Then the curtain fell, and the street took them, and Felix went down it beside
Addie with his father four hundred yards behind him at the end of a way he had
not been shown, and his hand at his throat, and a woman somewhere below waiting
in the dark to be introduced.
