# Practice, Interrupted

The lamps came at five.

Six of them, this time, and the men carrying them did not stop at the flat
line on the brick where the shadow had lain down the night before. They came
on past it. That was the first thing Felix understood, standing at the rail
with his hands on the iron and the quiet of the Hilt around him like a room —
that the line on the floor was hers and not theirs, that the rail stopped one
thing in the world and it was not six men with cudgels, and that the men had
known it all along and had only been waiting to be told.

Armand came first. Sunglasses down. No lantern; he did not need one with six
behind him. He walked up to the rail on the far side and put his hand on it,
on the iron, the way you put your hand on a fence you have decided is not a
fence anymore.

"No worries," he said.

Behind the six, back where the lamps did not reach, the dark had a shape in
it. Tall. Standing the way you stand when you are not waiting for anything.
And at her feet, three long shapes on the wet floor with their faces turned up.

"Delvaux," Armand said. "Step away from the rock."

His father did not step away from the rock. His father stood between the rail
and the altar with the folding knife open in his hand — the bone-handled
knife, the one he had bought on Main Street twenty years ago and trimmed wicks
with, the one Felix had watched him look at on the cot last night and put back
in his pocket without a word — and he said nothing, and the six came over the
iron.

---

It went by numbers.

That was the whole of it. Felix had thought, in the long hours since the
woman said *not long,* that there would be something else — that his father
would do a thing, that the Hilt would do a thing, that a rail the shadow would
not cross would somehow be a rail a man would not cross either. It was not.
Six men came over a waist-high piece of iron the way six men come over a
fence, and there were four people on the other side of it, and one of them
was a gaunt man with a pocketknife and one was a boy with a bad knee and two
were women, and the Hilt lay on its cloth and was a piece of iron.

They went for the women.

He saw why, later. He could not see it then. They went past his father — two
of them, with cudgels, keeping him back with the reach of the wood — and past
Felix, who swung at the first one and hit him and did not slow him down, and
they went for Janet, who had gotten up off the floor with her dispatcher's
face and her fists up like a woman who had once taken a self-defense class at
the parish and remembered one thing from it, and for Addie.

Janet hit one of them. He heard it — a flat wet sound, her knuckles on a face.
Then the man beside that one hit her, once, across the eye, and once again,
low, in the side, and she went down on one knee with a sound Felix would hear
for the rest of his life, and did not get up, and two of them had Addie by the
arms.

"Felix —"

She was fighting them. She fought the way she had fought the net, with
everything, the wrapped wrist and the other one, her boots, her teeth. It did
not matter. They were big and she was not and they had her by the elbows and
they were walking her backward toward the rail, toward the iron, toward the
far side of it where the lamps were and the dark had a shape in it, and she
was looking at him over their shoulders the whole way with her face gone white
under the freckles, and she was not saying his name anymore. She was saying
*don't.*

*Don't.*

She knew. She had said last night that she knew. *You're going to do it.*

They lifted her over the rail.

---

He had thought about it for a night and a day.

He had thought about it on the crate with his hand at his collar and the ring
against the stone, and on the floor with his knee out, and standing against
the wall under a charcoal mark a hand's width too high. He had turned it over
the way the thing in him had turned over in the tin-mirror room. He knew what
it cost. His father had told him plain, in order, like a man laying out tools:
*if she ever sees your face, it's done.* He knew that the shadow would not
come to a person who needed it — Anatole had said *like a dog,* his father had
said *it smells need on you* — and he knew that he needed it now more than he
had ever needed anything, and that this was exactly the wrong way to want it.

He knew all of that. It did not help.

Addie was on the far side of the rail with two men's hands on her and the
lamps in her face and her mother on the floor behind him breathing in a way
that was wrong, and there was a thing in him, low, behind the breastbone,
that had moved once when a candle bent and had gone still in this room and
was the only thing he had that six men did not.

He reached up under his collar.

His father saw him do it. He heard his father say his name — *Felix* — in the
rust voice, from behind the cudgels, and heard what was in it, which was not
*no.* Which was worse than no. Which was a man who had told him the rules
watching his son decide about them.

He got his fingers under the cord. The beads dragged up through his hair the
way they always did. The stone came out of the hollow of his throat, and the
ring beside it, and for a second they hung from his hand in the lamplight
between him and the rail, turning, the way they had hung between him and
Alcide across a counter on Friday, a lifetime ago, and his throat under the
collar went cold and open and *pointed at.*

He took it off.

Over his head. His own hand. On purpose. He held it in his right fist, the
beads and the stone and the ring, and he did not put it in anyone's keeping,
and he did not set it on any felt. He held it. And then, so that there would be
no question, so that it would be the thing he had chosen and not a thing that
had happened to him, he stepped over the rail.

---

Nothing came.

He stood on the wet brick on the far side of the iron with the necklace in his
fist and the quiet gone off him like a coat taken off in the wind, and he
reached for it — down, in, the way you reach for a word — and there was
nothing there. Not the animal turning over. Not the cold at the wrist. A boy
on a floor with six men and a woman looking at him. He could feel the thing in
him and it was *not coming,* it lay where it lay and did not lift its head, and
he understood with perfect clarity what his father had meant, because he could
feel himself needing it, the need coming up off him like heat, and the thing in
him smelled it, and turned its face to the wall.

*Come on.*

Nothing.

"There," she said, from the dark. "There he is."

She had stepped forward. Into the edge of the lamps. Her hands at her sides,
the belt at her waist, the bow across her back — and her eyes, which were
brown, and which were looking at him, and which were not sliding.

She could see him.

He watched it happen. He watched her face do a thing he had not seen it do in
four days of looking at it — across a counter, across a table, across her own
low table with the candle between them — which was *arrive.* The eyes came to
his face and stayed. They went over it. The mouth, the jaw, the hair down over
the sides, and she looked at the sides, the way everyone looked, and did not
look away, and her mouth came a little open, and she said, so quietly he
almost did not hear it over his own heart:

"Oh."

Just that. Fourteen years, and a coffee shop, and a room under a square, and
a rail, and *oh.*

She stopped where she stood. She had been walking; she stopped. As if the
ground had ended.

And the thing in him lifted its head.

---

It did not come the way the candle had. It came *badly* — that was the only
word he would ever have for it — up off the wet brick of the river way around
his sneakers the way water comes up through a floor, cold, slow, late, not
because he had called it but in spite of the calling, as if it had been going
to come anyway and he had only made it take its time. It pooled at his ankles.
It came up his legs like the flood at the low place, the pull of it, and lay
along his forearms, and he looked down at his own hands and could not see
anything and could feel it like ice all the way to the bone.

His eyes went red.

He did not see it. He would see it later, in a tin mirror, cooling. He felt
it — a change behind the eyes, a pressure, the lamplight going a different
color at the edges — and he saw *her* see it, and saw her face do the second
thing, which was recognize.

"There," she said again. Not to him.

The bow came off her back.

He saw it come. He saw the arrow on the string, black-fletched, the head of
it dark with something that was not blood, and he saw where it was pointed —
not at his chest. At his leg. At the meat of the thigh above the bad knee.
*Slows and sedates.* She did not want him dead. She wanted him held. She had
told him so at the rail with three people on the ground, and she drew the
string back to her cheek with her brown eyes gone to coals and her mouth
still a little open, and Felix stood in his own shadow with his fist full of
beads and could not move his feet.

His father came over the rail.

---

He had not put the knife away.

That was the thing Darneil would remember, in the bed, with the right side of
him gone to stone and his tongue too thick to say it: that he had gone over
the rail with the bone-handled knife open in his hand, the same knife, the one
he had bought on Main Street to use on her and had known the day he bought it
was useless and had carried for twenty years anyway. He went over the iron
with it the way a man goes over a fence into a yard he has been told all his
life is somebody else's — fast, badly, on legs that had not run in fourteen
years — and the quiet came off him at the rail like a door shutting behind
him, and she was there.

She had always been there. Fourteen years, four hundred yards. And he was
past the iron at last, in the dark where she could find him, with a knife she
had once watched him buy, and she did not so much as turn her head.

She was looking at the boy.

She was looking at the boy's *face* — Darneil saw it, saw her seeing it, saw
the thing come into her that he had spent fourteen years and a knot in a
cord keeping out of her — and her bow was up, and the arrow on it was dressed
with the color he knew from the vial on her table, the holding, the new thing,
the one that did not come back, and it was pointed at his son, and his son was
standing in a shadow of his own with his eyes gone the red of hers and could
not move.

Darneil did not decide anything. He was already moving.

He went across the ten feet of wet brick between the rail and the boy the way
he had gone across a flooded street once with a child under his coat — not
running, running was seen, but faster than a man who had not run in fourteen
years had any right to go — and he got there. He got his shoulder in front of
his son. He felt the boy's cold shadow lie against his own arm like a cat and
felt the arrow go in.

The right shoulder. High. A punch, and then a heat, and then — nothing. That
was the holding. Not pain. The *absence* of the arm, all at once, from the
shoulder down, as if somebody had unplugged it, and then the leg on that side
going the same way, and the brick coming up, and his son's cold hand under
his other arm holding him off the floor, and his own voice saying, thick,
already thick, "*Go* —"

---

He did not go.

Felix stood in the river way with his father's weight on his left arm and the
necklace in his right fist and the shadow up around both of them, and he
looked at the woman with the bow, and she was already reaching over her
shoulder for the second arrow, and her shadow came up off the floor to meet
her the way it did, slow, patient, rising, a tide going in —

— and it met his.

He felt it. Shadow on shadow, on the wet brick between them, hers and his,
and they were the *same* — that was the thing nobody had told him, that
Anatole had not known and his father had only guessed — the same shadow, out
of the same blood, come across the same water in the hold of the same boat,
and when hers touched his in the dark it did not fight it. It *hesitated.* It
lay still for the length of a breath the way a dog stops at the sound of its
own name in a stranger's mouth, and in that breath he shoved.

He did not know how. He shoved the way you shove a door with your shoulder —
with the whole cold weight of the thing in him, *at* her — and her shadow went
back a foot along the floor, and her arrow went wide into the brick, and her
face did the third thing, which he would think about for the rest of his
life, which was *flinch.*

"Addie," he said.

The two men had her still. Two of Armand's, holding her by the arms at the
edge of the lamps, where they had stood through all of it with their mouths
open. He looked at her. He looked at the men holding her. And he did the only
other thing he knew how to do, which he had never once done and knew the way
you know your own name — he took the not-being-seen, the thing his mother had
laid over herself in a coffee shop for four days, the small gray *nothing
here* that made an eye slide, and he laid it over Addie.

He felt it go. Like a hand over a candle.

The man on her left arm looked down at what he was holding and there was
nothing in his hand worth holding. Felix watched his fingers open. The man on
her right looked past her, at the rail, at the lamps, with a puzzled face,
and his hand opened too, and Addie Walker, with a scratch on her forearm from
an arrow's dressed head and her mother on the floor and her whole body gone
white, walked out from between two men who could not see her and across ten
feet of brick to Felix and put her hand on his father's chest, and nobody in
the river way looked at her at all.

"They can't —" she said. Her tongue was thick. "Felix. They can't —"

"I know."

"What did you *do* —"

"I don't know."

---

Then the river way filled with people.

They came from behind the six — from the direction of the hall, out of the
dark of the tunnel, with lamps and with no lamps, with net-weights on cords
and a mason's hammer and a child's fists — Ti-Jean first, small and fast and
screaming a name that was not a word, and behind him the four Dauzat girls
who had gone into the cisterns with him, and behind them Odile with her face
bruised and a lamp held high, and behind *her,* thick through the shoulders,
with a rag tied around his head and the blood gone black in his beard and two
fingers of his right hand bent the wrong way, Anatole Bordelon, walking.

"*Mine,*" Anatole said. Not loud. It went down the tunnel anyway. "This is my
river way. Those are my people. *Mine.*"

The six turned.

It broke, then, the way things break. Two of Armand's went down under the
Dauzat girls and the hammer and did not get up. One ran past Felix down the
tunnel into the dark. Armand stood where he was for one second with the
sunglasses on and his brother coming at him with a rag around his head, and
then Armand went — backward, past the woman, into the dark behind the lamps
where the three stretchers lay, with three of his own behind him.

The woman did not run.

She stood at the edge of the lamps with the bow down at her side and her
shadow lying along the floor with the foot of it still a foot back from where
his had shoved it, and she looked at him. At his face. At his eyes, which
were hers, and which were cooling — he could feel them cool, the red going
down out of them the way it went down out of hers, a coal deciding — and her
own were brown again, and she was not slid off, and she would never be again.

"Come to me, baby," she said. "You know the way now."

Then she turned, and went into the dark after Armand, without hurrying, and
the lamps of the three who had gone with him went away down the river way
toward the square and the crack and the room, and the tunnel was full of the
town, and his father was on the brick at his feet with his right side gone.

---

They got him inside the rail.

Felix and Anatole and Odile, the three of them, with Ti-Jean holding the lamp,
across ten feet of brick and over the iron, and the quiet came down on Felix
as he went over it like a hand on the back of the neck, and the shadow went
off him and lay down and was gone, and his eyes were only eyes, and his hands
—

His hands were cold.

He noticed it laying his father on the cot. The left one had a cut across the
palm from the rail, going in over the iron with a man's weight on his arm, and
it was bleeding, and it was *cold,* the way his wrist had been cold in the
tin-mirror room, and the right one, with the necklace still in it, was the
same. Cold to the bone. He opened the fist. The beads lay across his palm,
and the black stone, and the ring, and he looked at them in the lamplight and
did not put them on.

He put them in his jeans pocket.

His father was looking at him from the cot. The right arm lay on the blanket
like a thing that belonged to someone else. The right side of the face had
gone slack. But the eyes were open and they were his father's and they were on
him, and the mouth moved, thick, on the side that still moved, and Felix bent
down to hear.

"She saw you."

"Yes."

"Then it's — " The tongue would not do it. He tried again. "It won't hide you.
From her. Not — not again. I told you."

"You told me."

"You did it anyway."

"Yes."

His father closed his eyes. Opened them. Something happened in the half of his
face that could still do things, and Felix would not have called it a smile,
and it was.

"Good," his father said.

Behind them Addie was on the floor with her mother, holding her mother's head,
and her right hand was not working — he could see her trying to make it close
and it would not — and Janet was breathing in the wrong way with her eye gone
shut and her hand on her side, and Anatole was sitting against the brick with
Odile wrapping his fingers, and Ti-Jean stood in the middle of the crypt with
the lamp and looked at Felix the way he had looked at him in a doorway two
days ago and said nothing at all.

Felix went to the rail.

He stood inside it a moment with his hands on the iron. Then he stepped over.
He stood on the far side of it in the river way with his cold hands at his
sides and his pocket heavy and his father's blood on his sleeve, and looked
down the four hundred yards of dark toward the place where the lamps had gone,
where three people lay on stretchers who were somebody's, where a woman sat
on a floor with her hands open waiting for him to come and be seen again.

*You know the way now.*

He did. He had found another way. It was the only one there had ever been.

Above them all, ninety-four steps and a basement and a lot and a river road
away, an old man with a bad heart and a crowbar came down a stair that nobody
had barred, one step at a time, with a flashlight in his hand and a prayer in
his mouth he had not said in forty years, and did not know yet what he was
coming down to.
