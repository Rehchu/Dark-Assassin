# The Man Who Kept the Vigil

He wound the clock at eight.

He did it every morning at eight by the clock itself, which was the only way
there was to do it, and he had done it five thousand and some mornings running,
and he knew the number because it was on the wall. A brass travel clock with a
folding lid, his father's, that had to be wound each day or it would stop, and
if it stopped there was no morning. There was only the lamp and the damp and
the sound of the river going by somewhere in the stone, and a man could lose a
day in that and then a week, and his father had told him so, and his father had
been right about most things.

So: eight. The key in the back. Twelve turns. The lid folded down.

Then the wall.

He took the charcoal from the shelf — a stub of it, the end of a stick Odile
brought him from the hall's fire a few times a year in a twist of paper — and
he went to the place on the brick where yesterday's mark was, and he made
today's beside it. A short line. Every seventh line was longer. Every thirtieth
had a cross-stroke through it, and every three hundred and sixty-fifth had a
circle around it, and there were fourteen circles on the wall now going up from
the floor in a slow column beside the older column, the one in a different hand,
that ended the year before his began.

Fourteen circles. He put the charcoal back on the shelf.

The lamp was the kind you filled. He filled it. The wick was the kind you
trimmed. He trimmed it, with the knife, the same knife, folding, with a handle
he had bought on Main Street twenty years ago and that had never once been any
use to him for the thing he had bought it for, and that he used now for wicks
and beards and nothing else. He shaved by feel. He had not seen his own face in
fourteen years, except once, in a bowl of water, and had not liked it, and had
not looked again.

Then he sat down on the cot with his back to the brick, and he looked at the
thing on the altar, the way he did every morning, because he was its keeper and
that was what a keeper did.

---

It was not much to look at. That was the first thing anyone thought, and he had
thought it too, the first time, standing where the rail was with his father's
hand on his shoulder.

A grip, wrapped in wire. A crossguard, straight, plain, a little wider than a
hand. Above the crossguard, where a blade should have gone on for three feet,
nothing — a stub of iron the length of a thumb, broken square across, the break
gone black. All of it gone black. Iron that had been in the ground, or in water,
or in a hold like this one under a church, for longer than there had been a
town over its head. The wire on the grip had been silver once and was the color
of pewter now, worn down by hands to a dull shine in the places where a hand
went and left rough everywhere else. It lay on the altar stone on a square of
cloth his father had put there and he had never moved. It fit a hand. He knew
that. It fit a hand exactly, the way a thing does that was made for one.

And it was heavier than it should have been. Every time. Fourteen years, and
every time he picked it up it surprised him, the way a sleeping child
surprises you when you lift it, all that weight in a thing that small.

He did not pick it up this morning. He looked at it.

The crypt was brick, vaulted, low, the mortar sweating in the wet months, with
the altar stone at the far end under the only part of the ceiling a tall man
could stand up straight beneath. A cot. The lamp on a crate. A shelf of books
gone soft as bread, which he had read, all of them, more times than the wall
had circles. A bucket. A jug. The clock. And across the entry, where the crypt
opened on the brick tunnel that ran four hundred yards to the town — the rail.

Iron. Waist-high. A chancel rail that had stood in a chapel once, when there
was a chapel, when there was a sky over it, and had been carried down here by
men whose names were on the older column of the wall, and set across the mouth
of this room, and had never been moved.

Beyond it, the shadow did not come.

He had learned that the way you learn a thing you are betting your life on —
slowly, and then all at once. The first year he had not slept. He had sat on
the cot with the knife open in his hand and watched the tunnel mouth beyond the
rail the way a man watches a door he knows will open, and it had not opened.
Not the first year. Not the second. Nothing came down the river way in the dark
that was not Anatole or Odile with the bread. And in the third year he had
understood, at last, that his father had told him the truth, that the thing on
the altar did what the old men said it did, that within a stone's throw of it
the shadow would not come — not could not, would not, the way a dog will not
go into a room where something has died — and that he was safe in here.

Safe. He had laughed, the first time he thought the word. It had come out of
him like a cough.

He was safe the way a man in a well is safe from rain.

Past the rail she could find him. He did not know how far past — ten feet, a
hundred, the length of the river way — and he had never learned, because
learning would have cost him the thing he had come down here to keep. So he
stayed. He had not put a foot on the far side of that iron in fourteen years.
He had not seen the sky. He had not felt wind. He had learned, from her, in
the years before this room, how to walk through a city unseen, and he had
learned from his father how to keep a lamp lit in a hole in the ground, and he
had used the second thing every day and the first thing never, and he was
forty-six years old.

The boy would be eighteen. In August. He had made a mark for it.

---

The other marks were the ones he did not look at in the morning. He looked at
them at night, when the lamp was low and the clock had been wound and there
was nothing else to do with his eyes.

They went up the brick beside the cot, on the wall where a child would have
stood if there had been a child. A short line, low down, at the height of a
four-year-old's head — he had been sure of that one, he had held that head
against his chest in the rain and knew it to the inch. Above it, a line for
five. He had guessed. He had stood the lamp on the crate and closed his eyes
and tried to feel where a five-year-old's head would come to on him, and made
the mark, and it was probably wrong. Six. Seven. Each one a guess laid on the
guess before it. By ten he had given up pretending they were anything but a
man's wish drawn on a wall, and he had gone on making them anyway, every
August, one a year, until the last one was up near his own shoulder, a short
charcoal line with *18* beside it in his father's kind of handwriting, which
was the only kind he had.

Fourteen lines. A boy going up a wall in the dark, one year at a time, that
had never once stood against it.

Somewhere over his head that boy was real, and had a face, and was eighteen,
and did not know that any of this was under him. That had been the plan. The
plan had been good. He had given fourteen years to it and would give fourteen
more, and if the price of the plan was that the boy would never in his life
stand against this wall and let his father find out how wrong the marks were,
then that was the price, and he had known it in the rain, and had paid it.

He touched the cord at his throat. Her ring was on it. A signet, heavy, old
gold gone soft-edged, with her mark cut into the face of it — the mark that was
on the wax of three letters in a folder somewhere over his head, and on the
paper she had signed the day she took his name. He had taken it off her the
same night he took the charm, the last thing before he lifted the boy, and he
had never known why. To have something. To have one thing of hers that was not
the memory of her eyes going red in the dark.

He wore it the way she had worn the charm. He had never taken it off.

---

Odile came at half past eight with the bread, the way she did, and he heard
her a long way off in the river way — her step, and the lamp she carried, the
light of it coming along the brick before she did — and he stood up, because
a man stood up for Odile.

She stopped at the rail. She did not come through it. She set the bundle on
the post and looked at him, and he saw her face in the lamplight, and he knew
before she said it.

"Anatole's coming," she said. "Himself. He's on the way behind me."

He did not ask. He nodded. She looked at him a moment longer with the look she
had, the one that had never in fourteen years been afraid of him, and then she
went back the way she had come, and he stood at the rail with his hands on the
cold iron and watched the dark where she had gone, and listened.

A man's step. Slow. Anatole's step, which he knew as well as Odile's, and which
did not come to the rail more than three or four times in a year, and had not
come at all since the summer.

The light came first, the way it always did. Then the man behind it — thick
through the shoulders, a beard with the ends singed, the lamplit pallor they all
had down here, patched clothes, clean. He came to the rail and he did not stop
at it. He put his hand on the post and stepped through, the way Odile never did
and he alone ever did, and he stood inside the quiet with Darneil, two feet
away, and set his lamp down on the floor.

"Sit," Darneil said. It came out rusty. He had not said a word since Thursday.
"Mais, sit, Anatole."

"I'll stand." Anatole's French came up under his English the way it did when
he was tired. "I'll say it and then I'll sit."

"Then say it."

Anatole looked at the wall. At the column of circles. At the other column
beside it, the marks going up. Then at Darneil, and his face was the face of a
decent man who has carried a thing four hundred yards in the dark and is about
to set it down on someone who has been waiting fourteen years to have it set
down on him.

"Jan came down Friday night," he said. "Late. Eleven. She came to me in the
hall and she said it to me plain, the way she says things, and she told me to
say it to you the same way, in order, and I'm going to."

Darneil's hand had closed on the rail behind him. He did not know when.

"The boy has the box," Anatole said. "The Knights gave it to him when he moved
out, the way they were told. He opened the envelope Thursday at a table in
town. He has the necklace. He has the papers. He has her letters. He has been
to Alcide, on Friday, and Alcide told him what it is and what it's for, and
told him to keep it on, and sent him to the lot." A breath. "Her girl is with
him. Addie. She said tell Delvaux, and she said: do not present either of
them."

The lamp on the floor hissed.

"That's Friday. Then Saturday." Anatole put his hand flat on his own chest as
if to hold something in it. "Yesterday afternoon, twenty to five, the door at
the top of the bell-stair opened. A hand's width. My watchers were on the
stair, three landings down, in the dark, and they saw the light come through
and heard two voices and one of them was a girl's, and the girl said
*tomorrow.* And then the door shut." He looked at Darneil. "That's today. She
said tomorrow, and it's today, and it's raining like the end of the world up
there, and they're coming down."

Darneil did not move.

He had thought about this for fourteen years, the way a man in a well thinks
about rain. He had thought it would come as a sound — a step in the river way
that was not Odile's, a voice at the rail. He had not thought it would come as
Anatole standing in his lamplight with his hand on his chest saying *he opened
the envelope at a table in town.* A table. In town. The boy had sat at a table
in a town with a sky over it and opened an envelope Darneil had sealed with
this ring, and had taken out the thing Darneil had put on his neck in the rain,
and had held it in his hand, and had not known.

"What's his name," Darneil said.

"Felix. You know that. Felix Knight."

"Say it again."

Anatole said it again.

"He's eighteen," Darneil said. He did not know why he said it. He was looking
at the wall. "In August."

"He's eighteen."

"Is he — " He stopped. He had been going to say *tall,* and it was the
stupidest question a man had ever asked, and he could not ask it, and he could
not not ask it, and he stood there with his mouth open on it and Anatole,
who was a decent man, looked at the lamp on the floor and let him.

"Jan says he's a good boy," Anatole said, after a while, to the lamp. "She's
watched him. She says he's kind and he's scared and he'd rather not know
anything, and the girl pushes, and that's how they've gotten this far. She says
she chose well, with the Knights. She wanted you to know that. She said, tell
him she chose well."

Darneil put the back of his hand against his mouth and held it there until he
could take it away.

"All right," he said. "All right."

---

"She knows," he said.

They had sat, finally, both of them, on the cot, with the lamp between their
feet and the Hilt on its stone at the end of the room where they could both
see it. Anatole had told him the rest. The old man on Main Street, and the
coffee shop on Third Street, and the woman with the black hair who had started
there Thursday and worked the counter Friday and had her mouth at the boy's ear
for the length of a breath while the girl looked away. Jan had not seen it. Jan
had it from Alcide, who had it from the way the boy's face looked when he
walked into the shop. Anatole told it flat. He was a man who told things flat.

"She knows," Darneil said again. "She's felt him since the box left the
Knights' house. That's — that's a week. She's been up top every night for a
week, and she found the coffee shop, and she found the girl, and she stood
across the street from Jan's house — " He stopped. Listened. Nothing in the
river way. "She can't see his face. As long as it's on him she can't see his
face. But she's found the girl and she's found Alcide and she's found the
door, and now she'll wait at the bottom of it."

"She doesn't use the door."

"She doesn't have to. Your brother does."

Anatole was quiet.

"He's been going down to her," Darneil said. "You know it. You've known it a
year. Every time she sends for someone it's Armand who goes, and he goes by
her way and not yours, and he comes back up with sunglasses on his head and a
look on him like a man who's been promised something." He turned his head.
"Four people from up top came down your door in six weeks and were not
presented. Odile told me. They were *taken* to her, by him, and they didn't come
back. That isn't the truce. That's the thing that comes after the truce."

"I know what it is."

"Then you know what he'll do when the boy comes down."

Anatole put his face in his hands. He did it the way a man does who has not
slept, and he stayed like that a moment, and when he took his hands away his
face was the same face and he was still a decent man and it was not going to
be enough.

"The truce says presented," he said. "The third term. Whoever comes down the
door uninvited is presented to her, and she takes some and lets some go. My
grandmother made that term with her mother. It's kept us alive a hundred and
fifty years. If they come down my door and I don't present them, in front of
my people, with Armand standing there — " He shook his head. "I break it. I
break the thing that's between her and forty of us. For two."

"For one," Darneil said. "The girl she'll let go. It's the boy."

"For one, then."

"Anatole." He waited until the man looked at him. "Bring him by the river
way."

"What?"

"When they come down the stair — and they'll come down it, she's a Walker, she
said *tomorrow* with her hand on the ring — your watchers get to them before
Armand's do. You take them to the hall, because that's where you take people,
and you feed them, because that's what you do, and then you do not present
them. You bring the boy here. By the river way, to the rail, to me." He heard
his own voice and did not recognize it; it had a thing in it that had not been
in it for fourteen years. "I'll answer for the truce."

"You can't answer for it. You can't leave this room."

"I can answer for it from this room. She knows where I am. She's always known.
Let her come to the rail and say it to me." He looked at the Hilt on its stone.
"Let her stand at that iron and tell me she wants my son, and see how far she
gets."

Anatole looked at the Hilt too. He had never touched it. None of them had.
They fed the man who kept it and they did not ask.

"Armand won't hold to that," he said.

"Then you'll have to."

It sat between them with the lamp.

"I'll go up first," Anatole said finally. He stood. He picked up the lamp.
"Now. I'll go up the stair with my own people and I'll be at the bottom of it
when the door opens, and whoever comes down comes to me. Ours. Not his. To the
hall." He stepped to the rail and stopped with his hand on the post, inside
the quiet still, and did not look back. "And then I'll do what I can. I'll do
what I can, Delvaux. That's all I've got. I've never had more than that."

"I know."

"Say it to Jan, if you see her before I do. That I did what I could."

"I'll say it."

He went through the rail. The light went with him along the brick, and his
step, slow, and then the dark came back to the river way, and the sound of the
river in the stone, and the lamp on the crate, and the clock.

---

Darneil stood a long time at the rail after he had gone.

Then he did a thing he had not done in a year. He went to the altar and he
picked up the Hilt.

It was heavier than it should have been. It always was. It fit his hand
exactly, the wire rough on the sides and smooth where his hand went, and the
weight of it went up his arm and into his shoulder and settled there like
something that had been waiting, and the room was very quiet. He stood with it.
He looked at the rail, and the dark beyond the rail, and the four hundred yards
of brick beyond that, and the town, and the stair, and the door at the top of
it with the rain coming down on it, and a boy on the other side of the door
with a girl beside him who pushed.

*I will find a way to be a man you'd have been proud of.*

He had said it in the rain to a four-year-old who had not understood a word.
He had meant it. He had not, in fourteen years, found the way, and he had come
to think there was not one, that the way was this room and the marks on the
wall and a lamp kept lit, and that a boy who never knew was the whole of what
he had to give.

The boy was coming down.

He did not put the Hilt back on the stone. He stood at the rail with it in his
hand, inside the quiet, on the right side of the iron, and he did not sit, and
he did not sleep, and the clock went on toward eleven, and he listened to the
river way for a step that was not Odile's, and was not Anatole's, and had never
once in fourteen years come.
