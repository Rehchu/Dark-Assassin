# Prelude: The Quiet Room

There was a room under the city that no map had ever held.

It sat below the water table, below the old brick sewers the parish had
forgotten, below the buried streets of a town that had been standing here when
Alexandria was still a word nobody in this part of the world had said out loud.
The people who lived up top walked over it every day. They parked their cars on
it. They poured their coffee and lost their tempers and fell in love on a crust
of concrete and river mud that was, if you went down far enough, only a lid.

The woman who kept the room did not think of it as under anything. To her it was
simply where she lived, the way a fish does not think of itself as underwater.

It was dark, and she liked it dark. One lamp burned, and it burned low, a fat
candle in a dish of black glass that threw more shadow than light. The walls
were hung with cloth so old the color had left it, gray on gray on gray, and the
cloth swallowed sound the way the dark swallowed the corners. When she moved,
the cloth moved. When she was still, the whole room was still, and a person
standing in the doorway would have needed a long moment to find her in it, and
by then it would have been too late.

She was not, tonight, in any hurry to be found.

She sat at a low table with her hands open on the wood, palms up, the way you
might sit if you were waiting for something to be placed in them. On the table
were the tools of her evening. A stoppered vial the color of tea. A folding
knife with a bone handle worn smooth by a hundred years of thumbs that were not
hers. A length of cord. A small brass box that she did not open, because she
knew what was in it, and looking would not change it.

She was old and she was not old. Her face in the candlelight was a woman's face
in its prime, unlined, the brown of it warm and even, the bones good under it.
Her hair was black, so black it took the light and gave nothing back, and it
lay against her neck like something poured. Only her hands told the truth, and
even they told it quietly — the knuckles a shade heavier than the rest of her,
the veins standing up under the skin like roots that had grown too close to the
surface. She was older than her mother had been when her mother did this same
sitting, at this same table, in a room that had been darker still.

The bloodline went back further than the room. Further than the buried town.
It went back to a coast on the other side of an ocean and a queen who was not
called a queen, and it had come across the water in the hold of a ship, in a
woman's blood and in the small cruel knowledge she carried behind her teeth,
and it had walked up out of the swamp into this parish and it had never once,
in all those generations, let its light be seen by anyone it did not intend to
kill. That was the whole art of it. Not the poison. Not the shadow. Anyone with
patience could learn a poison. The art was the not-being-seen. The art was being
a quiet thing in a loud room, and staying quiet until the room was yours.

She closed her hands.

When she opened them again her eyes had changed.

It was a small change and it was the only change that mattered. The candle did
not flicker. The cloth did not stir. But the whites of her eyes had gone, and
in their place was a red so deep it was nearly black, the red at the center of
a coal when you have stopped feeding the fire and it is deciding whether to
die. The light in the room did not reach that red. Nothing reached it. It was
its own thing, lit from somewhere the lamp could not answer for, and when she
looked down at her own open hands the red laid a color on them that was not the
candle's color at all.

The shadow came up off the floor to meet her.

It did not rush. That was the thing people never understood, the few who lived
long enough to understand anything about it — they thought of shadow as a fast
thing, a struck-match thing, and it was not. It was slow. It was patient the way
water is patient. It came up around the legs of the table and pooled at her
wrists and lay along her forearms like a cat that had decided, after long
consideration, that she would do. She let it. She had spent forty years learning
to let it, learning the exact difference between calling a thing and needing it,
and the shadow could smell need on a person the way a dog could, and it did not
come for the ones who needed it. It came for the ones who could take it or leave
it. She could always leave it. That was why it never left her.

She sat like that until the candle had burned down a finger's width, her red
eyes open on nothing, the shadow breathing with her. Then she reached out with
one dark-laid hand and closed the brass box's latch, which was already closed,
which she had not opened, and she said a name.

She said it very quietly. The cloth ate it before it reached the walls.

It was not a name anyone up top would have known. It was not a name she had said
out loud in fourteen years, and saying it now cost her something, and she paid
it the way she paid for everything, without a sound and without a change in her
face. She had been looking for the thing that name belonged to for fourteen
years. She had looked in every way there was to look, and there were ways to
look that no one up top would have believed, and every one of them had come back
empty, and she had come at last to understand that empty was itself an answer.
You did not fail to find a thing this many times. Not her. She had never in her
life failed to find what she was hunting. If the world had gone silent where
that small light should have been, then someone had made it silent on purpose,
and had done it well, and had done it with the one craft in all the world that
could hide a thing from her.

Somebody had put out her light and hidden it in a bushel.

She had thought, for a long time, that this meant it was dead. That was the
kind of thought a person had at the low table, in the low light, with the red
in her eyes and nothing to spend it on. But she did not believe it anymore. A
dead light did not need hiding. You did not spend a craft that rare on a corpse.
The very fact of the silence was the proof against it. Whoever had done this had
done it because there was still something to hide, and that meant the light was
out there, somewhere over her head, walking around in the noise and the sun,
covered so well that even she, sitting in the one room in the world built to
find it, could not feel so much as its warmth.

She would feel it eventually. She had time. Time was the one thing the bloodline
had always had more of than it needed. But she was not a patient woman by
nature, whatever the shadow thought of her, and the not-finding had worn a place
in her the way a river wears a stone, and on the nights when the place ached she
did the only thing that had ever quieted it.

She went up top. And she practiced.

---

The overworld came into her by degrees, the way cold water does when you make
yourself walk into it instead of jumping.

She did not use the wooden door. The wooden door was for the ones who lived in
the ruins between her floor and the surface, the leader and his brother and
their small frightened court, and she did not care to be seen using anything
they used. She had her own ways up. They were older than the door. Some of them
were older than the town on top and the buried town below it both, cracks in
the world that had been cracks before anyone thought to build over them, and she
went up through the dark of them without a light, her hand on wet stone, the
shadow gone quiet and close around her like a coat, until the stone gave way to
brick and the brick gave way to a grating and the grating gave way, at last, to
air that moved.

She came out in an alley off a street she had no name for and no need of one.

It was late. It was the good hour, the hour when the bars had let their people
out and the people had scattered and the ones who were left on the street were
the ones who should not have been — a man too drunk to steer himself, a woman
crying into a phone against a brick wall, a boy waiting for a ride that the
tightness in his shoulders said was not coming. The town breathed around her,
warm and wet, the river close enough to smell, the neon of a gas station two
blocks over laying a green wash on the low clouds. Somewhere a dog was barking
at nothing. Somewhere a train was pulling long and slow through a crossing, its
horn stretched out and lonesome, and under all of it the frogs and the bugs kept
up the huge dumb noise the low country made every night of the world.

She loved it. She would not have said so — she did not say much of anything, and
love was a word she had let go of a long time before she let go of the name in
the brass box — but she loved it, the loud warm careless surface of it, the way
it never once looked down. She stood in the mouth of the alley with the shadow
lying flat and thin around her feet and she let the town not see her, and the
not-being-seen filled the ached place in her the way water fills a footprint.

Then she chose.

She did not choose the way people up top imagined it, if they imagined it at
all — and they did, she knew, in the days after; they sat in their kitchens with
the paper open to the story and they told each other it could have been anybody,
it was random, there was no rhyme to it, and they were right, and being right
did not help them. She did not choose the drunk man, though he was the easiest.
She did not choose the crying woman, though grief made a person slow. She chose
the boy, the one waiting for the ride, because the tightness in his shoulders
said he had been let down before and expected to be let down again, and a person
like that had already done half her work for her. He would not fight. He would
only think, when it started, that here it was, the thing he had always known was
coming, and he would be almost grateful for the proof.

She crossed the street. She did not hurry. Hurry was seen.

He heard her too late, the way they all did — not because she was silent, she
was not silent, her shoes made a sound on the pavement like anyone's — but
because the part of him that should have turned had already been told, by the
shadow that reached him a half-second before she did, that there was nothing
behind him worth turning for. That was the poison she was proudest of, and it
was not in the vial. It was the shadow's own lie, the small gray suggestion it
laid over a person that everything was fine, that the street was empty, that the
cold spot at the back of the neck was only the night coming on. He believed it.
They believed it right up until they couldn't, and by then her hand was on his
shoulder, gentle, almost kind, turning him the last little way she needed him
turned.

His eyes found her eyes.

She let him see them. That was the only mercy in her, and it was not a mercy she
gave for his sake. She gave it for her own, because in the half-instant before
the shadow took the light out of a person there was a thing that happened in
their face, a thing she had been chasing for forty years and had never once
gotten enough of — the moment the surface broke and the person underneath the
person looked out, unhidden, the way no one was ever unhidden up here in the
noise. He looked out at her. He saw the red. He understood, in the wordless way
the dying understand, that the story he had told himself about being let down
had always, from the beginning, been about this. And then the shadow closed over
him the way still water closes over a dropped stone, without a splash, without a
ring, and the boy who had been waiting for a ride sat down slowly against the
wall as though he had only decided to rest, and the vial in her pocket stayed
stoppered, because she had not needed it, because she almost never needed it,
because the poison was the thing she used on the ones she wanted to suffer and
this boy had suffered enough.

She stood over him for a moment. The frogs kept on. The train had passed. Down
the block the gas station buzzed its green buzz, and a moth beat itself against
the light there, again and again, in love with the thing that would kill it if
it ever got what it wanted.

They would find him in the morning. They would call it random. There would be no
wound to speak of, nothing taken, no reason — a healthy boy dead against a wall
with a look on his face the coroner would write down as *consistent with cardiac
event* because there was no other box to put it in. The police would work it a
week and shelve it. The paper would run it small. And somewhere in the parish a
mother would learn that the world was the kind of place where a thing like this
simply happened, for no reason, to anyone, and she would be right, and being
right would not help her either.

The Mistress felt the ache go quiet.

It was not gone. It never went. But it had loosened its grip, the way it always
did, enough to get her back down into the dark and through another handful of
the long empty days, and that was all she asked of a night like this. She turned
from the boy without looking at him again. She was already thinking about the
name in the brass box, the small light hidden somewhere over her head under a
craft even she could not see through, and she was thinking, as she walked back
toward the crack in the world that would take her home, a thing she had thought
a thousand nights and would think a thousand more.

Somebody had hidden it from her. Somebody clever. Somebody who had loved it as
much as she did.

That was fine. She had all the time there was, and she was very good at what she
did, and no charm ever made held forever. One day the craft would slip, or the
one who wore it would grow careless the way the young always did, and for one
breath the light would show — one breath, one flicker, one warm place in all
the cold miles of the parish — and she would feel it, and she would go to it,
and she would take back what was hers.

She went down into the dark to wait.

Above her, unknowing, the city rolled over in its sleep, and did not feel the
weight go off its chest, and did not know it had ever been there.
