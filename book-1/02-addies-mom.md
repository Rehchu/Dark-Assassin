# Addie's Mom

The helmet was on the hook inside her bedroom door where it always was, and the
keys were in the dish on the dresser where they always were, and Addie had both
of them in her hands and was back on the landing inside of thirty seconds, which
was about how long she could stand to be alone in her room right then with the
face she had just seen in the kitchen window.

She took the stairs two at a time. She always did. The house had a sound for it,
a hollow double-thump the whole way down that her mother had given up on years
ago, and tonight she made the sound on purpose, because a normal night sounded
like that, and she wanted this to be one.

Halfway down she heard her mother in the hall.

Not talking. Just there. Her mother could stand in a room and you would know it
the way you knew a radio was on in another part of the house, and Addie knew it
now, three steps from the bottom, and she did the only thing there was time to
do. She put the cheer on. The loose bounce, the smile, the *hey.* A woman who
read faces for a living was standing at the foot of the stairs, and Addie did
not have time for the face she had just seen in the window over the sink.

Addie headed down the stairs when she was immediately stopped by her mom, Janet.

Her mother was standing where the hall opened into the front room, with the
blinds of the front window behind her. Addie's own build. Addie's own coloring,
gone gray at the temples in a way her mother refused to do anything about.
Reading glasses on a cord around her neck. The gray cardigan. Her phone in one
hand, dark, the way she held it when she was not looking at it and was not
going to put it down either.

She was off tonight. Addie registered that the way she registered weather. Her
mother worked twelve-hour nights at parish dispatch, four on and three off, and
on an off night the television was on in the front room whether anyone was
watching it or not. It was not on. The house was quiet enough to hear the
refrigerator.

"Where do you think you're going in such a cheerful mood?" Asks her mom.

*Cheerful.* Addie heard the word land and knew exactly what it was. Her mother
was not falling for it. Her mother was naming it. That was a thing she did, and
she did it in a voice so flat you could go a whole day without noticing you had
been caught.

Addie kept the act up anyway. It was the only one she had.

"I'm headed to meet up with Felix at Ugly Mug, and talk about some news he
recently found out." Replies Addie.

"What news."

It was not a question the way her mother said it. It was the dispatch voice, the
one that got flatter the more something mattered, and Addie was already turned
half toward the door, and she gave it in the fewest words she could find.

"He was unpacking the last box from his parents. Old stuff. He found papers."
She shifted the helmet to her other hand. "Adoption papers. He thinks they're
his."

She watched her mother's face while she said it, because she could not help
watching her mother's face, and it did nothing. Not surprise. Not *the Knights
would never.* Not the little inward pull at the corner of the mouth that meant
her mother had an opinion and was keeping it. Nothing at all, the way a still
pond does nothing.

"Huh," Janet said.

Then: "Papers."

Then nothing.

Addie filed it where she filed most things her mother did, under her mother
being her mother, and felt the other thing come up behind it — *you mentioned
that place once, you said the word* orphanage *in this kitchen and I was
fourteen and I remember it* — and she did not ask. Not now. Later. She had told
herself *later* in the garden not five minutes ago and she told herself *later*
again now, and it went down easier the second time, the way things do.

"I won't be long! I'm just going to be gone enough to talk this through, then
I'll be back before the sun sets."

"The sun set an hour ago," her mother said.

Addie looked at the front door. The glass in the top half of it was black. Not
blue, not the gray-blue it should have been at ten to six on a fall evening, but
black, the porch light burning a hole in it and nothing behind the hole at all.
It was true. She had known it was true in the garden and she had said it anyway,
because it was what she always said, and she found she did not want to take it
back.

"Then before it comes up," she said.

Her mother let that go too. She let a lot go. It was one of the things Addie
loved about her and one of the things that made her, some nights, want to shake
her by the cardigan until something fell out.

"While you're out then, can you pick up some sage, butter, and honey?"

"Sage?"

Addie was thinking cooking. Thanksgiving was a week off and her mother did the
whole thing herself, start to finish, and started earlier every year. "Are you
starting *now?*"

"For the house," her mother said, and did not say what a house did with sage.
"The fresh kind. Not the jar. The bunches, in produce. Get two." A beat. "Real
butter. And the local honey, the one in the jar. Not the bear."

It was a lot of instruction for three things. Addie did not say so. She was
counting the minutes to Third Street in her head and the count was already
wrong.

"I'll give you a few dollars to pick it all up, and you can use what's left to
buy you something to drink." Says Janet.

Her mother's hand went into the cardigan pocket and came out with the money
already folded — a twenty and a ten, creased down the middle together, the way
you fold a bill you have been holding for a while and have decided on. Not
counted out. Not fished for. Already there. Addie noticed that the way you
notice a picture hanging a half-inch crooked, and put it back straight in her
head, and did not think about it.

"Be careful, there have been a string of missing persons cases lately."

"You always say be careful."

"I don't always say that."

As she said it her eyes went past Addie. Past her shoulder, to the front window,
to the blinds and whatever was on the other side of the blinds, and came back.
It took less than a second. Addie had spent eighteen years learning to read that
face and she read it now and got nothing she could use.

"What do you mean, a string?"

Her mother gave it to her the way she would have given it to a unit in the
field. In order. No adjectives.

"Four in six weeks. Grown people. Not the kind that wander off. None of them on
the news yet." She looked at the phone in her hand and back up. "Text me when
you get there."

"It's a coffee shop, Mama. It's not a war."

"Text me."

Addie grabs the cash from her mom, gives her a hug, and rushes out the door in
an instant.

The hug was the thing she would think about later, when she had time to think
about things. Her mother held on for half a beat longer than a hug for a coffee
run needed. One hand flat between Addie's shoulder blades, pressing, the way you
press a stamp down to make sure it takes. Addie felt it and was already out the
door, and the door was already closing behind her, and she was on the porch in
the cold.

It hit her all at once. The first real cold of the year, the smell of it — wet
leaves and somebody's woodsmoke from down the block and under that the river,
which you could smell from anywhere in this town if the wind was right. The
porch light made a yellow circle and the yard outside it was dark. The bike was
a darker shape in the drive.

Keys. Helmet under her arm. The folded money went into her left jacket pocket,
the one with the zipper, and she zipped it.

As she makes her way to her bike, she notices someone across the street.

A person. Standing on the far sidewalk, between the two streetlights, exactly
where the light of one gave out and the light of the other had not yet begun.
Tall. Facing the house. Not walking. Not smoking, not looking at a phone, not
waiting for a dog to finish. Just standing, and it was the standing that Addie
saw before she saw anything else — the stillness of it, the way you notice a
thing in a room that is not moving when everything else is.

There was no car on the street she did not know. There was no face. The dark
and the distance did not give her one; she got a shape and a height and a set to
the shoulders, and something about the height, or the way the person stood,
rang a bell somewhere far back in her that she could not find the rope of.

She did not think *man.* She did not think *woman.* She did not think anything
yet.

She gets this gut wrenched feeling, almost like she is going to be sick.

It came up under her breastbone like a cold hand turning over. Her mouth went
wet. It was the exact feeling of the second before you are sick on a boat, when
the horizon tilts and your body decides something without you. Her hand found
the seat of the bike and held on to it. She looked down at her own boots on the
concrete and let it pass, one breath, two, the way she had learned to do with
the flu when she was nine.

She looks back across the street, and in that moment, they were gone.

Gone how, she could not have said. There were no footsteps. There was no car
door, no gate, no shape moving off along the sidewalk toward the corner. The
space between the streetlights was only space, a strip of dark sidewalk and a
hedge and the front of the Guidrys' house with one lamp on upstairs. She waited
a breath. Two yards down a dog started up, three barks, and stopped as if
somebody had put a hand on it.

Behind her, in the house, she heard the blinds of the front window move and go
still.

Her mother, looking out.

Addie did not turn around to check. She knew that sound the way she knew the
sound of her own name.

Addie was in a rush to meet up with Felix, so she didn't take much note of it.

Helmet on. Strap. Keys in the ignition and the little green light. The sickness
drained out of her as fast as it had come, all at once, like water out of a
sink, and she took that as proof of what it was — nothing, no dinner, the paper
she had finished an hour ago still sitting in her chest, and a drink she had not
had yet. Bodies did things. Hers was doing one.

She jumped on her bike, revved the engine, and before you know it, she went on
her way.

Jackson Street took her in the way it always did, like a river you have swum
in your whole life. She had come off her own street fast, over the hump of the
railroad crossing two blocks down without slowing, the way she was not supposed
to — the crossing lights dark, the culvert under the tracks a black mouth she
had passed ten thousand times and never once looked into — and Jackson Street
Extension had turned into Jackson Street the way it did, without telling you,
and the houses had gotten older and closer together and then there was traffic.

Addie was feeling ambitious.

She decided to zip in and out of lanes, as she loved performing fearless lane
splitting stunts, dodging every car that she came up on.

Tail lights. A pickup in the right lane doing thirty with its blinker on and no
intention of turning. A city bus in the left, lit up inside and empty. The gap
between them was a gap because she decided it was, and she took it, and the
pickup's horn came late and small behind her and she was already through. The
cold poured in at the collar and the cuffs and the gap where her jacket did not
quite meet her jeans, and she leaned into it, and she let it.

*This is the part I'm good at,* she thought, the small clear part of her that
never stopped narrating, the part that had said *the last ordinary night* at the
kitchen window and would not take it back. *Not the paper. Not the phone. This.*

MacArthur went by underneath her in a wash of sodium orange. Then the light
changed, the way it did when you came into downtown — the streetlights older
and closer and a different color, the buildings brick and low, the grid taking
you in. She could see the levee at the end of every cross street, a long dark
shoulder against a darker sky. Third Street. She checked her mirror once, at the
light, for a tall shape, for anything, and saw headlights, which was all anybody
ever saw in a mirror.

She could see it in the horizon, the coffee shop.

Ugly Mug from a block off was the brightest thing on Third Street. The big
front windows lit gold from inside, string lights along the top of the glass,
the pastry case glowing like a jewelry counter, a dozen little tables and people
at half of them with their coats still on. And outside, on the sidewalk under
the front window, standing in the light it threw, a short heavy boy in a hoodie
with his hair down over his ears, a folder clamped flat under one arm and a cup
in each hand.

Standing still.

Felix was never still. He talked with his hands and he walked when he thought
and he could not sit through a movie without getting up twice. He was standing
under the window like a person waiting for a bus that had already come, and
that, more than anything her mother had said, more than the sidewalk across from
her house, put the cold hand back under her ribs for one second before she got
it out of there.

She pulled in at the curb in front of him and killed the engine.

Upon arrival, Addie takes her helmet off, and runs up to Felix.

"I ordered for you already." He said as Addie walked up.

"Iced white chocolate mocha with an extra shot, and I made sure they didn't put
any whipped cream on it."

"Iced," she said. "In this."

"It's what you get."

It was the first thing he had said all night that sounded like Felix. She heard
it and something in her let go a notch. She looked at him under the window
light, really looked, the way she had not been able to over the phone. The face
from the call was still there, held together on purpose, every part of it doing
its job. His eyes went to the folder under his arm and away, and back, the way
your hand goes to a pocket to make sure a thing is still in it. He was holding
it against his ribs like something that might get away from him if he let it.

Neither of them said the word *papers* on the sidewalk. She did not touch the
folder. She did not ask to see it. There was a table for that, and a door, and a
minute, and she was not going to spend them out here in the cold.

Felix hands the drink to Addie, and she takes that first sip and savors the
moment as if it's the last time she'll ever have a drink.

It was perfect. It was cold enough to hurt and exactly sweet enough and he had
gotten the extra shot right, and for the length of that sip the paper and the
phone and the sidewalk across from her house were somewhere else, and there was
only this, a drink she liked, made right, handed to her by a boy who knew how
she took it.

She held on to that. Thirty seconds. She let herself have thirty seconds of it.

Through the glass beside them she could see the counter. A bearded man in a
black apron, the manager, glancing at the clock over the espresso machine and
then at the door, the way you look at a door when somebody is late. A girl on
the machine, ponytail, steaming milk. Nobody else back there. Tables, coats,
somebody laughing at something on a phone.

From the alley mouth beside the building, off to her left, came a sound — a
metal lid set down on metal, once, and then nothing.

She looked. The alley was a dark slot between the coffee shop and the building
next to it, a dumpster, a wet gleam of something on the ground, no one.

Felix did not look. He had not heard it, or he had and it was a lid on a
dumpster, which was what it was.

Her phone buzzed against her hip. She got it out one-handed, the drink in the
other, and it was her mother, two words, no punctuation: *text me.*

She thumbed *here* and sent it and put the phone away.

"Come on," Felix said. He shifted the folder under his arm and did not look at
it this time. "Before I lose my nerve."

She went in first. Helmet under her arm, the drink in her hand, the twenty and
the ten folded in her left pocket against her hip. The door had a bell on it,
and the bell rang, and the warm came out to meet her — coffee and sugar and
milk and the heat of a room with too many people in it — and she walked into
it like walking into a lit house out of the dark, which was what it was.
