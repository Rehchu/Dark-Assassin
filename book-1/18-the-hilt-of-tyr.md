# The Hilt of Tyr

He heard them at a quarter past one by the clock.

Not Odile's step. Not Anatole's alone. Four sets of feet in the river way,
far off, where the brick tunnel bent at the cistern outfall and the sound of
anything coming along it reached the rail a full minute before the light did
— and he had been standing at the rail since the boy went to her and came back
from her, since Ti-Jean ran to say *she let them go,* since he had sat down
for the first time in a night and a day and then stood up again because sitting
was not a thing his body would do. He had the Hilt in his hand. He had not put
it down since Sunday morning. His arm had stopped feeling the weight of it
the way an arm stops feeling a thing it has decided to carry.

Four sets of feet. Anatole's, slow. A woman's, quick and even, a step he had
last heard on a wet porch fourteen years ago going away from him. A second
woman's, lighter, uneven — a girl favoring a wrist. And the fourth.

He listened to the fourth.

It limped. It came heavy on one side and light on the other and it kept up
with the rest anyway, because the girl beside it would not let it fall behind,
he could hear that too, the way the two steps stayed together on the wet brick
the way two steps stay together when one person has a hand on the other. A
young man's weight. Not a child's. He had held a child against his chest in
the rain and this was not that weight, this was a man's, coming along four
hundred yards of dark toward an iron rail on a bad knee, and Darneil Delvaux
stood behind the rail with his father's father's father's charge in his fist
and found that he could not breathe and did not need to.

The light came round the bend.

Anatole's lamp, held high, the way Anatole held it. Then the shapes behind it,
three, coming out of the dark into the yellow the way people come up out of
water — a woman in a gray coat with her hair gone gray at the temples, walking
like she owned the tunnel, which she did; a girl with copper hair in a rain
shell with a pack on her back and her arm through the arm of —

Of a boy. Short. Heavy through the shoulders. A hoodie, and a bandage on one
knee through the torn jeans, and long brown hair down over the sides of his
face, and a hand up at his collar, flat, over a place where a cord went under.

His face.

Darneil had made fourteen marks on a wall for a face he had never once been
able to picture, and here it was, coming along the brick in a lamp's light with
its mouth a little open and its eyes — green, they were green, she had said
they would be brown and they were green — going past Anatole and past the rail
and finding the man behind it.

They found him. They stayed. The boy stopped walking.

*Tall,* Darneil had wanted to ask, on Sunday, and could not. He was not tall.
He came up to about the fourteenth mark on the wall, the one Darneil had made
in August with *18* beside it, and then a hand's width short of it, and Darneil
stood behind the rail and looked at his son and thought, with the small part
of his mind that still did arithmetic, *I guessed wrong by a hand,* and could
not stop thinking it, and could not say anything at all.

It was Jan who spoke.

"Delvaux," she said, from beside the lamp. The flat voice. Fourteen years and
it had not changed. "I said I'd never come to the rail."

"You said that."

"I've brought you your boy."

---

Felix looked at his father's hands first.

He would not have been able to say why. Later, when Addie asked him — because
she had seen it, she had seen him do it, she saw everything — he would say that
he had been looking at the Hilt, at the thing in the man's fist, and that his
eyes had gotten as far as the fist and stopped. That was true. It was not the
reason.

The reason was that he was afraid of the face, and the hands were a place to
stand.

They were big. Bigger than his own, and he had big hands for a short man; long
in the finger, knuckly, the nails cut with a knife. Gray-brown with lamp-dirt
in the lines of them. And across the knuckles of the right one, where it held
the black iron thing against the rail — scars. Old ones. White. Four of them in
a row, one on each knuckle, the kind you got from skinning a hand on something
hard and not letting anyone look at it after.

*Skinned his knuckles bloody on the iron.*

He did not know where the thought came from. He had never heard the words. But
they were there, in Diane's voice, in the voice of a man in the rain that
Diane had never been able to describe — *your father says it was raining like
the end of the world* — and Felix looked at the four white scars on the hand
that had carried him through it, and then, because there was nothing else left
to look at, he looked up.

The man was gaunt. That was the word. Not thin — gaunt, the bones of the face
standing up under skin that had not seen sun in fourteen years, a beard cut
close and badly, black hair gone gray in one streak at the temple, and eyes
that had squinted so long at a lamp they had forgotten how to open all the way.
Forty-six. He looked sixty. He looked like a man who had been kept in a hole,
and he was standing behind a rail with a piece of a sword in his hand looking
at Felix the way Felix had never in his life been looked at by anyone — not by
the Knights, not by Addie, not by a woman on a floor with her hands open — as
if Felix were the only thing in the world that had ever been worth looking at,
and it hurt to do it, and he was going to do it anyway.

"Hey," Felix said.

It was the stupidest thing he had ever said. It came out of him like a
hiccup. Beside him Addie made a sound that was almost a laugh and was not.

"Hey," his father said.

His voice was rust. It had a formal thing in it, an old thing, the voice of a
man who had learned to speak in one place and had not spoken in another for
long enough to forget which. He stopped, after the one word, and his head
turned, a little — listening, Felix realized, listening to the tunnel behind
them, the way a dog listens — and then came back.

"You're Felix."

"Yes."

"You're — you're eighteen. In August."

"Yes."

"I made a mark for it." The man's eyes went to the wall behind him and came
back, fast, as if he had not meant to let them go. "I've made a mark for all
of them. I guessed wrong. I guessed you'd be — " He stopped. He put the back
of his free hand against his mouth, hard, and held it there, and Felix watched
a grown man stand behind a rail and not be able to finish a sentence, and
something in his own chest that had been clenched since the pawn shop let go a
notch, and it hurt worse than the clenching had.

"Come in," his father said, when he could. "Come inside the rail. All of you.
It's — nothing comes in here. Come in."

---

The rail was cold under his hand and he went over it the way you go over a
fence into a yard you have been told all your life is somebody else's.

Inside was a room. He had not been able to see that from the tunnel. A crypt,
brick, vaulted, low enough that Anatole ducked, with a cot and a lamp on a
crate and a shelf of books gone soft and a clock — a brass clock, folded, on
the shelf — and at the far end, under the one high place in the ceiling, a
stone. An altar. Plain, square, the top of it worn smooth, with a square of
cloth on it and nothing on the cloth.

His father laid the black iron thing down on the cloth.

He did it slowly. Both hands. The way Addie set down a plant she had just
repotted, the way you set down a thing that is heavier than it looks and that
you have been carrying long enough to have stopped feeling. It made no sound
on the stone. It lay there in the lamplight, a grip wrapped in wire gone the
color of pewter, a crossguard, a stub of iron broken square, black all the
way through, and nothing else, no blade, and Felix looked at it and did not
feel anything, and then did.

The quiet.

He had felt it at the rail and not known what it was. He knew it now. It was
the feeling of the tin-mirror room with the candle straight — of the thing
behind his breastbone lying still, *all* the way still, stiller than it had
ever been, not asleep, not turned over, but simply not there, the way a sound
you have lived beside your whole life is not there the morning it stops. He
had carried a thing inside him for eighteen years and had felt it move once,
last night, and now, in this room, ten feet from a piece of black iron on a
stone, it was gone, and the absence of it was so complete that he put his hand
flat on his own chest to make sure he was still in there.

"You feel it," his father said. He was watching him. "Good. That's what it's
for."

"What *is* it."

"Sit down and I'll tell you what I know, which isn't much, and what my father
told me, which is less, and what the old men told him, which is a story." His
father sat on the cot. He put his hands on his knees. "Sit. All of you. Jan.
Anatole, if you're staying."

"I'm staying till four," Anatole said. "Then I've got a hall."

They sat. Felix on the crate by the lamp with his knee out. Addie on the floor
against the brick with the pack in her lap and her mother beside her, not
touching, a hand's width of air between them that neither of them closed. His
father looked at the four of them in his room and something went across his
face like a man looking at a table set for guests he had stopped expecting.

"It's a hilt," he said. "You can see that. The grip and the guard of a sword
and the stub of the blade, broken off, a long time ago, somewhere across the
water. The French carried it. That's the part everybody agrees on — that it
came over with the French when they came up the river and built the post at
the rapids, and that they thought it was a treasure, and that they lost it,
the way people lose things when a town burns twice and floods forty times. It
was in the ground under the chapel for a hundred years. Then the men who kept
this chapel found it, and found out what it did, and kept it. The protectors,
they called themselves. Of the light. My father was one. His father. Back to
the chapel."

"What does it do," Addie said.

"Nothing." His father almost smiled. "To you. To Anatole. To Jan. It's a piece
of iron. You could hit a man with it. But to the blood — " He looked at Felix.
"To her, and to you, and to me, a little, because I lived close to her a long
time and some of it gets on you — within a stone's throw of it, the shadow
won't come. Not can't. *Won't.* It leaves a room this is in the way a dog leaves
a room. That's the rail. That's why she's never once in fourteen years come up
that tunnel and put her hand on my shoulder, and she knows exactly where I am.
It's the one thing in the world she can't come near."

"And held?"

The room was quiet.

"Held," his father said, "by one of the blood, long enough — it binds. The
shadow in them goes still. Sleeps. Doesn't wake." He looked at the Hilt on its
cloth. "The old men said Tyr gave his hand to bind the wolf. That's what they
said. It's a story. I don't know if it's a true story. I know that if she ever
closed her hand on that, the thing she carries would die in her, and she'd be
a woman with old hands, and she'd rather be dead. And I know it's the only
thing in the parish she wants worse than she wants you."

Felix looked at the Hilt.

"It's one piece," his father said. "Of three. The old men said that too. Where
the others are, nobody in this room knows, and I've had fourteen years to
think about it and I don't. One piece holds one room. That's what we've got."

---

"The necklace," his father said. "May I see it."

Felix's hand was at his collar before the sentence was done.

"Not off. Don't take it off. Just — hold it out. Let me see the cord."

He drew it up out of his shirt. The beads came up through his hair the way
they always did and he held the loop out from his throat in his fist, the
stone hanging, the cord across his knuckles, and his father leaned in from the
cot and looked at it the way Alcide had looked at it, not at the stone, at the
string, and put out one finger, and touched the cord where it was thicker two
beads from the stone. The knot.

"I tied that," his father said. "In the rain."

He took his finger back.

"The cord broke. At the low place on the river road, with the water going down
the drain and you under my coat. It went off your neck into the water and I
went in after it up to my wrists and I got it — one bead had caught the grate.
One bead. And I knotted it with my hands shaking so bad I couldn't see the cord
and I put it back on you and I said the thing I said, and I've thought about
that knot every night for fourteen years, whether it would hold." He looked at
it. "It held."

"Alcide saw it," Addie said. "At the shop. He stopped on it."

"He would. It's not his knot." His father sat back. "Put it away. Under your
shirt. Keep it there."

Felix put it away.

"It hides you," his father said. "You've had that from Alcide and from Anatole
and from the note I wrote, and you've got it right, and there's more, and you
should have all of it before tonight." He said it like a man laying out tools.
"Worn — on your skin — she can't feel you. Can't see you, can't find you, can't
hold your face in her eye at arm's length across her own table. You've seen
that. *Kept* — off your neck but in your keeping, in a box under your roof, in
a friend's pocket, on a felt square within reach — she feels a warmth. Up,
near, roughly where. No face. No door. That's how the Knights' house passed for
silence for fourteen years, sealed in wax, in a warded house, with you under
the roof, and that's how she found your block last week and not your window.
And off — " He stopped. "Off your neck and out of everybody's keeping, or in
the hands of somebody who doesn't hold it as yours. Then you're a candle. Then
she sees you from anywhere in the parish. And there's one more thing, and it's
the thing that matters, and I need you to hear it."

"I'm hearing it."

"If she ever sees your face — with it off, plain, the way she's never once been
able to — then it's done. The charm will dim you from her after. It won't
*hide* you. Not ever again. A charm hides from what hasn't seen. Once she's
seen you, she can find you the rest of your life, worn or not, and there's no
ward and no craft and no rail that undoes it." His father looked at him.
"That's what she wanted at the table. That's what she wanted in her room. That's
what she whispered in your ear — Jan told me you told Anatole, I know the shape
of it. *Take it off under the sky.* She doesn't want you dead, son. She wants
you *seen.* Once. And then she's got you for good."

Felix's hand was flat on his chest over the stone.

"She's been up top," he said. "In the daytime. In an apron. She served me
coffee."

His father's face went through something.

"She's never done that," he said. "Not in fourteen years. Not in the twenty
I've known her. She goes up at night and she comes down before light and she
does not *work a shift.*" He stood. He went to the rail and put his hands on
it and stood with his back to them, looking down the river way. "Then she's
closer than I thought. Then she'll come tonight."

---

The folder came out of the pack at three.

Addie took it out and laid it on the altar step below the stone, and opened it,
and his father knelt — knelt, on the brick, on one knee, the way you kneel to
look at something you have been afraid of — and looked at the papers with
Felix's name on them and did not touch them. Then at the envelopes. The three
sealed and the one broken. The red wax with its mark.

He reached up to his own throat, and drew out a cord, and on the cord was a
ring.

Gold. Old. Heavy, the edges gone soft, with a mark cut into the face of it
that Felix had seen in wax on four envelopes and in his father's knuckles in
the lamplight, and it was hers, and his father had worn it against his skin
for fourteen years, and he lifted the cord over his head now and held the ring
in his palm and looked at it.

"I took this the same night," he said. "The last thing before I lifted you. I
don't know why. To have something." He held it out. "I've had it long enough.
Put it on the cord with the stone. It's yours. It was always going to be
yours."

Felix took it.

He did not put it on the cord. Not yet. He held it in his fist, warm from his
father's throat, and looked at the mark on it, and then at the three sealed
envelopes, and his father saw him look, and picked up the first one.

"They're hers," his father said. "To you. Written the week you were born. In
her mother's hand — a hand nobody up top can read and I can, barely, because
she taught me the shape of it in a year when she taught me things." He turned
it over. "One for each of the line's turnings, she said. Three. First blood,
first shadow, first — the third one. I never read them. I sealed the one with
the necklace and I put the three in with it because they were yours and I
wasn't going to be the man who kept his son's letters from him, whatever was in
them." A breath. "You want it read."

"Yes."

"You're sure."

"Read it."

His father broke the wax.

It cracked the way the first one had, at the coffee shop, under Felix's
thumbnail, and his father peeled the flap back with the care of a man opening
a thing that might not be his, and took out a single sheet, folded once,
covered edge to edge in a slanting hidden hand, and held it to the lamp, and
read.

He read it badly. Haltingly, a word at a time, going back, stopping, his lips
moving over the shapes before the sounds came, translating out of a language
that was not French and not anything into the plain English of a man who had
not spoken much of any language in fourteen years. He did not read the first
line. He looked at it, and his mouth shut, and he went to the second.

"*You came at the dark of the moon, which is right. You came with your eyes open
and you did not cry, which is right. You have my mother's mothers in you and
they have been waiting on the other side of the water a long time for a boy.*

"*One day you will bleed. A cut, a fall, a fight — it does not matter. The
first blood is the first turning. When it comes, do not be afraid of it. Watch
it. It will be darker than other people's, and it will be slow, and something
under the floor will lift its head. That is the line waking in you. That is
what you are.*

"*Nobody up top will tell you. They do not know. Your father does not know,
though he thinks he does; he was not born to it and he cannot feel it lift.
Only I can tell you and only I will. When the first blood comes I will be
close. I have always been close. Look for me in the dark and I will be the
thing in it that is looking back.*

"*Keep the beads on. Not for the reason he will give you.*"

His father stopped.

"There's a line under that," he said. "It's a name. Hers. I'm not reading it."

Nobody said anything. The lamp hissed. Outside the rail the river way went on
being dark.

"She wrote that to a baby," Addie said finally. Her voice was strange.

"She wrote that to *me,*" Felix said.

He looked at the sheet in his father's hand. He could not read one mark of it.
It had been written the week he was born by a woman who had held him with her
eyes open in a room with cloth on the walls, and it was the first thing she had
ever said to him that she had meant him to keep, and it was terrible, and it
was — he did not have a word. It was not nothing. He had spent four days
wanting it to be nothing and it was not.

*Keep the beads on. Not for the reason he will give you.*

"She knew," Felix said. "Eighteen years ago. She knew you'd tell me to keep it
on. She knew you'd take it. She knew *before I was a week old* —"

"She knows things." His father folded the sheet along its crease and put it
back in its envelope, and set it on the altar step with the other two, the
sealed ones, and did not look at those. "That's what she does. That's the
whole of what she does. She's known for fourteen years where I was, and she's
let me sit here, because she knew you'd come, and she knew I'd — " He stopped
himself. "The other two stay sealed. That's mine to say and I'm saying it.
First shadow. And the third. You're not ready for the second and God willing
you'll never be ready for the third."

"First shadow," Felix said. "I felt it. Last night. A candle —"

"I know. Anatole told me." His father's hand came out, and stopped, and did not
touch him. "I know."

---

At four Anatole stood.

He had been quiet a long time against the brick, and he stood the way a tired
man stands, in pieces, and picked up his lamp.

"I've got a hall," he said. "I've been gone three hours and my brother's been
gone since midnight and I don't like the arithmetic of that." He looked at Jan.
"You're staying."

"I'm staying."

"Then I'll come back at dark with Odile and bread and whatever I've still got."
He went to the rail and stopped there, inside, one hand on the post, the way he
had on Sunday morning. "Delvaux."

"Anatole."

"I did what I could."

"I know you did."

"Say it to Jan."

"She's sitting right there. Say it yourself."

Anatole looked at Jan, and did not say it, and Jan looked back at him with the
dispatcher's face and said, "I heard you the first time, Anatole," and he went
through the rail and along the brick with the light going away ahead of him,
and the four of them sat in the crypt with the clock and the lamp and the Hilt
on its cloth and three envelopes on the altar step, one open.

Felix got up. His knee had stiffened and he made it work anyway. He went to the
wall.

He had been looking at it for three hours and not looking at it. The marks.
Two columns of them, charcoal, going up the brick beside the cot — the short
lines, the long lines, the crosses, the circles, fourteen circles, an older
column beside them in a hand like his father's and not — and to the left of
the columns, on the wall where a child would have stood if there had been a
child, the other marks.

He had seen those from the crate. He had known what they were.

He went and stood against the wall.

He did it without saying anything, with his back to the brick and his heels
against it and his hands at his sides, the way you stand for a doctor, and he
felt the top of his own head come to rest a hand's width under a short
charcoal line with *18* beside it, and stood there.

Behind him, on the cot, his father made a sound.

"Shorter," Felix said. To the wall. "You guessed wrong. By a hand."

"By a hand."

"I'm short. I've always been short. Robert's six one and Diane's five ten and
I'm five six and I never — nobody ever said anything about that either." He
did not turn around. "You should have made the marks lower."

"I'll make them lower."

"There aren't any more to make."

"Then I'll make one," his father said, "for nineteen. Lower. When it comes."

Felix stood against the wall with his heels on the brick and the mark over his
head and the ring in his fist and the stone at his throat and did not move,
and his father did not move, and Addie sat on the floor with her mother and did
not move, and in the river way beyond the rail, four hundred yards off, the
clock's small sound went out into the dark and was eaten.

At six he took the ring out of his fist, and lifted the cord over his head,
and slid it on beside the black stone, and put the loop back over his hair,
and let both of them drop into the hollow of his throat — the bead Alcide had
cut and the ring his mother had worn — and tucked them down inside his collar,
and put his hand over them, flat, once.

"Okay," he said. "Tonight."
