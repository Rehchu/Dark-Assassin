# What Janet Knew

The call came in at two-forty.

She had known it would. Not the minute — nobody knew the minute — but the
night. She had known it since Thursday, when she had stood at the blinds and
watched a tall shape stand between the streetlights across from her house and
then not be there, and she had known it harder on Saturday when the rain came,
because the rain always brought it, and she had sat down at the console at
seven with her headset on and her coffee going cold and her reading glasses on
their cord and waited for the phone to ring the way you wait for a kettle.

Six hours of the ordinary. A wreck on MacArthur, nobody hurt. A man who could
not find his wife and then found her. A kid who had taken something and was
scared. She had worked them all in the flat voice, the one that got flatter
the more it mattered, and she had watched the clock over the board, and at
two-forty the line lit and she took it.

A man's voice, shaking. A driver on the river road who had seen a parish truck
sitting at the low place with its lights on and its door open and had stopped
to see was the man all right, and the man was not all right. Sitting against
the levee wall. Eyes open. Not breathing. No blood, the caller kept saying, no
blood, no nothing, he's just *sitting* there, like he sat down to —

"I understand. Stay with him. Stay on the line with me. What's the nearest
cross street."

She dispatched it. She said the words she said. Unit to the river road at the
low place below Riverbend, male, fifties, unresponsive, no visible injury,
caller on scene. She said *possible cardiac* because that was the box, and she
would read the coroner's word for it in the paper on Wednesday, small, and the
word would be *consistent,* and she took the cap off the pen on the desk with
her teeth and wrote on the inside of her left wrist, below the cuff of her
sweater where nobody at the console could see it, *2:40.*

The forty-second.

She finished the call. She took the next one. She did not cry; she had not
cried at a console in nineteen years and she was not going to start on the
night her daughter had not come home.

---

At seven she signed out and drove.

Not home. Not yet. She drove the river road in the first gray light with the
heater on and the wet everywhere, the rain gone and the wind gone with it and
the front through at last, cold behind it, the sky scrubbed clean — and at the
low place the water had gone down and the road was a road with leaves plastered
to it and a parish truck gone and a piece of yellow tape on the levee wall
where a big soft man had sat down to rest.

She did not slow. She looked.

Riverbend was next. She turned in past the flag and drove to the Building C
side and there was the bike, where she had found it Sunday afternoon in the
rain, where it had sat all night. Addie's bike. Helmets locked to it with the
cable, two, the spare and the black one, beaded with wet.

Janet sat in the car with the engine running and looked at the bike.

Sunday she had woken at three to a note on the counter in her daughter's
handwriting — *Riverbend w/ F, back by dark, don't worry* — and had driven here
in the rain, and found the bike, and gone in under the stair, and stood in the
basement with the water heater humming, and looked at the door in the far wall
standing a hand's width open with the ash swept and laid, and had not gone
through it. She had stood there with her hand on the ring for a long time. Then
she had gone home, because she was on shift at seven, and because Anatole had
them, and because a woman who went down that stair at four on a Sunday
afternoon with a shift to work was a woman who did not come back up for it,
and she had a console to sit at, and a call to take.

She had taken it. It was on her wrist.

Now it was Monday, and she was off, and Addie had not come home.

She put the car in park. She got out. She locked it, beside the bike, and
stood a moment with her hand on the wet seat of the bike the way she had put
her hand on Addie's back on the stairs on Thursday, and then she got back in
the car, because there were two things to do first, and she was a woman who did
things in order.

---

The map was on the inside of her closet door.

She had put it there fourteen years ago, the week the boy went to the Knights,
a parish road map from the glove box taped to the wood at eye level behind the
coats, and she had kept it there since, and Addie had never once opened this
closet because it was her mother's closet and Addie was untidy but she was not
a snoop.

Two colors of pen.

Black: forty-one small marks, each with a date beside it in her dispatcher's
hand, going back fourteen years, scattered across the map the way a thing
scatters when it is not choosing. Downtown. The river road. A parking lot on
MacArthur. Under a bridge. One in the Garden District, nine years ago, that she
still could not look at. *Consistent with cardiac event,* every one. A healthy
person against a wall with their eyes open. The paper had run each of them
small, and she had cut each one out and put it in a shoebox on the closet
shelf, and she had made the mark.

She took the pen from her coat and made the forty-second. The river road, at
the low place. *2:40.*

Red: four. All in the last six weeks. All clustered — that was the thing that
had made her stand in front of this door on a Tuesday in October with her coat
still on and feel the floor of her stomach go out. Four red marks around
Riverbend, along the river road, on the levee, and none of them a body. Not
found. Not against a wall. Gone. Grown people who did not wander off, and none
of them on the news, because the parish did not put a thing on the news until
it was sure what the thing was, and the parish was not sure, and Janet was.

She did not know why the red ones were new. Fourteen years of black and never
a red. That was the one thing on the door she could not read, and it frightened
her more than the forty-two.

She closed the closet.

She went out to the garden in her coat.

The orchid cactus was where Addie had left it on Thursday night — half out of
its pot on the potting bench under the clip light, its roots naked in the cold
that had come in behind the front, five years of the plant's patient work
exposed for four days to weather it had never been meant to meet. Addie had
left it like that. Addie had told herself she would finish it, and had left it,
and had gone to meet a boy about a folder, and had not been back since.

Janet repotted it.

She did it with her coat on and her hands bare in the cold, the way she did
things when there was a thing to do and no time to do it right — the new pot,
the soil, the roots gone soft at the tips and firm above, tamped down, watered
from the can. It took four minutes. It was the only thing in the garden or the
house or the parish that she could put back the way it was supposed to be, and
she did it, and she stood over it with her hands dirty and thought about a boy
who had brought a cutting of it home from Belize in a sock because it was
supposed to live where you would never expect it.

Then she washed her hands at the outside tap, and got in the car, and drove
to Main Street.

---

Alcide opened the back door before she knocked.

He had been standing behind it. She could tell by how fast it came open and by
his face, which was the face of a man who had not slept in a chair against a
door and had done it anyway. Seventy-one years old with white in his beard and
a bad heart and a rag at his belt at eight in the morning.

"Jan."

"They went down. Sunday. They didn't come up."

"I know. I've been —" He stopped. He looked past her at the alley. "Come in."

"I'm not coming in. I'm going down. I came to say it to your face so you'd
know where I was." She said it the way she said everything. "Addie's under
the city with the Knight boy and Anatole has them, or had them, and I'm going
down the door and I'm not coming up without her. If I don't come up, that's
where I am."

Alcide stood in the doorway with his hand on the frame.

"I have a truck," he said.

She looked at him.

"I have a truck," he said again, "and a crowbar, and I've got a heart that
won't take a stair, and I've spent twenty years behind three locks and a chain
because I made a thing for a woman once near closing and never got over it,
and I'm saying I have a truck. If you call, I'll come to the door. I'll come
to the *door,* Jan. I can't come down it. But I'll be at the top of it with the
truck running." He wiped his hands on the rag. "That's what I've got. It's more
than I've said in twenty years."

"I know it is."

"Go on, then."

She went. In the alley she heard the door shut, and one lock, and then the
second, and then the chain, and she thought, not for the first time, that the
sound of Alcide Fontenot locking his door was the loudest thing on Main Street,
and that there were people in this parish who had been alive a long time on the
strength of it.

---

She went down the stair at nine.

She had a right to it. That was the thing nobody up top would have understood
and nobody down here had ever once questioned: Janet Walker went down the
wooden door when she needed to, and the watchers at the foot of the bell-stair
knew her step before they saw her lamp, and let her by. Her grandmother had
been born on the old street. Her grandmother's mother had carried her up the
stair in her arms in the year the town on top put in electric light and the
family had decided, in a hall made of a warehouse, that some of them would go
up and try it. The Walkers had gone up. Three generations of sun. It had not
gotten the street out of them. It had not gotten the *weather* out of them —
the cold at the back of the neck, the sickness in the gut when a certain thing
was near — and Janet had stood at the blinds on Thursday and felt it on her own
street and had known, then, exactly how far it had come.

Ninety-four steps. The sage at the bottom, and the lamp. She did not need the
lamp. She walked the old street in the dark with her hand trailing the brick
the way she had walked it at nineteen, past the swan bottle on its sill, past
LEMOINE, past the doorways, and she felt the watchers see her from wherever
they were and fall in behind her and did not turn, and at the curtain of the
hall she stopped, and took off her reading glasses, and put them in her coat
pocket, and went in.

---

Addie saw her mother come through the curtain and did not know her.

That was the first thing. A woman in a tan coat with her hair gone gray at the
temples and her face set in a way Addie had never seen it set, walking into a
hall under the city at half past nine in the morning as if she had walked into
it a hundred times, with forty pale people going quiet at the sight of her and
a boy running down the length of the table shouting *Miss Jan, Miss Jan* — and
it took Addie a full second, sitting on the bench with the pack between her
feet and Felix beside her, to put the word *Mama* on it.

Then she was up. Then she was across the hall. Then she had stopped, six feet
short, because her mother had held up one hand, flat, the way she did at the
console when someone was talking over a call.

"Are you hurt."

"My wrist. It's — it's nothing. Mama —"

"Is he hurt."

"His knee. Mama, *what are you —*"

"Sit down."

Addie sat down. She did not decide to; her body did it, the way it had done
things her mother said since before she could talk. Felix had stood up when she
did and he sat down too, slowly, on the bench, with his hand at his collar, and
her mother came the rest of the way across the hall and stood in front of the
two of them with the whole town watching, and Anatole came down from the head
of the table and stood beside her, and she did not look at him.

"I'm going to say it," Janet said. "In order. Don't stop me. When I'm done you
can say whatever you want to me and I'll have earned all of it. But I'm going
to say it in order or I won't get through it."

"Okay," Addie said.

Her mother looked at Felix.

"Fourteen years ago I was a caseworker at the Alexandria Orphanage," Janet
said. "Its last years. There were eleven children in it and a leaking roof and
a diocese that wanted the land. I did the files. I did the placements. I was
good at it." A breath. "In August of that year a man came to my office at the
end of the day with rain on his coat and asked me if I was Walker, and I said I
was, and he asked me if I was *the* Walker, the one whose people had come up
from under, and I said I didn't know what he meant, and he said, *You do. You
feel it. That's why I came to you.*"

The hall did not move.

"He had a boy. Four years old. He didn't have him with him — he'd left him
somewhere safe, he said, for the length of the conversation. He needed papers.
Real ones, through the orphanage, through the court, so that the boy would have
a name and a family and a birth certificate nobody would look at twice, and he
needed me to find the family. He needed them to be ordinary. He said that word
five times. *Ordinary.* He needed them to be kind and to do what they were
told and to never once ask why, and he needed me to pick them, because I could
look at a person and know."

"The Knights," Felix said.

"I chose the Knights. They were in my church. I'd watched them for a year. They
alphabetized things and they cried at hymns and they wanted a child more than
anything in the world and they'd been told they couldn't. I went to their house
on a Tuesday and I told them a boy was coming, and that they would be asked to
keep three things, and they said yes before I'd finished the sentence." She did
not look away from him. "I did the papers. I sat with the judge. I put the
orphanage's name on the top of the page so it would be real, and it is. You're
theirs. That was never a lie. Everything else was."

Felix's face had gone still.

"He brought you in the rain. He wouldn't say his name to them and he wouldn't
say it to me, and I knew it anyway, because I'd felt what was on him the way
I'd felt it on nobody in my life, and I knew what he was running from and what
he'd taken and what he'd put around your neck. He said three things at their
door and went. I never saw him again. I know where he is." She looked, for the
first time, down the hall, toward the far end, toward the curtain and the
street and the river way beyond it. "I've known for fourteen years. I've sent
word to him through Anatole when there was word to send. I never went to the
rail. That wasn't mine."

"The box," Addie said. Her voice did not sound like hers.

"I told the Knights to keep it with him. He'd said *with,* so I said with. I
told them to put it away where he'd never find it and to give it to him the
day he left home, because that was the third thing the man said, and because
I thought — " Her mother stopped. It was the first time she had stopped. "I
thought a boy grown was a boy who could carry it. I was wrong about that. I
was wrong about the day. Diane called me on a Sunday to say Robert had driven
the last box over and I felt the floor go, and I've been standing in front of
my closet door every night since."

"Your closet."

"There's a map on it. I'll show you. I'll show you all of it." Janet's voice
had not changed and her face had. "It's got forty-two marks in black on it.
People who sat down against walls with their eyes open and were called
*random.* One every few months for fourteen years. They're hers. Every one.
That's what she does when she can't find what she's looking for. I've written
the time of every one of them on my wrist at that console and gone home and
made the mark." She pushed up her left sleeve. On the inside of the wrist, in
blue ink, going a little in the wet: *2:40.* "That's last night. The river road.
A parish man checking drains ahead of the rain."

Addie put her hand over her mouth.

"And it's got four in red. Six weeks. Grown people, gone, not found, not
against any wall. That's the string I told you about on the stairs. That's not
her. I don't know what that is." Janet looked at Anatole then, once, and
Anatole looked at the floor. "Somebody down here does."

---

"The house," Addie said.

She had not meant to say it. It came out of her the way the sickness came,
from underneath. "Thursday. The kitchen. The sage. You sent me for *sage* —"

"I sent you for sage because I was out and I needed to lay it fresh. I laid
it with what I had while you were at the coffee shop and I laid it again when
you brought the rest. The sills. The doors. Butter and honey on a saucer at the
back, because that's what my grandmother did and her grandmother, and I don't
know why it works and I know that it does." Flat. In order. "That's the smell.
That's why the kitchen was clean. That's why the car was gone — I went to
Alcide's back door at seven to tell him the boy had opened the box and would
be sent to him, and he said *she'll make sure of it,* and he was right. That's
where I was. I wasn't going to say any of this to you on the stairs with a
helmet under your arm."

"You said *be careful.*"

"I don't always say that."

"You said — " Addie's voice went and came back. "I said I'd be back before the
sun set."

"You said before the sun set," her mother said, "and the sun had been down an
hour, and I let you, because it's what you always say, and I thought it might
be the last ordinary thing you ever said to me in that hall." Her hands were at
her sides. "I've been thinking about that for four days."

Nobody in the hall said anything.

"The van," Felix said.

Janet turned.

"Diane said — my mother said the woman who did the papers might go to their
church. My father thought so. And Addie and I met at — we met on a church
trip. Freshman year. The youth van." He was looking at Janet the way he had
looked at the tin mirror. "Was that you?"

"Yes."

Addie made a sound.

"One time," Janet said. She said it to Addie. She said it fast, for her, the
only fast thing she had said. "One van. One trip, to the water park, the summer
you were fourteen. I was a chaperone because I asked to be, and I put a
copper-haired girl in the back seat and a boy with his hair over his ears in
the seat beside her, because I needed to know if you'd *feel* it near him —
the thing he had on him, the quiet — or if it would slide off you like it
slides off everybody. And you sat next to him for forty minutes and you
laughed at something he said and you didn't feel a thing, because the charm was
in a box in a closet and he was just a boy, and I thought, good. Good. She's
free of it. She won't have to carry it." A breath. "And then you came home and
told me about the boy with the ears who wrote about buses, and I knew I'd been
wrong about that too."

"You *put* us —"

"One van. One time." Her mother's voice cracked, finally, all the way through,
in front of forty people, and she let it. "The rest was yours, Addie. All of
it. Every day for four years. I put you in a seat. You did the rest. I've
watched you do the rest and I've never once, not for one day, been anything
but glad of it, and if you never forgive me for the seat I'll understand that
too. But I'm not going to stand here and let you think it was me. It wasn't
me. It was you. All of it."

Addie was crying. She had not known she was. She sat on the bench with the
pack between her feet and her wrapped wrist in her lap and the tears going
down her face in the lamplight, and her mother stood six feet from her and did
not come closer, and did not have to.

"You feel it," Janet said, quietly. "Don't you. The sick. When she's near."

"Yes."

"You felt it Thursday. Across the street. You felt it in the coffee shop, and
at the door, and coming down the street last night — Ti-Jean told me on the
stair, he said you went white a minute before anybody." Her mother's face did a
thing that Addie had never seen it do, which was give up. "I hoped you
wouldn't. That's the truth. I hoped it had thinned out enough in three
generations that you'd get the sun and not the rest of it. I hoped you'd get
your father's ears and not my mother's." She almost smiled. "You didn't get
either. You got one of each. You got that from my mother, the left one. She'd
have liked you."

Addie put her hand up to her own ear, the left one, the one that sat a little
lower, that she had never once thought about in her life, and held it.

---

Anatole cleared his throat.

"Jan," he said. "There's — you should know. Since last night. Armand's gone
down her way and not come up. He's got men. Six, maybe more. The four in red —
" He stopped. He looked at the two of them on the bench, and at her, and made
himself say it. "I think that's him. I think he took them to her. Not
presented. *Taken.* I've thought it six weeks and not said it because he's my
brother and because if it's true then the truce is already broken and I'm the
one who let it break. I'm saying it now."

Janet nodded once, the way she nodded at the console when a unit confirmed.

"Then we haven't got long," she said. "And I'm not going up without these two,
and I'm not taking these two up past that door with Armand's men on the stair
and her below it." She looked down the hall, toward the far end. "Delvaux's
expecting them."

"Midday. By the river way. I said I'd take them myself."

"You'll take three."

Anatole looked at her.

"I've never gone to the rail," Janet said. "It wasn't mine. It's mine now."
She pulled her sleeve down over the ink on her wrist. "Get us a lamp."

Addie stood up. She got the pack on, both straps. She did not know what she
was going to say to her mother and she did not say anything, and her mother did
not say anything either, and they stood there in the hall six feet apart with
the whole town watching and the same left ear, and it was Felix who moved
first — Felix, with his knee, getting up off the bench with his hand at his
collar, and limping the six feet, and stopping in front of Janet Walker, who
had chosen his parents and sat with a judge and put him in a van.

"You watched me," he said. "My whole life."

"Yes."

"Was I all right?"

Janet looked at him for a long moment. The dispatcher's face. The flat voice,
when it came, flatter than Addie had ever heard it, which meant it mattered
more than anything her mother had ever said.

"You were always all right," she said. "That was the whole point of it."
