# The Pawn Shop

Felix and Addie sped outside and jumped on Addie's bike.

The heat on the sidewalk was the wrong kind for the week before Thanksgiving,
thick and still and standing, and Felix came out into it with the folder already
gone into the bag on the back of the bike and his hand up at his collar, flat
over the cord, the way it had been going all morning without his telling it to.

They had no idea what they were going to uncover.

They knew they had to figure out where the necklace came from, and why nobody
told Felix what was going on.

That was the whole of it. That was what he had, standing on Third Street in the
white glare with a stranger's voice still in his ear. Not a plan. A thing at his
throat that somebody had made, and a woman who said an old man on Main Street
would know who, and four blocks between here and there.

Four blocks. He could have walked it in six minutes. He opened his mouth to say
so.

"Put this on" said Addie as she was giving Felix a spare helmet.

It was the black one, the one that lived strapped behind her seat and that he
had worn exactly twice in four years, both times to prove something to her and
both times regretting it. It smelled like the inside of a helmet. He put it on.
The strap went under his jaw and the world went small and loud.

"This might get a little wild"

Addie revved up her bike's engine, and took off.

Felix immediately grabbed her around the waist before he could fall off.

There was no other choice. The bike went out from under him and forward at the
same time and the only solid thing in the world was her, and he got his arms
around her jacket and locked his hands together over her stomach and put his
helmet against the back of hers and held on. Third Street went sideways. The
bank on the corner, the flag on it hanging dead in the still air, went by so
close he could have touched the brick.

She had no regard for her own safety nor Felix's.

He knew that about her the way you know the color of a person's eyes. He had
known it since the first time he saw her ride, freshman year, out of the church
parking lot with her hair loose under the helmet and the youth pastor shouting
something after her that she could not hear. He had loved her for it, from a
distance, at first, and up close, later, and he had never once in four years
gotten used to being on the back of it.

The only thing she had on her mind was getting to the pawn shop.

Left turn, right turn, zipping in and out of traffic.

Off Third. Onto Jackson for one block, the wrong way it felt like, a delivery
truck idling in the lane with its flashers on and Addie going around it on the
side where there was not room to go around it, the mirror of the truck going
past his shoulder near enough that he felt the air off it. Then right. Then a
light that was yellow and then was not. Then a pickup, and a gap, and the gap
closing, and Addie in the gap.

She was being reckless, and Felix terrified.

He had his eyes shut. He opened them because shut was worse. The pavement went
by a foot under his sneaker in a gray blur and he watched it and thought,
clearly and with great calm, *if I come off this I will slide,* and then thought
about how much of him there was to slide, and shut his eyes again.

He grabbed her tighter, and tried to get her to stop.

"Addie —"

The helmet ate it. He said it into the back of her head. He pounded once, flat,
on her stomach with both locked hands, which was the signal, and she took it
for *go faster,* or took it for nothing. The bike leaned. His stomach did not
lean with it. His stomach stayed where it had been, somewhere back on Jackson
Street, and the rest of him went on without it.

Before he knew it, they had arrived.

The engine cut. The world stopped moving and he did not, for a second, and then
he did. Brick. A curb. A parking meter with a bag over its head. Heat coming up
off the sidewalk through the soles of his shoes. He got one leg over and stood
on it and it held, mostly, and he got the helmet off and the air came at him
warm and still and smelling of hot tar and the river.

GOLD AND SILVER PAWN SHOP

had displayed vertically on a sign attached to the building.

The sign ran down the corner of the building on a metal bracket, letter under
letter, the way signs had been made when this street was the whole town — GOLD
at the top, SHOP down near his eye, the paint gone chalky and the metal rusting
through in a line under every letter like each one had been crying. It had been
white once. The building under it had been red brick once, and was still, under
the years.

"I never want to be on that thing again" said Felix while he was trying not to
be sick.

He said it to the sign. He had a hand on the meter. Addie was already off the
bike and had her helmet under her arm and was looking at him with the face she
used when she was deciding whether to laugh, and decided not to, which was
worse.

"You're fine."

"I am not fine. I am the opposite of fine. That was four blocks, Addie. You
could have walked it. I could have walked it. My grandmother could have —"

"You don't have a grandmother."

"That's not the point." He swallowed. It stayed down. "That is not the point."

They checked out the building.

It was quaint, and it seemed to have seen better days.

One storefront wide. A door with a glass panel and a hand-lettered card in it
that he could not read from here. One window, and the window was the thing:
deep, the old kind with the display shelf behind the glass, and crowded — a
saxophone on a stand, a row of watches on a velvet board gone gray, a stack of
plates, a lamp, a rifle with a tag on the trigger guard, a child's bicycle. And
hung from the top of the window frame on a thread of fishing line, turning
very slightly, or not turning, in the dead air behind the glass, a little
unicorn mobile, five white unicorns on five threads under a hoop, the kind you
hung over a crib.

Above the window, two more windows, curtained. Somebody lived up there.

"Are they even open"? Asked Addie. "I hope I didn't waste a trip over here for
nothing".

The card in the door said CLOSED. He could read it now. Not the kind with a
clock face and the little hands you moved; a plain card, hand-lettered, the
word and nothing else, hung on a string from a nail on the inside of the glass.
It was a quarter past noon on a Friday and the shop was closed and the street
was empty. Not empty the way a street is at noon. Empty the way a street is
when something has just gone by.

He looked up and down it. Brick fronts. An awning. Doorways in shade on the far
side, deep ones, the sun straight down and making shadows that were only under
things. Nobody in any of them. A cup in the gutter that did not roll.

A figure in the window moves, and makes Addie jump.

She actually jumped. He felt it through the sidewalk. Behind the saxophone and
the plates, back in the dim of the shop, something had shifted — a shape, a
shoulder, a person moving from one place to another place and stopping — and
then the display window was only a display window, the unicorns, the rifle, the
watches on their board.

"I think they're open. I see someone inside" responded Felix.

He had, for a second. He was not sure now what. He put his hand up to the
collar of his shirt and the cord was there and the stone under it against his
skin, and he went to the door.

Something happened at the door before he got there. He would think about it
later and not be able to say what. A sound behind the glass — a bolt going
back, or a chain — and then the CLOSED card swinging a little on its string as
if the door had moved in its frame. He put his hand on the handle and it turned.

He walks inside, and notices trinkets, books, clothes, and even a little
unicorn mobile hanging.

It was cool inside, and dim, and it smelled like a hundred years of other
people's things. The light came from the window and from two small gooseneck
lamps clipped to a glass counter along the back, and that was all, and it took
his eyes a second. Then it came up out of the dark at him all at once — shelves
to the ceiling on both sides, a wall of guitars, a rack of leather jackets with
the shoulders gone soft, glass cases with rings in them and cases with knives in
them and one case with nothing in it at all but a folded flag. Books in stacks.
A birdcage. A row of televisions from three different decades, all off. And the
unicorns, closer now, turning on their fishing line in the window an inch from
his shoulder, five little white horses going around and around in air that was
not moving.

Addie came in behind him and the door swung to and the street was gone.

Before either one of them could say anything, a man approaches from behind a
curtain and heads to the counter.

The curtain was at the back, dark green, hung across a doorway beside the
counter, and it was still swinging when the man was already at the glass. He
was not tall. He had white in his beard and a rag at his belt and a shirt
buttoned to the throat, and he moved the way you move in a room full of things
that could fall, and he came to the counter and put both hands flat on it and
looked at them.

Not at Felix. At Addie first, a long second. Then at Felix, and something went
across his face and was gone.

"Can I help you with anything?" He asks.

Felix and Addie both approach with caution.

They did it without deciding to. Down the aisle between the jackets and the
guitars, side by side, slow, the way you walk up on a dog you do not know. The
glass counter had squares of black felt laid along it under the lamps and a
loupe on a cord and a little brass scale, and behind the man, on the wall, a
board hung thick with keys.

Felix set both helmets down on the end of the counter, his and hers, and the
bag with the folder in it on the floor by his foot, and he stood there with his
hand at his collar.

"We are told that you might be able to help us with this"

Felix hands the necklace over to the shopkeeper.

He took it off to do it. That was the thing he would remember. He reached up
under his collar and got his fingers under the cord and lifted it, the beads
dragging up through his hair, the stone coming out of the hollow of his throat
where it had sat since eleven-thirty, and for a second it hung from his hand in
the lamp light between him and the man, turning, and his throat under the
collar felt cold and open the way a hand feels when you take a ring off it that
you have worn for years.

Then it was in the man's hand.

The man did not look at the stone.

He examines the string upon which the necklace hangs.

That was the first thing. Not the black bead that had brought them here — the
cord. He ran it through his fingers slowly, the whole length of it, the way you
run a rope through your hands looking for a fray, and his thumb went along it
and stopped. Stopped on a place two beads from the stone where the cord was
thicker. A knot. A small tight knot that Felix had never noticed, tied in the
cord and the ends trimmed close.

The man's thumb stayed on the knot a long moment. His face did not change.
Then his thumb went on.

He begins to individually inspect every hand crafted wooden bead, then finally
the obsidian for which the rune is engraved.

Every bead. He turned each one under the lamp. He looked at the grain of them,
the marks on them, the wear where they had rubbed against a throat, and there
were eleven of them and he did not skip one. Felix counted. He had nothing else
to do with the time. Beside him Addie had gone still, the way she went still
when she was watching something she meant to remember.

And then the man's fingers came to the stone, and closed on it, and turned it
over, and he tilted it into the light so the mark came up out of the black — the
stem, the two tines — and he stopped.

He stared at it for a brief time, almost as if he was mesmerized by it.

His mouth came a little open. His eyes did not move. The lamp was on the stone
and the stone gave nothing back and the man looked into it the way you look at
a photograph of a house you used to live in, and Felix, watching him, felt the
cold at his own throat where the necklace had been, and felt the man's stillness
go into the room and fill it the way the smell of the old things filled it,
and did not breathe.

The unicorns turned in the window.

"Wherever did you get this?" The man asked.

His voice had changed. Felix could not have said how. It was the same voice,
plain, a receipt voice, and something had gone out of it or come into it.

Addie and Felix quickly glanced at each other, then looked back at the man.

"I'll ask again. Where did you receive this item from?"

He asked again, but this time with anger in his voice.

The hands on the counter had closed. The man's knuckles had gone white where
they gripped the necklace. He was not looking at the stone anymore. He was
looking at Felix's face, and at the sides of Felix's face, under the hair, and
Felix felt the heat come up his neck and could not stop it, and the anger in
the man's voice went up a notch as if the heat had done it.

"I don't know, it was in a box of some old stuff my parents left me when I
moved out." Said Felix as he stepped back with fear.

He had not meant to step back. His body did it. One step, off the counter, his
hand coming up empty to the collar where there was nothing now, and he heard
his own voice come out thin and young and hated it.

"A box," the man said.

"When I moved out. Last week. My parents — my dad brought it over. Old stuff.
Pictures. That was in it. In an envelope. With a note."

"What did the note say."

"To keep it on." Felix's hand was still at his throat. "To never take it off."

The man looked at the necklace in his fist. He looked at Felix's empty collar.
Something happened in his face that Felix would understand later and did not
understand now — not anger, or not only. The kind of look a man gives a stove
he has just realized has been on all night.

"Do you want to know about this necklace" the man asked.

"Of course we do! It's why we are here!" exclaimed Addie.

It came out of her like a shout in a church, and it went up into the dark
among the guitars and the birdcage and the shelves of other people's things,
and hung there, and the man did not flinch at it.

He looked at her for a long moment, the way he had when he came through the
curtain. Then he looked down at the necklace, and laid it, very carefully, on a
square of black felt under the nearer lamp, and reached up, and turned the lamp
so that it fell full on the stone.
