# Research

The grocery on Jackson closed at eight and it was twenty past seven and she made
it in nine minutes, which was a record even for her, and she did not remember a
single one of the lights.

Addie rushed to the store to retrieve the items her mom asked for.

She left the bike crooked in a space by the cart return and went in with her
helmet still under her arm because she did not trust the lot. The store had the
Thursday-night feel, half the registers dark, a kid mopping an aisle nobody was
in, the music turned down low enough that you could hear the coolers hum. She
went straight to produce. Sage was not where sage should be. She found it at the
end of the herb rack behind the cilantro, the fresh bunches in their little
plastic sleeves, silver-green and soft and smelling of nothing until you put
your face down to them, and then of everything — of Thanksgiving and of her
grandmother's kitchen and of something under both of those that she could not
have named.

Two. Her mother had said two.

She took two, and reached into her left jacket pocket for the twenty and the
ten, and her fingers closed on wood.

Immediately, she realized she had Felix's necklace.

She stood there in produce with her hand in her pocket and the beads under her
fingers and her whole body went cold and then hot. She could see it — she could
see herself doing it, standing up too fast at the table with the thing in her
fist, shoving the fist into the pocket, the hand coming out empty. She had
walked out of the coffee shop with it. She had ridden two miles with it against
her hip. He had a note in a folder that said *at all times* in block capitals
and she had it in her pocket with the grocery money.

She did not take it out. Not in the store, not under the lights. She got her
fingers around the bills without touching the stone, which was harder than it
sounds, and drew them out, and she stood a second longer with her hand over the
pocket as if the necklace might climb out on its own.

*Two seconds,* she had said to him. *I'm not going to run off with it.*

She grabbed the items she needed, and headed home.

Butter, the real kind, in the box. Honey — she stood in front of the honey for
longer than she should have, the plastic bears in their row and behind them the
squat glass jars with the hand-lettered label from the man out on the Boyce
road, and she took the jar, because her mother had said the jar and her mother
had said not the bear and Addie had learned a long time ago that her mother did
not say things twice for no reason. The kid at the one open register did not
look at her. She had eleven dollars and some change left and she did not buy a
drink with it. She was not thirsty. She was not anything. She was a girl with a
bag and a thing in her pocket that did not belong to her, and she wanted to be
home.

The lot had emptied out while she was inside. The light over the cart return
buzzed and flickered and did not quite go out. Her bike was where she had left
it, crooked, and she stood beside it and hooked the bag to the seat with the
bungee cord and pulled the cord twice to be sure, and then she put her hand flat
over the left pocket of her jacket the way you put a hand over a sleeping thing,
and held it there, and felt the beads through the canvas, and felt the stone.
Then she got on.

She rode home slow. She kept her left hand on the grip and the pocket against
the tank and she took the turns like a person who had never lane-split in her
life.

When she pulled up to the house, her mom's car was gone.

The drive was empty. The porch light was on. The front room was dark and the
kitchen was lit, the yellow of it laid across the side yard, and there was no
gray sedan in the drive and no gray sedan on the street, and Addie sat on the
bike with the engine off and the bag hanging from her wrist and looked at the
place where the car should have been.

"I wonder where she went," she thought.

Her mother did not go places on an off night. Her mother sat with the television
on and a crossword she did not finish and went to bed at nine like a person who
had spent enough of her life awake at three in the morning to know what sleep
was worth. It was ten to eight. The car was gone.

Addie looked across the street. The sidewalk between the two streetlights was
sidewalk. The Guidrys' upstairs lamp was still on.

She went in.

The bag is brought into the kitchen, and placed on the table.

She could tell it had just gotten cleaned.

Not the way it was cleaned on a Saturday, with the radio on and her mother in
the old jeans. Cleaned the way you clean a room you are getting ready for
something. The counters were sparkling, the floors looked like it could be eaten
off of. The dish rack was empty. The stove top had been taken apart and put back
together. And the smell — that was the thing she stood in the doorway and could
not get past. Not bleach. Not lemon. Something green and burnt, faint, like a
match struck and blown out, like the smoke off a bundle of leaves, and it was
strongest at the window over the sink, the black window where she had seen her
own face two hours ago, and at the back door.

There was a saucer on the sill of the back door. A white saucer from the good
set, with a curl of butter on it gone soft, and a spoonful of honey beside the
butter, and nothing else.

Addie looked at it for a while.

Ants, she thought. Something for the ants, some trick from her grandmother's
side of the family, the side that had come up out of the country with a hundred
things you did to a house that nobody could explain and everybody did anyway.
Her mother had a few of those. She kept a broom by the back door that nobody
swept with. She would not let you put a hat on a bed.

Addie set the sage on the table beside the bag, both bunches, and did not touch
the saucer.

She got her phone out. She typed *where are you* and looked at it and deleted
it, because that was her mother's line, not hers, and it felt wrong in her
thumbs. She typed *home. got your stuff.* and sent that instead. The phone
showed her *delivered* and then showed her nothing. The kitchen ticked. The
smell of the burnt leaves sat in the room the way a person sits in a room, and
she stood in it a moment longer, and then she went to the refrigerator because
that was what you did in a kitchen.

Addie grabs a snack out of the fridge, and heads up the stairs.

A string cheese and the last of the grapes. She ate the cheese on the stairs
and did not taste it. Her room was at the end of the hall, the door she had put
a lock on herself when she was fifteen and her mother had let her, which was
either trust or not caring and Addie had never decided which.

Immediately after arriving in her room, she locks the door, opens her computer,
and starts looking up "Black Rocks" on the internet.

She did not take her jacket off first. She sat down at the desk with it on and
the laptop woke up under her hand and she typed the two words into the bar and
hit enter before she had decided to, and only then did she reach into the left
pocket and take the necklace out and set it on the desk beside the keyboard, in
the light of the screen.

It lay there in a loop. The wooden beads. The cord gone dark. The black stone
in the middle of them like a hole.

There was a ton of information relating to black stones and different rocks.

Onyx. Jet. Tourmaline. Something called shungite that she had never heard of and
that a woman in a video was very excited about. Basalt. Coal, which made her
laugh once, out loud, in the quiet room, and then stop. Pages and pages of it,
crystal shops and rock-hound forums and a museum in Arizona, and she scrolled
with one hand and held the stone up to the screen with the other, turning it,
comparing.

She pulled up a picture, and placed the rock next to it.

A black glass, the picture said. Volcanic. Formed when lava cooled too fast to
grow a crystal in. Sharp enough when it broke that people had made knives out
of it before there was iron. In the picture it was a rough chunk with a fresh
face that caught the light in a curve like the inside of a shell, and inside
the curve were the same tiny frozen-smoke lines she had seen under the pendant
lamp at Ugly Mug.

"AH HA! This looks like it could be a piece of Obsidian," she exclaimed with
joy.

It was a small joy and it went a long way. She had a word for the thing now.
That was something. That was a place to stand.

"The letter said to never take it off. I wonder why that is."

She looked at the loop of beads on the desk. She thought about the block
capitals on the half-sheet, pressed hard, blue ballpoint. *Do not take this off
under any circumstance.* And she had, or she had made him, and then she had
walked off with it, and it was here on her desk two miles from where it was
supposed to be, and she found she was not laughing anymore.

She decided to dig a little deeper, and see what could be the reasoning.

It took her twenty minutes to get past the shops. Everybody wanted to sell her
one. But under the shops there were the other pages, the ones with the black
backgrounds and the purple writing, and she went down into them the way you go
down a stair you do not entirely trust.

According to the research she found on the internet, "Obsidian is used as
protection against negative energies, and blocks psychic attacks."

She read it twice. She read the page it was on, and the next one, and the one
after that, and they all said some version of it in some version of the purple
writing. A shield. A mirror that showed you the truth and sent the rest back
where it came from. A stone for people who needed to not be found by the wrong
kind of looking. She did not believe any of it, and she read all of it, and she
kept the necklace in the light of the screen the whole time and did not once
look away from it for long.

She wondered "why would there be a note that says do not take this off?" "Is
someone after him?"

The question came up in her the way the sick had come up on the sidewalk, all at
once and from underneath. *Is someone after him.* A boy who had been in a box
for eighteen years. A note with no name. Four letters in a hand that had been
taught to hide. A waitress who could not look at him and could not stop looking
at her.

Her phone buzzed on the desk. She jumped like the desk had bitten her.

Felix. Two texts, a minute apart, the second one all capitals.

*do you have it*

*ADDIE. THE NECKLACE.*

She stared at them. She could see him at his kitchen table, the folder open, his
hand going to his throat and finding hoodie. She thumbed back before the guilt
could finish arriving: *I have it. It's safe. I'm so sorry. Don't panic. Give me
an hour, I'm finding something out. Call you when I'm done.* She watched the
dots appear and stop and appear and stop, and then: *ok. an hour. keep it on
you.*

*Keep it on you.* She did not put it on. She did not know why she did not. She
set the phone face down on the desk and picked the stone back up.

Her mind kept racing. Hundreds of thoughts kept coming across her mind, and she
could not find an answer.

Who wrote the note. Who sealed the envelope, and with what, and why in a
language that was not one. Why the Knights, who labeled their cabinets, had
never once said the word. Why the waitress. Why the sidewalk. Why her mother's
car was gone on a Thursday night and the kitchen smelled like a match and there
was butter on a saucer by the door. None of it went together and all of it sat
in the same place in her chest, and the thoughts went around and around it like
the rock forums went around and around obsidian, everybody sure, nobody right.

She kept examining the piece of Obsidian.

Under the desk lamp it was blacker than the screen. The frozen-smoke lines went
deep into it and stopped, the way you can see a little way into dark water and
no further. It was warm. It had been warm on the desk for an hour with nobody
touching it and it was warm now in her palm, warmer than a rock had any business
being, and she told herself that was the lamp.

There was a faint engraving on it that almost resembling a fork.

She tilted it into the light until the mark came up out of the black — the
stem, the two tines at the top, cut so fine and so clean that it had to have
been done with something harder than the stone, by somebody who had done it
before. It was not a fork. She had known that at the table. It was too careful
to be a fork. It was a mark that meant something to whoever cut it, and she
turned to the laptop and started to look.

Addie kept digging into the research and discovered that it was an ancient rune
used as protection against enemies.

That took her to eleven and a half. She went through alphabets she had never
heard of, rows of little angular letters that all looked like sticks, and she
held the stone up next to each one and none of them was it, and then one of
them nearly was — a stem, two branches — and the page under it said what it was
for. Protection. Against enemies. Against the ones who came to do you harm. Put
it on a shield, the page said, or on a door, or on the thing you carried, and
they would not find you, or they would find you and lose their nerve.

She sat back.

"Now why would you need a stone that protected against negative energies, and a
carving that allegedly protects you against enemies," she wondered.

Belt and suspenders. Two locks on one door. Somebody had wanted this to work
very badly, and had put on it everything they knew how to put, and had sealed
it in wax and written *at all times* on a piece of paper and given it to a
four-year-old.

*Is someone after him.*

She looked at the phone, face down. She looked at the window. From the desk she
could see a strip of the street between the blinds, one of the two streetlights
and the dark under it, and the sidewalk under the dark, and it was empty, and
she looked at it anyway for longer than it took to see that.

She got up and went to the window and looked down at the drive. Empty. A
quarter to ten. Her text to her mother sat on the phone in her hand, delivered
two hours ago and answered by nobody — her mother, who answered *text me* in
under a minute every time, who had made her promise on the stairs. Addie stood
at the blinds with the necklace in her other hand. Across the street the
Guidrys' upstairs lamp went off, and the dark under the far streetlight was only
dark, and she made herself look at it until she was sure.

This was all too strange for Addie, and she didn't know of anyone that would
have any answers.

Her mother would have said *come downstairs and eat something.* Felix would have
said *I hear you* and not heard her. The Knights would have said nothing at all,
the way they had said nothing for eighteen years. There was nobody. There was a
girl in a locked room with a rock and a laptop and a jacket she still had not
taken off, and below her a house that smelled of sage and butter and honey, and
outside it a driveway where her mother's car should have been and was not, and
in her hand a thing that someone, once, had made with everything they knew, so
that a boy she loved would not be found.

She put it down on the desk beside the laptop, where she could see it. She left
the lamp on. She got up and checked the lock on the door, which she knew was
locked, and it was.

Then she sat back down, and pulled the laptop toward her, and went looking for
whoever might have carved it, without the first idea what she was looking
for.
