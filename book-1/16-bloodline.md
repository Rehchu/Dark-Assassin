# Bloodline

Anatole had them put in a room with a candle.

It was off the hall, one of the side vaults, with a curtain for a door and two
pallets on the floor and a shelf with a jug on it, and a square of tin nailed
to the brick over the shelf that had been polished until it gave back a face,
more or less. The candle stood in a saucer on the shelf under the tin. It was
the only light. Odile had lit it, and looked at the two of them, and gone out,
and the curtain had fallen, and beyond it the hall had gone on being a hall —
low voices, a child crying once and being hushed, men's feet going to the
curtain at the street and coming back.

Felix sat on the pallet with his knee out straight and his back against the
brick.

He had not said anything since the street. Addie had let him not say it
through the curtain and across the hall and into this room, and now she sat
down on the other pallet with the pack between her feet and her wrapped wrist
in her lap, and looked at him, and waited, the way she had waited on the levee.

"I'll tell you," he said. "I said I would."

"I know."

"I don't know how to start it."

"Start with the table."

He started with the table.

---

"She leaned down," he said. "You'd turned around. You were drinking. She
leaned down like she was going to pick up a napkin and she put her mouth
right — " He touched the side of his head, the hair there. "Right at my ear.
Like this. Close. And she said it low, so it was just for me, and it took her
about as long as it took you to drink."

"What did she say."

He looked at the candle.

"*He'll tell you never to take it off,*" Felix said. "That's what she said
first. Before we'd even heard of the pawn shop. She said: *He'll tell you
never to take it off. He's wrong, baby.*" His voice was flat. He was reading
it off the inside of his own skull, where it had been for four days. "*Take it
off some night, out under the sky, and find out what those ears are for. I'll
hear you.*"

The candle burned.

"That's it," he said. "That's all of it. Every word. Then she stood up and I
said *I'll have to check it out,* because I didn't — I didn't know what else a
person says, Addie, when a waitress says a thing like that to him in a coffee
shop at eleven in the morning, and then you turned around and she said Gold
and Silver, Main Street, and we went."

Addie did not say anything for a while.

"*Those ears,*" she said finally.

"Yeah."

"That's why you didn't tell me."

"Yeah."

"Because to tell me you'd have to say it."

"Nobody says it." He was looking at the tin over the shelf, not at her. "In
eighteen years. Not my parents. Not you. Not one teacher, not one kid on a bus
in Ireland, not the guy at the pawn shop who looked at them like they were the
sun. Nobody. They look and they look away and they don't say it, and I've spent
my whole life inside that, and then a woman I'd known for one day put her mouth
at my ear and said the word like it was — like it was the *point.*" He
swallowed. "And I couldn't say it to you. I couldn't. I got to the pawn shop
and he said *keep it on* and I thought, she said he would. She knew. And I
still couldn't."

"Felix."

"What."

"Your ears."

He looked at her.

"Your *ears,*" Addie said. "I'm saying it. Four years. I trained myself not to
look, I did it on purpose, freshman year, in the van, I decided I wasn't going
to be one of the people who looked, and I did such a good job that I never once
said it out loud, and that was *wrong,* that was the wrong thing to do, and I'm
saying it now. Your ears are pointed. They've always been pointed. I love you
and your ears are pointed and I saw hers."

"You saw —"

"Down there. When her hair moved." She held his eyes. "They're the same as
yours."

---

Anatole came in at three.

He came through the curtain slow, the way he did everything, with a lamp in
one hand and his other hand empty, and he looked at the two of them on the
pallets and at the candle and he did not sit down, and Felix understood from
the way he stood that whatever the man had come to say he had been deciding
whether to say it the whole way across the hall.

"She let you go," Anatole said.

"Yes."

"Armand said." He set the lamp on the shelf beside the candle. "He said it to
my people at the curtain and went back down her way and hasn't come up. He's
angry. I've seen him angry. It's not — " He stopped. "That's mine to carry.
Not yours. I came to say a thing and I'm going to say it."

"Then say it," Felix said. He heard himself sound like Anatole, and did not
care.

Anatole looked at the tin mirror. He looked at it a while.

"You asked me at my table who she is," he said. "I said *her.* I said we don't
say the name, and that's true, we don't, and I said Delvaux should be the one
to tell you the rest, and that was true too, and it was also a man hiding
behind a rule, and I've been doing that a long time in front of a lot of people
and I'm done doing it tonight." He turned. "You're of her line, son. You know
that. Alcide told you you were hers, and Ti-Jean told you you were the one,
and you've had four days of people looking at the sides of your head. You
know. I'm just going to say it plain."

"Say it."

"She's your mother."

The candle burned.

"The man behind the rail's your father. You've had that since my table. This
is the other half. The woman under the square with the cloth on the walls —
the one who served you coffee, Ti-Jean says, up top, in an *apron,* which she
has never once done in a hundred and fifty years of my people's memory — she
carried you. She had you down here. Delvaux took you out from under her one
night in the rain when you were four, with her own charm on your neck, and
gave you to Jan, and Jan gave you to the Knights, and she's been looking for
you every night since." A breath. "That's who *she* is."

Felix did not move.

He had known. That was the thing. Anatole was right; he had known since the
pawn shop, since *you are hers,* since a boy in a doorway said *the one she's
been waiting for* — he had had it in him for days the way you have a splinter,
a thing under the skin you keep the hand away from. And now a tired man with a
lamp had said it in a room with a tin mirror, and it was out, and it was not
different from having it in. It was only louder.

"What is she," he said.

Anatole sat, then. On the floor, with his back to the brick and his big hands
on his knees, the way the old men sat in the hall.

"I'll tell you what my grandmother told me," he said. "It's what we've got."

---

"There's a line," Anatole said. "A blood. It came across the water a long
time ago — before the French, before the town, before the town under the town.
It came in a woman, in the hold of a boat, and it walked up out of the swamp
into this parish, and it's been here ever since. Her mother's mothers, she
says. That's the only way she ever says it."

He looked at the candle.

"They had ways, the old ones. My grandmother said her grandmother's grandmother
remembered — remembered the ways, or remembered being told. Most of it's gone.
Lost. What's left is two things, and two things is enough." He held up a
finger, the way he had for the truce. "The poison. That's a craft. She brews.
She's got a belt of it and there's not a doctor up top who could name one vial
of what's on it. That's the small thing." A second finger. "And the other.
The one we don't have a good word for down here, so we say *the shadow,*
because that's what it looks like when you see it, and God help you if you
see it."

"What does it —"

"It comes up off the floor." Anatole said it plainly. "That's what my
grandmother said and that's what I saw, once, when I was nine, at the far end
of the street, before the truce was renewed in my father's time. It comes up
off the floor for her like a dog. Slow. And her eyes go red — not *flash,* not
like a story — they go red the way a coal goes red, and then somebody she's
put her hand on sits down against a wall and doesn't get up." He stopped. "It
took my people one at a time for eighty years before my grandmother went down
and made the truce. One every few months, on the old street, going for water.
That's what it does. That's all of what we know it does. There's more. She
doesn't show us more."

Felix's hand was at his collar. He did not know when it had gone there.

"And it's in you," Anatole said.

"No."

"Son —"

"*No.* I've never — I don't — nothing comes up off any *floor* for me, I'm a —
I write about *buses,* I —"

"It's in you the way her face is in you." Anatole did not raise his voice. "The
line breeds true. It always has. Every one of them, back as far as we
remember, the same look — tall, mostly, and dark, and the eyes go red, and the
ears." He looked at the side of Felix's head, and did not look away, the way
everyone in the world looked away. "They all have them. Every one of that
blood. It's the mark of it. She wears her hair down over hers, has since
before I was born; my grandmother said her mother did the same. And you wear
yours long." He said it gently. "You've worn it long since you were a boy, I'd
guess. Because nobody had to tell you."

Felix put both hands over his face.

"That's what she meant," Addie said. Very quietly. "*Find out what those ears
are for.*"

"That's what she meant."

"She wants him to take it off."

"She wants him to take it off out under the sky where she can see him do it,
and she wants him to find out what he is when he does, and she's not wrong
that he'd find out." Anatole stood. He picked up his lamp. "I'll say one more
thing and then I'll leave you. That charm's Alcide's best work and it's hers,
and Delvaux put it on you for a reason, and I'd keep it on. I'd keep it on if
the room was burning. That's not the truce talking. That's a man who saw a
thing at nine years old."

He went to the curtain.

"Jan comes down when her shift ends," he said, with his back to them. "Seven,
eight. She always does when there's trouble, and there's trouble. And at midday
I'll take you to the rail myself, by the river way, the way I said Friday I
would, because I'm done being a man who says things. Sleep if you can."

The curtain fell.

---

He could not sleep.

Addie did, finally, near five, on the other pallet with her wrapped wrist
against her chest and the pack under her head, and he sat with his back to the
brick and his knee out and watched the candle and did not think, because
thinking was the thing he could not do with what was in the room.

*She's your mother.*

A woman on a floor with her hands open. A voice at his ear. A face he had
looked at across a counter, and across a table, and across a room, and had
never once *seen* — because he had been looking at a waitress, and there had
been a waitress to look at, and now there was not.

His mother had brought him a mug of coffee and set it down without a sound and
he had drunk it.

He was cold.

He noticed it the way he noticed things down here, late. His left wrist. Not
the hand — the wrist, the inside of it, where the veins were, a cold spot the
size of a coin that had not been there a minute ago and was there now, the way
a draft is there, the way a hand is there before it touches. He rubbed it. It
did not go. It went deeper, if anything, a cold that was under the skin and
not on it, pooling, the way water pools in a low place, and he looked down at
his own wrist in the candlelight and could not see anything and could feel it
like ice.

He was angry. He had not known he was angry until he felt the cold, and then
he knew it — a plain hard anger with no bottom to it, at a woman on a floor,
at a man behind a rail, at a stranger in the rain and a folder in a closet and
a lifetime of people looking at the sides of his head and not saying, and it
came up in him all at once the way the cold had, from underneath, and it was
*his,* and he did not want it, and he could not put it down.

The candle bent.

He watched it happen. The flame, which had stood up straight in its saucer for
three hours in a room with no wind in it, leaned. Toward him. Not toward the
curtain, not toward the draft that had lifted his mother's hair in a room under
the square — toward *him,* where he sat against the brick with his knee out and
his wrist going cold, as if he were a window somebody had opened, as if
something in the room had turned to look at him and the flame had turned with
it.

He stopped breathing.

The flame stayed bent. A second. Two. The cold in his wrist went down into the
bones of his hand and he felt, under the anger, under everything, a thing
*move* — not in the room. In him. Low, behind the breastbone, in the place
where Addie put her hand when she was sick, a slow heavy turning-over like an
animal changing its position in its sleep, and it was not the fear, and it was
not his heart, and it had been there his whole life, and it had never once
moved before.

His hand went to the necklace.

He gripped it through his shirt. The stone under his palm, warm, against the
cold. He gripped it the way the man in the rain must have gripped it at the
edge of a drain, with his whole hand, and he thought *no,* and he thought *keep
it on,* and he thought *I'd keep it on if the room was burning* —

— and the thing in him settled. Slowly. The way it had moved. The animal
turning back over, and going still, and the cold in his wrist stopping where it
was and going no deeper, and the flame on the shelf straightening, one degree
at a time, until it stood up in its saucer the way a candle stands, and gave
its light to the room, and did not lean.

He was shaking.

Across the room Addie was awake. She was up on one elbow on the pallet with
her eyes open, and she had seen it. He could tell she had seen it. She was
looking at the candle, and then at him, and then at the candle, and she did not
say anything, and her face was the face from the sidewalk.

"Don't," he said. "Don't say it."

She did not say it.

---

He got up at six. He did not know why. His knee took his weight and hated
him for it, and he crossed the little room to the shelf with the jug on it and
the candle in its saucer and the square of tin nailed over them, and he looked.

A face, more or less. The tin was old and the polish was uneven and it gave
him back a boy in a hoodie with his hair down over the sides of his head,
short, heavy, tired to the bone, a stranger's face that he had been shaving in
mirrors for four years without once looking at the sides of.

He looked at the sides of it.

He pushed the hair back with both hands and held it there, off his ears, and
looked at them in the tin by candlelight, for the first time in his life, on
purpose.

They were pointed. They had always been pointed. The tops of them came up
past where a top should stop, not much, a quarter inch, a curve gone a little
too far — the way hers had, under the black hair, when the draft lifted it.
Her ears. His. The line breeds true. He stood there holding his own hair back
like a man holding back a curtain and looked at the mark of a blood that came
across the water in the hold of a boat, and it was on him, it had been on him
in every school picture in a shoebox in a hall closet, and Diane Knight had
put those pictures in the box and never once said the word.

He let the hair down.

And in the tin, for one breath, before it fell — his eyes.

Green. They were green; they had always been green. But at the edges of the
green, in the uneven light off old metal, the color had gone *dark* — a rim,
thin, at the very outside of the iris, not red, not anything he could have
named if you had asked him, just a darkness where there should have been a
line of green meeting white, as if something under the color had come up close
enough to the surface to be seen through it.

One breath.

Then the hair was down and the candle was straight and his eyes in the tin were
a tired boy's eyes, green, ordinary, and the cold in his wrist was only cold.

He stood there with his hands on the shelf.

*Find out what those ears are for.*

He had found out. Not all of it. Enough. He had found out that there was a
thing in him that a candle could see, and that it moved when he was angry, and
that a rock on a string was the only reason it had gone back to sleep, and that
his mother had wanted him to take the rock off out under the sky exactly so
that it would not.

He turned around.

Addie was sitting up on the pallet with the pack in her lap. She had been
watching him at the mirror the whole time and she did not pretend she had not.

"He said midday," Felix said. "By the river way."

"Yes."

"I want to go now."

She looked at him.

"I'm not asking you," he said. "I'm not asking Anatole. I'm not waiting for
your mother or for the truce or for whatever she's going to do when she's
decided tonight wasn't enough. I've been pushed down every stair in this
place, Addie, by you and by her and by a man in the rain, and I'm done being
pushed." He touched his collar. The stone was there. "He's four hundred yards
away. He's been four hundred yards away since I was four years old. I want to
see him."

Addie got up. She put the pack on, both straps.

"Then let's go find Anatole," she said, "and tell him it's midday."
