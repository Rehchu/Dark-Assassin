# Riverbend

He had had the folder open and closed since seven.

Open: the papers on top, ALEXANDRIA ORPHANAGE in bold, his name typed under it
the way it was typed on everything, *Felix Robert Knight,* and the date that
made him four. The half-sheet with the note. The three envelopes he did not
touch, lying in their row with the wax he did not look at. Closed: the folder
flat on the kitchen table with his hand on top of it, the way he had sat at the
table in Ugly Mug, and the window with no curtains letting in a gray Saturday
that could not decide what it wanted to be.

Open. Closed. Open.

He had checked the beads under his shirt four times since he woke up. He knew
it was four because he had started counting after the second, as a way of not
doing it, and it had not worked.

Diane had texted twice since Thursday. *Call me, honey.* Then, Friday night,
late: *call me honey. please.* No period on the first one. A period on the
second. He knew his mother's texting the way he knew her handwriting and the
period was her standing in the kitchen with the phone in both hands.

He had not called.

There was a thing he was not thinking about. He could feel the shape of it,
the way you can feel a step in the dark before your foot finds it. A woman's
mouth at his ear in a coffee shop, warm and low and close, and a word in it
that he had not let himself get as far as, and would not now, and was not.

His phone buzzed on the table. Addie, 9:40.

*10. Riverbend. Bring the folder — the office might have records. Shoes you
can walk in.*

He did not want to bring the folder. He looked at it for a while. Then he put
it under his hoodie, flat against his stomach, and zipped the hoodie over it,
because she had said to, and because he could not think of a single place in
this apartment where he would rather it was.

---

She was at the curb with the spare helmet held out.

"Slow," he said.

"I'll drive like your mom."

She did. That was the thing. She rode down the side street and out onto Jackson
at exactly the speed limit, both hands on the grips, her back straight under
his arms, and he could feel her being careful the whole way, and it was worse
than Friday. Friday she had not been thinking. Today she was. Today she thought
it mattered.

Jackson Street to the river road. The levee came up on their left like a long
green wall with the sky sitting on top of it, and the smell of the river came
over it, mud and something older than mud. Warm again. Overcast, close, not a
leaf moving. The road ran along the foot of the levee past the last of
downtown and then there was no more downtown, just the road and the grass bank
and the town on the right going from brick to vinyl.

The road dipped. A low place, a hundred feet of it, where the pavement went
down toward a storm drain set in the curb — an iron grate with last week's
leaves caught in it, dry, a candy wrapper, a dead frog gone flat. She slowed
into it and came up out of it and shouted back over her shoulder, the words
torn up by the wind:

"That floods."

He looked at the grate going by. It was a grate.

---

Three buildings.

Brick on the bottom, vinyl on top, three stories each, with white vinyl
balconies stuck on the front of them like drawers somebody had left open.
A, B, C, in big letters on the ends. A flag on a pole in front of the first
one, hanging dead. A strip of landscaping between the buildings and the
parking lot, young trees in mulch rings, a bench nobody had sat on. And behind
all of it, close, the levee.

That was Riverbend.

He had seen it before. Not this one — all of them. He had written up a hundred
places like this from a hundred bus windows, in his own plain words, for the
blog: *the complex on the edge of town that looks like the complex on the edge
of every town.* He had seen these exact balconies in Cork, Ireland, on a
building by the river, and had written that they looked like drawers. There
was nothing to look at here. That was the first thing that got to him. A place
with his name on a piece of paper under his hoodie, and it looked like
nothing.

Addie parked on the Building C side, where the lot ran up against the strip.
She locked both helmets to the bike with the cable. She looked at him.

"Office first," she said. "Records."

"Records of what?"

"Of you."

---

The leasing office was in A, on the ground floor, with SATURDAY 10–2 on the
glass in white letters and a bell on the door. Inside it was carpet and a
desk and a rack of brochures and a bowl of mints, and a girl behind the desk
with a phone in her hand who put the phone down when the bell rang and smiled
like she meant it.

"Hey y'all. Looking at units?"

"Not exactly," Addie said.

Her name was Kayla. She was maybe twenty-five and she was bored and she was
kind, and both things were all the way through her, and Felix liked her before
she had finished the smile. She had a Riverbend polo and a coffee with her name
on the cup.

The plaque was on the wall by the door. He saw it while Addie was talking.
Brass, the size of a sheet of paper, screwed into the drywall at eye level,
with the developer's logo at the bottom and, above it, in the raised letters
people used for things they wanted to sound like history:

*On this site stood the Alexandria Orphanage, operated by the Sisters —*

He stopped reading. He did not need the rest. He had the same words in bold on
a piece of paper against his stomach, and here they were on a wall, and it was
the first time in his life he had seen them anywhere but in his own hands.

"— any records," Addie was saying. "From when it was the orphanage. Files.
Anything."

"Oh, no, hon. Nothing like that." Kayla shook her head. "The building came
down before the developer even bought the lot. Years before. Whatever files
there were went to the diocese, I think, or Catholic Charities in Baton Rouge?
You'd have to write. Like, an actual letter." She looked from Addie to Felix
and back. "Why, what are y'all looking for?"

Felix unzipped the hoodie. He took the folder out and opened it and showed her
the top of the first page. Only the top. The header, and his name under it. He
had his hand over the rest, and he did not take the envelopes out, and he did
not show her the wax. *Do not show them around like you showed me.*

Kayla looked at it. Her face did the thing faces did.

"Oh, honey," she said.

"Yeah."

"Oh, *honey.*" She put her coffee down. "Okay. Okay, so I don't have anything,
I really don't, but —" She thought. She was a person who liked to have
something to give. "Okay, two things. One, C is built on the old foundation.
The developer saved money. That's why it floods — every time it rains hard,
the basement in C. Maintenance is always down there. Like, always. So if
there's anything old, it's under C. But it's just a basement."

"And two?"

"Two is dumb." She picked the coffee back up. "There's a lady comes by. Every
few months. Says she's thinking about a unit in C, and I show her one, and she
looks at it real careful, and she never takes it. Been doing it since before I
started. Reading glasses on a string. Gray in her hair, here." She touched her
own temples. "Nice lady. Sad, kind of. I always figured she had a kid here or
something. Back when." She shrugged. "I don't know why I said that. It's not
records."

Addie had gone quiet.

Felix saw it. He saw her go quiet the way you see a light go off in a window
across a street — not the dark, just the difference. She was looking at the
brochure rack. She did not say anything. He did not know why, and he did not
have room in him right then to find out, and he let it go by.

"Thank you," he said to Kayla. "Really."

"Y'all come back if you want to see a unit," she said, and picked up her phone.

---

In the lot, Addie held out her hand.

"I'll carry that. You keep losing things."

He gave her the folder. She put it in her backpack, flat against the back
panel, and zipped it, and put the pack on both shoulders and pulled the straps
tight, and it did not come out again that day. He watched it go and was
relieved, and felt the relief, and was ashamed of it, and kept both.

"I'm going to call my mom," he said.

Addie walked ten feet off and stood with her back to him and did not pretend
well.

Diane picked up on the first ring.

She was crying before he said anything. He could hear it — the breath she
took, the way she said "Felix" like it was a word she had been practicing. And
then she apologized for crying. She always did. She would cry at a commercial
and apologize to the television.

"I'm sorry, honey. I'm sorry. I'm so —"

"Mama. It's okay. I just — I need to ask you some things. Can I just ask you
some things?"

"Yes." A breath. "Yes."

He asked them plain. He was standing beside a motorcycle in a parking lot with
a levee behind him and he asked his mother, who had labeled the cabinet doors,
whether he was adopted, and she said yes, and he asked when, and she said
"You were four, honey. Almost four. It was August," and he asked how.

"A man brought you."

"What man?"

"I don't know his name. He didn't — he wouldn't say. He came to the front door
in the rain. Your father says it was raining like the end of the world. He had
you inside his coat. He handed you to me." Her voice went. It came back. "He
handed you to me in the doorway and he said three things and he did not stay.
He would not come in. He said them and he went."

"What three things?"

She had them ready. He could hear that she had them ready, that she had said
them to herself in the kitchen for fourteen years and still could not say them
to him without stopping between each one.

"*Keep it with him.*" A breath. "*Never tell him until he leaves home.*" A
breath. "*When he leaves, give him the box.*"

Felix put his hand flat on the seat of the bike.

"Keep what with me."

"The necklace. It came in that envelope, honey, sealed, with the note inside.
We never opened it. He said *with* you, so we kept it with you. Your father put
it in the folder with your papers so it would never get separated from them.
It's been in that folder in that box in the hall closet since the night you
came."

"Did you know what it was?"

"No." Simple. "We were told to keep it with you. We kept it with you."

"Why didn't you make me wear it?"

"He said *with* you, honey." She said it like a woman reading a line off a card
she had kept in her wallet. "We did what he said. We did exactly what he said."

He closed his eyes.

"And the papers. Who did the papers."

"A woman from the orphanage. She did all of it — the forms, the court, all of
it, so it would be real. It is real, Felix. You're ours. It's all — it's all
real." A breath. "She asked us not to keep her name. So we didn't. We never
wrote it down. Your father thinks she goes to our church."

"You think she goes to your church."

"Your father does. I don't look. I never let myself look."

There was a silence with the river in it.

"Are you wearing it?" Diane said.

"Yes."

"Good. Good." Then, in a rush: "Come to church tomorrow. Come to lunch. Please.
Your father's making the roast. Please, honey."

"I'll try."

In the background he heard his father say something, orderly and low, the way
he said things — *tell him we love him, tell him the papers are in the safe if
he needs the originals* — and Diane said "Robert says —" and Felix said "I
heard him. Tell him okay," and she said "I love you," and he said it back, and
meant it, and hung up, and stood there with the phone in his hand and the lot
in front of him.

Addie came back. She did not ask.

He told her anyway. The three things, flat, in order, the way Diane had said
them. *Keep it with him. Never tell him until he leaves home. When he leaves,
give him the box.* A man in the rain. A woman from the orphanage who did not
want her name kept. He said it to the levee.

"Okay," Addie said, after a while.

The kindest people he knew had kept a box in a hall closet for fourteen years
because a stranger at the front door in the rain had told them to. His whole
childhood, every alphabetized shelf of it, had been a set of instructions
somebody else wrote. He touched the beads through his shirt. He was angrier at
the man in the rain than at his parents, a lot angrier, and he did not know why
that was, and it made him angrier.

---

"I'm going to call mine," Addie said.

She walked. Down the strip behind C, along the mulch rings and the young trees,
with the phone at her ear and her free hand in her jacket pocket, and Felix
stood by the bike and heard her half of it come back to him in pieces off the
brick.

"Mom. The unit you looked at at Riverbend. When was that?"

A pause.

"Because I'm standing in it. In the lot."

She stopped walking.

"In the *what?*"

A long pause. He watched her back.

"You meant *what?*"

She hung up. She stood there a second with the phone down at her side. Then she
came back up the strip, and her face was white, all the way white, the freckles
standing out on it like somebody had flicked a brush.

"She said don't go in the basement," Addie said. "Just like that. I said I was
in the lot and she said *don't go in the basement.* Like — like she'd been
waiting to say it." She swallowed. "Then she said she meant the flooding. That
the buildings flood. That she'd heard that."

"How does your mom know it has a basement?"

Addie did not answer.

It was the first time in four years he had seen her not want to know
something. He saw it happen — the thing she did to other people, the pushing,
turning around in her and finding nothing to push on — and he did not say
anything, because he did not know what you said.

---

They walked the lot the way you walk a place looking for a thing you cannot
name.

Behind C, where the landscaping gave up and the grass ran to the foot of the
levee, he found it. A line. The concrete slab of the building ended, and past
its edge, half-buried in the grass, there was brick — old brick, soft-edged,
laid in a line that did not stop where the building stopped. It kept going.
Twenty feet past the wall of C, then a corner, then back toward the levee. A
footprint in the ground, bigger than the building standing on it, and the
wrong shape.

He crouched and put his hand on it. The brick was cool and damp.

"Somebody built here before the orphanage," he said. It came out of him the
way things did when he was not scared enough to stop them. "Before the city
got out this far. This is way older than nuns. And then the nuns built on top
of it. And then somebody built on top of the nuns." He looked up at the vinyl
balconies. "It's all just — sitting on each other."

Addie took a picture of it. Then another, closer, with her boot in the frame
for scale.

"I want to go home," he said.

"Kayla said maintenance is always down there."

"I heard her."

"The office closes at two."

"I know what time the office closes."

She did not push. She just stood there with the phone in her hand and the
levee behind her, and she waited, and the waiting was worse than the pushing,
and he had known it would be.

---

The stairwell of Building C was ordinary. That was the whole trouble with it.

A woman coming down with a laundry basket on her hip, who said "'scuse me" and
did not look at them. A kid's bike leaning against the wall under the first
flight, pink, with streamers. A bulletin board with a flyer for a lost cat. The
smell of somebody's lunch.

Under the stair, a door.

MAINTENANCE, on a plastic sign screwed into it at an angle. A steel door,
painted the color of the wall. A hasp, and a padlock in the hasp — and the
padlock was not locked. It hung open on the staple, its shackle lifted, the way
a lock hangs when somebody has come out and meant to come back.

"It should be locked," Addie said.

She lifted it off. It was that easy. She held it in her hand and looked at it
and put it in her jacket pocket, and pulled the door.

Concrete steps, going down. Eight of them he could see, and then the dark took
the rest. The air that came up out of it was cool and wet, the way air is under
a house, and under the wet there was something else — burnt, and sweet, a smell
he knew from somewhere and could not put his finger on, like a kitchen where
someone had cooked with herbs and then put the fire out.

He got his phone out and turned the light on and pointed it down.

On the third step, a bootprint.

Wet. Drying at the edges, the concrete going pale around it, the center still
dark. Bigger than his foot. A lot bigger than hers. And it was pointing up.
Toward them. Somebody had come up these steps out of that dark not long ago in
wet boots, and had gone out this door, and had left the lock hanging open on
the staple because they were coming back.

Below the reach of the light, the dark did a thing.

He could not have said what. Nothing moved. It was more like the way a person
who has been standing still in a room decides to stand stiller when they hear
you on the stairs. And under that — far down, or not far, he could not tell — a
sound. One sound. Not water. Not a pipe. A sound like a weight being set down
on stone by somebody who did not want it heard.

Felix pulled the door to.

"Not now," he said.

"Felix."

"Not now."

"There's nobody down there," Addie said. "It's a basement. It's a maintenance
guy and a water heater."

"There's somebody down there."

He had his hand flat on the steel of the door. He did not know which of them
was right. He thought, standing there with the kid's bike and the lost-cat
flyer and his palm on a door that said MAINTENANCE, that they might both be.

---

At two o'clock the lights in the leasing office went off.

They watched it from the bike. Kayla came out with her coffee and her keys and
locked the glass door and got into a little white car and drove out past the
flag and turned left onto the river road, and the lot went quiet in the way
lots went quiet on Saturday afternoons, a car every few minutes, a door
somewhere, and then nothing.

"We wait till it's emptier," Addie said. "And we go down with the phone lights."

"Or we go home."

"Or," she said, and she turned around on the seat of the bike and looked at
him, "we sit on the levee for an hour, and *you* decide. Not me. You. I've been
deciding all week. I decided the coffee shop and I decided the pawn shop and I
decided this. I'm done. It's your name on the paper." She got off the bike.
"An hour. Then you tell me what we're doing."

He looked at Building C. At the stairwell door, propped with a brick, and past
it, where he could not see, the door under the stair with no lock on it.

Then he got off the bike, and they walked up the back of the levee together,
through the long grass, to the top, and sat down in the gray light with the
river brown and wide and slow below them and the whole lot laid out at their
backs — the three buildings, the flag, the bike where it was, the door where
it was, open.

He put his hand up to his collar and checked the beads under his shirt.

Five.
