# The Unlocked Basement

He decided at a quarter past three.

They had sat on the levee for an hour and then another twenty minutes, and the
river had gone by below them the whole time without caring, brown and wide and
slow, a barge somewhere upstream sounding its horn once and not again. The sky
had stayed the color of a sheet. Addie had not said a word. It had cost her
something, not saying it, and she had paid, and she had watched him the whole
time out of the side of her eye, the way you watch a pot.

"We look," Felix said finally. "At the basement. That's all. We look at it, we
don't go through any door, we don't go down any stairs, and if there's a guy
with a mop down there we say sorry and we leave."

"Okay."

"That's the whole thing. I'm not — that's it. Say okay again."

"Okay."

She meant it when she said it. That was the part she would come back to.

---

The stairwell was emptier than it had been at one-thirty. The pink bike was
gone. The lost-cat flyer had a corner loose and moved a little in no wind at
all, or in the draft that came up from under the stair, which she felt on her
ankles before she saw the door.

MAINTENANCE.

She took the padlock out of her jacket pocket. She looked at it in her hand —
a cheap brass thing, the kind you bought at the hardware store in a blister
pack, with the shackle lifted and the little keyhole staring up at her — and
she thought about putting it back on the staple, and then she thought about
the bootprint, and she put it back in her pocket.

Felix had his phone out with the light already on. His hand was steady. She
noticed that. His hand had not been steady on the counter at the pawn shop and
it was steady now, and she did not know if that was better or worse.

"Me first," he said.

"Why you?"

"Because it's my name on the paper." He pulled the door. "You said."

She had said. She let him go first.

The steps were concrete and there were fourteen of them. She counted because
she was going to have to come back up them. The bootprint on the third step had
dried to nothing, a pale place on the gray, and she stepped over it anyway, and
so did he. The air came up to meet them, cool and wet, and the burnt-sweet
smell came up with it, stronger with every step, and by the bottom she had a
name for it that she did not say out loud.

Then the floor, and Felix's light went out ahead of them into a room that did
not end where a room should.

---

It was big. That was the first wrong thing. Building C stood on top of it and
Building C was not small, but this went on past where Building C's walls would
have been — she could feel it more than see it, the light not finding a far
wall, the dark having a depth to it the way a swimming pool does when the
lights are off. The floor was concrete, poured rough, with a drain in the
middle and a dark stain around the drain where water had stood and gone down
and stood again. The ceiling was low. Pipes ran along it, new ones, white PVC,
and under the new pipes were old ones, iron, painted and flaking, going
nowhere anymore.

And the walls.

Concrete for the first four feet, gray, with a tidemark on it at knee height
where the floods had come and gone and left their line. And above the
concrete, brick. Old brick, soft-edged, the mortar between it crumbling, the
same brick she had photographed in the grass behind the building with her boot
in the frame. Somebody had poured a new floor and a new skirt of wall into a
room somebody else had built a long time before, and had not bothered to hide
it, because who was going to come down here.

A water heater in the near corner, humming. A shelf of paint cans. A mop in a
yellow bucket, dry. A folding chair. An extension cord that ran along the floor
and up into the ceiling and disappeared.

Nobody.

"Hello?" Felix said. His voice went out into the room and did not come back.

Nobody.

"It's a basement," Addie said.

"It's a big basement."

"It's a basement with a water heater and a mop." She heard herself and did not
believe herself and said it anyway. "You said we'd look. Let's look."

They looked.

He kept the light. She kept a hand on his sleeve, which she would not have done
a week ago and did not think about now. They went along the near wall, past the
water heater, past the shelf. There was a door in the wall, steel, painted, with
a lever handle, and it opened on a closet full of a boiler and nothing else. A
second door, the same, on an empty room the size of a car with a drain in it.
Then the wall went on, and the concrete skirt went on, and the old brick went
on above it, and the light showed them nothing but more of the same, and
Addie's boots on the rough floor were the loudest thing in the world.

The smell got stronger as they went.

She had a name for it. She had had it since the third step. It was the smell of
her mother's kitchen on Thursday night — that green burnt note, a match struck
and blown out, leaves on a fire — and under it now, close, the other thing,
sweet, thick, the thing that had been on the saucer by the back door. She did
not say the name. She walked toward it. It was the only thing in the basement
that was not concrete or brick or dark, and she went toward it the way you go
toward a lit window.

"Addie."

"What."

"The wall stopped."

She looked. His light was on the far wall — there was a far wall, after all,
forty feet from the stairs, the old brick coming all the way down to the floor
here with no concrete skirt over it, as if whoever poured the floor had run out
of concrete or run out of nerve before they got this far. Old brick, floor to
ceiling. A wall that had been standing here before the orphanage, before the
city, before anything with a name.

And in the middle of it, a door.

---

It was wood.

That was the whole of what was wrong with it, and it was enough. Everything
else down here was concrete or steel or iron or PVC, everything was the color of
things that did not care, and in the middle of the old brick wall was a door
made of wood, wide planks of it, gray-brown, with the grain standing up out of
it the way grain stands up out of wood that has been wet and dry and wet a
hundred times and has decided to live anyway. Cypress. She knew it the way she
knew live oak — you grew up here, you knew the wood things were made of. An
old, old cypress door, with iron straps across it gone black, and an iron ring
for a handle, hung in a frame set right into the brick.

No lock on this side. No hasp. No sign that said anything.

It was shut, and it fit its frame the way a door fits when it has been shut a
long time and opened a long time and shut again, and at the foot of it, along
the sill, on the concrete, there was a line of gray ash.

Ash, and in the ash a few dry leaves, curled, the silver gone out of them. And
beside the line, in the corner where the sill met the frame, a saucer. White.
Chipped. With a smear of something on it gone dark and hard, and the smell
coming up off it thick and sweet.

Addie stood and looked at it and the basement went very quiet around her.

"That's sage," Felix said. "That's — somebody burned sage down here."

"Yes."

"Why would somebody burn sage in a basement?"

She did not answer. She was looking at the saucer. She was thinking about a
white saucer from the good set on the sill of her mother's back door with a
curl of butter on it and a spoonful of honey, and her mother's car gone from
the drive, and her mother's voice on the phone three hours ago saying *don't
go in the basement* like a woman who had been waiting fourteen years to say
it.

*Some trick from her grandmother's side of the family.*

She had a feeling like a floor tilting. She put her hand on the brick beside
the door to stop it and the brick was cold and dry and old under her palm, and
did not help.

"Addie."

"I'm fine."

"You're not fine. You look like you did on the sidewalk."

"I'm *fine.*"

She was not. It had come up under her ribs the way it had on Thursday, the cold
turn, the wet mouth — fainter, this time, far off, like the same sickness felt
through a wall — and she did not know what it was and she was not going to
stand in a basement in front of a wooden door and try to find out. She breathed.
She looked at the door. She put her hand on the ring.

"You said we wouldn't."

"I said we wouldn't go *through* it."

"Addie —"

"A hand's width. That's all. I want to see what's on the other side of a wooden
door in a wall that's older than the city. Don't you? Don't you want to know
what it's a door *to?*"

He did not say no. That was the thing. He stood there with the light and he did
not say no, because he did want to know, and she saw it, and she pulled.

---

It was heavy.

It was heavier than a door had any right to be, and it did not swing — it
dragged, the bottom edge of it rubbing over the sill and the ash, and it moved
an inch, and then another, and then it went the way a heavy door goes once it
has decided, and she stopped it with her body when it had opened the width of
her hand.

The air came through.

Cold. Not cool, the way the basement was cool. Cold, the cold of stone that had
never once been in the sun, and moving, a slow steady breath of it pushing out
through the gap and past her face and on into the basement behind her, and it
smelled of the river. Of mud, and wet stone, and something older than mud, the
smell that came over the levee on a still day but stronger, closer, as if the
river were not on the other side of the levee at all but under it, under
everything, running along down there in the dark where nobody had put it.

Felix put the light through the gap.

A stair.

Brick, the first few steps, the same soft old brick as the wall, worn into a
dip in the middle of each tread by feet. And then, where the light gave out,
not brick anymore. Stone. Gray stone, cut, going down. Going down and turning,
the way a stair turns inside a tower, except that this one turned the wrong
way, down instead of up, around a core of the same stone, and the light went
down it six steps, eight, ten, and the dark took it, and the stair went on
where the light could not.

Neither of them said anything.

The cold breath came up out of it and moved her hair.

And then, from below — from far below, or not far, she could not tell, the
stair ate distance the way it ate light — a sound.

It was not water. She had been listening for water; there was water down there
somewhere, she could hear it, a thin far running sound like a tap left on in
another house. This was not that. This was a sound that a thing made when it
had been holding still and shifted its weight. One sound. Stone under
something heavy, the smallest grind of it, and then stillness so complete that
she could hear Felix not breathing beside her.

The light in his hand did not move. It was pointed down the stair. It was
shaking, very slightly, the circle of it trembling on the eighth step.

"Close it," he said.

She did not move.

"Addie. Close it."

She closed it.

It went the way it had opened, dragging, the sill scraping under it, and it
met its frame with a sound like a word, and the cold breath stopped, and the
basement was a basement again, a water heater and a mop and a smell of sage,
and her hand was still on the iron ring and she could not for a second make it
let go.

---

"Tomorrow," she said.

They were at the foot of the concrete steps. She did not remember walking back
across the floor. His light was pointed at the ground between them and his face
above it was the face from the pawn shop counter, the one that had stepped
back.

"Tomorrow," she said again. "With lights. Real ones, not phones. In the
daytime, like the man said. We come back and we —"

"No."

"Felix."

"No. You heard it. You heard it, Addie, don't tell me you didn't, there is
something at the bottom of that stair and it *moved,* and I am not going down
there tomorrow or any other day to find out what has a stair under an orphanage
and burns sage on the doormat. No." He was shaking. He was not hiding it. "I
don't want to know. Okay? I'm allowed to not want to know. It's my name on the
paper and I don't *want* to know what's down there. I want to go home."

She looked at him.

She wanted to know. She had wanted to know since Thursday and she wanted to
know now with her whole body, and she could see that he had gone the other way,
that whatever the stair had done to her it had done the opposite to him, and
that if she pushed now she would push him off something.

"Okay," she said. "Home."

"Okay."

She went up the fourteen steps behind him. At the top she took the padlock out
of her pocket and hung it back on the staple, open, the way they had found it,
and looked at it hanging there, and thought *whoever you are, I know you're
coming back,* and went out under the stair into the last of the light.

---

Dusk had come while they were down there. It came early now, the light going
gray and then going, and with it the wind had gotten up at last — the first
wind in two days, warm still but moving, pushing at the young trees in their
mulch rings and lifting the flag on the pole in front of A into a slow lazy wave
and dropping it again. Clouds coming in from the southwest, low, the color of a
bruise. The lot was empty. Every window in Building C had a light in it and not
one of them had a face.

She unlocked the helmets. She handed him his.

"Felix."

"What."

"I'm going down. Tomorrow." She said it to the helmet in her hands, and then
she made herself say it to him. "You don't have to. I'm not asking you to. But
I am. I have to. I don't know how to explain it and I'm not going to try. I'm
going down that stair tomorrow with a real light and I'm going to see what's at
the bottom of it."

"Addie —"

"I'm telling you so you know. That's all. So it's not a surprise."

He put the helmet on. He did not answer her. He got on the bike behind her and
put his arms around her and held on, and she felt him shaking through her
jacket, and she kicked the engine over and it caught, and she took them out of
the lot past the flag and left onto the river road with the wind behind them
now, pushing.

She did not lane-split. She rode like a person carrying something.

At the low place the road dipped toward the grate and the leaves in it were
moving now, lifting and settling, and she looked at it going by and it was a
grate, and the road came up out of the dip and went on into the dark toward
the town.

---

She let him off at his corner at half past six with the streetlight coming on
orange over his head. He stood there with the helmet in his hand and the folder
in her backpack and the necklace under his shirt, and he looked at her.

"I hear you," he said.

He did not. She could see that he did not. He said it the way he always said
it, and she loved him, and she took the helmet out of his hand and strapped it
behind her seat and did not say anything, because there was nothing to say that
would not be pushing.

The ride home was two miles and she did it slow. The wind had gone cold at the
edges. Her mother's car was in the drive when she pulled in and the kitchen
light was on and the front room was dark, and she sat on the bike for a minute
in the drive with the engine off and looked at the sidewalk across the street,
between the two streetlights, where there was nothing, and then at the kitchen
window, where there was nobody.

She went in the side door so she would not have to go through the kitchen.

Upstairs she locked her door and took the folder out of the backpack and put it
on the desk and did not open it. She got her phone out.

*home. fine. talk tomorrow.*

She sent it. Then she sat on the bed in her jacket with her hands between her
knees and the wind picking up outside, and thought about a saucer at the foot
of a door, and a saucer on a windowsill, and the same sweet smell on both, and
she did not sleep for a long time, and when she did she dreamed of the stair.
