# The Brother's Gambit

He had bought the sunglasses on a Tuesday in September at a gas station on
MacArthur Drive with eleven dollars of the town's money, and he had bought
them as a joke.

That was the truth of it, and he held on to the truth of it the way you hold
on to the first rung of a thing. He had gone up the door at two in the
morning for batteries and lamp oil and the antibiotics the Dauzat girl
needed, the way somebody went up every week, and he had stood in the white
light of the gas station with the cooler humming and a boy behind the counter
not looking at him, and there had been a rack of sunglasses by the register,
and he had thought: *I have never once in forty-two years needed a pair of
these.* And he had bought them. To have a thing that was for the sun. To wear
in a hall lit by lamps and watch his brother's face when he did.

Anatole had not laughed. That was the second truth. Anatole had looked at him
with the sunglasses pushed up in his hair and had said, gently, the way he
said everything, *Take those off, Armand,* the way you say it to a child, and
Armand had not taken them off, and had not taken them off since, and somewhere
in the year between that Tuesday and this one the joke had gone out of them
without his noticing and left the other thing.

He wore them now. Down over his eyes. In a room with one candle in it, on the
floor, in the dark under the dark, at what his brother's clock would have
called nine in the morning.

"You're wearing them again," she said.

"Yes, ma'am."

"In my room."

"Yes, ma'am."

She did not tell him to take them off. That was the whole difference between
her and his brother, and it was the whole reason he was on this floor.

---

He had been born in the dark.

Everybody down here had, that was nothing, that was the town — but he had been
born in the year the cistern went bad and the school of six was a school of
two and Anatole, eight years old, had already been the boy who carried the
lamp for their father to the rail. Armand had come after. He had always come
after. He had grown up in a hall where his brother's name was already a thing
people said with their hands on their chests, and he had carried water, and he
had gone up the door for flour, and he had stood on the old street at the far
end where the cracks were and looked down into them and felt the thing every
one of them felt, the cold at the back of the neck, and had not been afraid of
it.

That was the thing about him. He had figured it out at nineteen. He was not
afraid of her. Everyone in the hall was — their grandmother had come back with
white hair, and the truce was said like a prayer, and the fourth term was a
gift they thanked her for — and Armand Bordelon had stood at the mouth of a
crack at nineteen and felt the cold and thought, *so what.* So what. She was a
woman in a room. She wanted things. Everybody who wanted things could be
talked to.

It had taken him twenty years to get sent for. He had been patient. He had
watched the ones she sent for — a Lemoine, once, who did not come back; a
Dauzat, who came back and would not speak — and he had made himself the man
who was in the square when the runner came, the one who said *I'll go,* the one
who came back and did speak, easy, no worries, and told the hall nothing they
could use and her everything she wanted. A year of it. A year of her crack in
the dark and her room and her voice, and of carrying things up and carrying
things down, and of standing at the top of the stair in the basement of
Building C at three in the morning with the door open a crack, looking at a
water heater, breathing air that had been outside.

He wanted the sun. He had told her so in a hundred ways. He had never once
said it plain, because a man did not say a thing like that plain to a woman
like that, and she had never once laughed, and last night, in this room, with
the boy gone back up her crack and the three on their stretchers and the drain
man dead on the river way, she had said it for him.

*Bring me the boy and I will show you what the sun is for.*

He had not brought her the boy. The boy had said no. He had stood at the rail
and watched a short fat kid with a bad knee tell her no to her face with the
red on him, and something had happened in Armand's chest that he did not have
a name for and did not want one — not fear, he was not afraid of her, but the
thing beside fear, the thing you feel watching someone do the thing you have
never once dared.

He had come back down her crack behind her and the stretchers and he had
stood in this room while she took the belt off and laid it on the table, and
he had said, before she could, "He'll come out."

"He'll come out," she had agreed.

"Then what do you need me for."

And she had looked at him through the sunglasses for a long time, and then
she had told him.

---

"The town," she said now. "Is yours."

She said it the way she said everything, low and slow and warm and not to be
argued with, with her hands open on the wood.

"Not tonight. This morning. Now. Your brother has fourteen people in that
hall who'd stand in front of him and twenty-six who'd stand behind you if you
gave them a reason, and you've got six of your own with cudgels who've been
down my crack and come back up and are not afraid anymore. That's enough.
That's more than enough. You walk in and you take it. You take the hall and
you take the door and you take the stair. You tie your brother to his own
table and you put the ones who won't stand behind you in the cistern room
with the door barred, and you do it in front of everybody, and then it's
yours."

"And then?"

"And then it's *mine.*" She smiled. It did not reach anything. "That's the
part you've been waiting a year to hear, so hear it. With the town yours, I
walk the old street. In the open. Past every doorway, past the swan bottle,
past LEMOINE, into the hall, and I sit down at your brother's table, and forty
people who have said *her* for a hundred and fifty years look at me and turn
their faces to the wall. I've never done that. I want to." She let it sit. "And
with the door yours — with the stair yours, and the basement, and the lock
that hangs open on the staple — I go up it. Not at night. Not through a grate
in an alley. Up the stair, through the basement, out under the stairwell of
Building C at ten in the morning, and across the lot to a leasing office where
a girl named Kayla shows me a unit."

He did not understand. She saw that he did not.

"I rent it, Armand. A unit in C. Ground floor. I sign a paper with a name on
it and I pay the deposit with money you've been carrying up for a year, and
then there is a door in this town that is *mine,* with my name on the lease,
and I come and go through it by daylight like a woman with a job. A woman with
a job at a coffee shop, maybe. Who lives at Riverbend." She looked at him. "I
want a door. I've wanted a door for forty years. Your brother's people have
kept one from me with a line of ash and a saucer of honey and a *lease,* and
I'm done being kept from things by people who are afraid of me."

"And the rail."

"The rail is reached from the town's side only." She said it like a woman
setting down the last card. "There is one way to the crypt, and it's the river
way, and the river way starts at your brother's hall. With the hall yours, the
Delvaux man is a man in a box. I can't go in. Nobody has to. He can sit behind
his iron with his father's piece of junk until he starves, and I'll put six
of your men at the mouth of the river way so nobody feeds him, and I'll go up
my new door and rent my unit and wait for the boy to come out, because he
will, because they always do, and I'll be standing in the *sun* when he does."

She leaned forward.

"You want the sun, Armand. I'm going to give it to you. Not a pair of glasses.
A name up top. A car. A door you can walk through at noon. I'll need a man
up there who can be seen — a face people remember, a man who can sign for
things — and I've never once had one, and you're going to be it." Her hands
closed on the wood. "And all it costs is your brother."

He sat with that.

He found it did not cost him as much as he had thought it would.

"Yes, ma'am," he said.

---

He took the hall at eleven.

It went the way she had said it would, which was the thing he would remember
— not the fight, which was short, but the way it went *exactly the way she had
said,* as if she had already watched it and was telling him what she had seen.

Six men with cudgels through the curtain from the street side. Two more,
who had been in the hall all night pretending to be his brother's, standing
up from the benches when the curtain moved. Anatole at the head of the table
with a lamp and a piece of bread and Odile, and his face — Armand watched his
face — going from tired to understanding in the time it took the curtain to
fall, without passing through surprise, because his brother had known. His
brother had known for a year and had done what he could, which was nothing,
which was feed a man in a hole and say the truce like a lease and hope.

"Armand," Anatole said.

"No worries," Armand said. "Sit down."

Anatole did not sit down. He came around the table with the lamp in one hand,
slow, thick through the shoulders, the way he did everything, and he said, in
front of everybody, "Put those down and we'll talk," and Armand hit him.

He had not known he was going to. He had thought there would be more talking.
But his brother came at him with a lamp and a piece of bread and his hand out
like a man reaching for a child's shoulder, and the thing that had been in
Armand's chest for forty-two years came up all at once and he swung the cudgel
and it caught Anatole across the top of the head and opened it, and his
brother went down on one knee on the swept brick with the blood coming down
into his beard and the lamp going over and rolling, and did not make a sound.

The hall made one. All of it. One long sound like wind.

"Anybody else," Armand said.

Fourteen stood up. He had known it would be fourteen; she had said fourteen.
Étienne Dauzat and his boys. The schoolteacher. Old Lemoine with his hands on
his knees who got up off the bench for the first time in a year. They stood,
and his six went into them, and it was short, because they were old and afraid
and his six were young and had been down her crack, and it was ugly, because
Odile got in front of Anatole on the floor with her arms out and one of the
six hit her too and Armand let him.

They tied his brother to his own table. To the trestle, with cord from the
nets, wrists behind him, and Armand did the fingers himself — two of them, the
right hand, bent back until they went, because she had said *in front of
everybody* and because he wanted to see if he could. Anatole did not scream.
He looked at Armand through the blood with the face he had, the decent one,
and said, thick, "Where's Ti-Jean," and Armand looked, and the boy was gone.

Gone. The runner. Out through the cistern door in the first ten seconds with
four of the Dauzat girls behind him, into the channels under the town where
nobody could follow who did not know them, and Armand had known them once, and
did not anymore.

"Let him run," Armand said. "Where's he going to go."

He put the fourteen in the cistern room and barred it. He put two of his own
on the bell-stair with the door at the top of it barred from below, the way it
was always barred, except that now the men below it were his — and above it,
they told him later, an old man had come down the concrete steps with a
flashlight and a crowbar at midnight and had stood in the basement with his
hand on the iron ring for an hour and had not been able to move a door that
was barred from the other side, and had gone back up and sat down on the
bottom step of the stairwell of Building C with his hand on his chest.

"Let him sit," Armand said.

He put four at the mouth of the river way.

Then he went to the far end of the street, to the square, to the crack in the
wall, and he stood there with the sunglasses on and the lantern beside him,
and he sent the runner he had — a Dauzat boy of his own, twelve, who feared
him — down her way to say it was done.

---

She came up at four.

He had never seen her come up her crack. Nobody had. She came up it the way
she went down it, without a light, and the first he knew of her was the cold
coming out of the gap in the wall ahead of her like a breath out of a mouth,
and then she was standing in the square by the well with her hands at her
sides and the belt at her waist and the bow across her back, and behind her,
carried by the two of his men she had kept below, the three stretchers, with
the three faces turned up.

She looked at the square. At the well with its rotten lid. At the doorways.

"I've never stood here," she said.

Then she walked.

He went ahead of her with the lantern because that was what he did, and she
did not need it, and he knew she did not need it, and he carried it anyway
down the old street past the doorways and the swan bottle on its sill and the
lintel that said LEMOINE, and the town came out of the side vaults to see.

They did. That was the thing. He had thought they would hide. They came out —
the ones who had stood behind him and the ones who had only stood, women with
children, the old, the schoolteacher's sister — and they lined the old street
in the lamplight the way people line a street for a thing going by, and when
she came past them, tall, dark, her hair down over her ears, walking their
street for the first time in the memory of anyone alive, they turned their
faces to the wall.

Every one. Not looking down. To the *wall.* Turning, as she passed, the way a
row of sunflowers turns, until she was by, and then not turning back.

She did not look at them. She walked to the curtain of the hall and went
through it and stood inside the door of the warehouse vault where the goods
had waited for the river, and looked down the long table made of doors at her
brother-in-law's brother tied to the end of it with his head bleeding into his
beard, and at the fire, and at the slate on the floor where the school had
been, and she sat down.

At the head of the table. In Anatole's place. With her hands open on the wood.

"There," she said. "That's done."

Anatole lifted his head. He looked at her down the length of his own table
with the one eye the blood had not shut, and he did not turn his face to the
wall, and Armand saw her see that, and saw her not mind it.

"Bordelon," she said. "Your grandmother had white hair when I met her, and I
was young. She made a good bargain. It kept your people alive a long time,
and it kept me fed, and it's over." She looked at the four at the river way's
mouth, and at the two on the stair, and at Armand with the lantern. "Your
brother's going up top with me. He's going to have a name. You're going to
keep a smaller town with a door that has fewer keepers, and you'll do it,
because you do what you swear, and that's the one thing about you I've always
liked."

"He'll come out," Anatole said, thick. "The boy. For his father."

"Yes."

"And then you'll have him."

"And then I'll have *seen* him." She stood. "That's all I've ever wanted from
any of you. To be looked at and not slid off. He looked at me last night
through a rail with his hands on the iron and he did not slide, and he's the
first thing in forty years that hasn't, and he's mine, and I'm going to go
stand at that rail now with three people who are somebody's, and wait for him
to look at me again."

She went out through the curtain.

Armand followed with the lantern. He followed her down the old street past the
turned faces and into the mouth of the river way, past his four, into the
brick, with the stretchers behind and the shadow coming up off the wet floor
around her feet like a tide coming in, and he thought, walking, about a gas
station on MacArthur Drive with a cooler humming and a rack of sunglasses by
the register, and a boy behind the counter who had not looked at him.

Up top, they would look at him. She had said. A name, a car, a door at noon.

He pushed the sunglasses up into his hair, in the dark, so he could see the
brick, and found that he had done it without deciding to, the way his brother
took a hand off a child's shoulder, and put them back down, and walked on into
the black behind her toward the rail.
