# She Knows Where He Is

The light was on.

He had asked for that. He had lain awake all night in his own bed for the
first time since Saturday with a box under it that made the room quiet and a
window with no curtains letting the streetlight in orange, and he had not
known what was next, and it had been exactly what he thought it would be
like, which was terrible, and he would not have traded it. And at nine on
Thanksgiving morning he walked into room 412 at Rapides Regional with a
paper cup of coffee from the machine in the lobby and the blinds all the way
up and the parking garage outside gone white with frost, and his father was
awake, and looking at the window, and had been for some time.

"You said with the light on," his father said.

"Yes."

"It's on."

Felix sat down. He put the coffee on the tray table where the good hand could
get it, and his father looked at it and did not take it, and looked at him.

The tongue was better. He heard that. Whatever they had put in the bag on the
pole had done something overnight, or the thing had done it on its own, and
when his father said *on* it came out nearly the way it had come out on
Monday, from a cot, in a lamp's light, when the man had said *I tied that. In
the rain.* The right side had not changed. It lay in the bed the way it had
lain.

"Ask," his father said.

"Is there another one."

He watched it land. He had thought about how to say it all night and had
gotten it down to that, four words, because his father did not like long
questions and because he did not want to give himself room to not ask it.

"You said *whole.*" He put his hands on his knees. "*I keep it until it's
whole.* That's what I said. Holding it. And it's a hilt. It's a grip and a
crossguard and a piece of a tang broken off square, and I've been holding it
since yesterday morning and I've been lying under it all night and I know
what it isn't, and I'd like to know, please, with the light on, what the rest
of it is and where."

His father looked at the window a while.

"There's a second," he said.

---

"The Blade."

He said it the way he had said *the wolf,* on Monday, in the crypt — as a
thing they said, in a low voice, not narrating it. "There's a second piece.
The Blade. It went up the river when the Hilt came down it. That's how my
father put it and how his put it to him, and I never got more than that out
of either of them and I asked. *Up the river when the Hilt came down.* Three
hundred years ago. The same Frenchman, or another one. It broke, or they
broke it, so it couldn't be — " He moved the good hand. "So one man couldn't
carry the whole thing off. Three pieces. Three sets of keepers. That was the
idea. Three sets of people like us, in three holes, keeping their piece from
her mother's mothers."

"Where."

"Natchitoches."

Felix waited.

"The old fort on the Cane." His father said it slowly, the way you say a
thing you have said to a wall for fourteen years and never to a face. "The
oldest town in the state. Fifty miles up. There was a fort there, French,
before there was a fort anywhere, on the Cane River, and the protectors who
kept the Blade were there, and my father's father wrote to them, twice, when
he was a young man, and got one letter back, and then nothing. That's all.
That's every word of it. *Natchitoches. The old fort on the Cane.* That's all
my father knew, and all I know, and now it's all you know, and I'm sorry it
isn't more."

"And the third."

"I don't know the third."

"You know *something.*"

His father turned his head on the pillow and looked at him with the good side
of his face and the eye in it that had seen the sky yesterday and was not
going to stop seeing it, and for a second Felix saw the man from the
prologue of his own life, the one in the rain, with the knife he knew was
useless.

"New Orleans," he said. "Where they came ashore. That's a guess and it's my
father's guess and he was guessing. Don't take it up the river with you. Take
Natchitoches. That one's real."

"I'm not — " Felix stopped.

He had been going to say *I'm not going anywhere.* He had said it to Diane on
the phone from the truck and to Addie on the stair and to himself, all night,
under a quiet box, and he found, in room 412 with the light on and the word
*Natchitoches* in the air between them like a road, that it was not true and
had not been true since he put a piece of iron in his pocket, and he shut his
mouth on it.

His father saw him do it.

"Not this week," his father said. "Not this month. It's been up there three
hundred years. It'll be there in the spring." The good hand moved on the
blanket. "Have your dinner. Bring the rolls. Let the girl's mother sit at a
table with the people she gave you to and say what she's got to say. Then
come back here and tell me what it was like, because I'd like to know, and I
can't go."

"You could. In a chair. They said — "

"Not today." He looked at the window. "Today I'm going to lie here and look
at a parking garage. You have no idea. Go on."

Felix stood. He stood at the foot of the bed with his hands cold in his
pockets and looked at the chart in its clip, at the name typed on it, and at
his father under it.

"She's out there," he said. "Somewhere. Right now."

"Yes."

"Doesn't that — "

"Every day for fourteen years, boy." His father did not look away from the
window. "The only thing that's different is now she knows I know. Go eat."

---

The Knights' house had the leaf in.

He saw it from the door — the dining table pulled out to its whole length,
the extra section that lived in the hall closet fitted into the middle with
the seam showing where the finish did not match, six chairs, the good cloth.
Robert had done it. Robert did the leaf; Diane did everything else; that was
the division, and it had been the division for as long as Felix had been
alive in this house, and he stood in the front hall with a bag of rolls from
the only store open on Thanksgiving morning and looked at it, and could not,
for a second, go in.

Diane came out of the kitchen with her hands wet.

She did not say anything. She had said everything on the phone, nine times,
and once more from the truck, and she came down the hall and put her wet
hands on his face and looked at him — at his eyes, and his hair pushed back
the way he had never worn it, and the ears, which she had seen every day of
his life and never once said a word about — and she started to cry, and said
"I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I said I wouldn't," and did not stop.

"Mom."

"I *know.*"

"I'm okay."

"You're not, honey. Look at you." She wiped her face with the back of a wet
hand. "Robert. Robert, he's here."

Robert came from the dining room with a chair in his hands.

He was a tall quiet man with reading glasses on his forehead who filed his
receipts by month, and he put the chair down at the end of the table, and
came and stood behind his wife, and put his hand on Felix's shoulder over
her, the way he had put it there at every graduation and every airport, and
said, "Son," and that was all he said, and it was enough, and it was too
much, and Felix stood in the hall of the house he had grown up in and let
them.

Then Addie came up the walk behind him with a pot in her arms.

And behind Addie, on the walk, in a tan coat with a black eye gone yellow and
her hair gone gray at the temples, walking carefully, with her hand not quite
on her side, Janet Walker; and Diane looked past Felix, out the door, and
went still.

"You're Mrs. Walker," she said.

"I'm Janet."

"From the orphanage."

"Yes."

Diane looked at her a long time, with her wet hands and her wet face, at a
woman she had met twice, fourteen years ago, in an office with a filing
cabinet, who had asked her not to keep her name, and who had been living two
miles away the whole time with a daughter who rode a motorcycle.

"You did the papers," Diane said.

"I did."

"You picked us."

"I watched you for a year," Janet said, on the walk, not coming up the step.
"At church. I watched him carry the plates and I watched you cry at the
Easter service and apologize for it, and I thought, that's them. I never told
you why. I never told you anything. I'm not going to say I'm sorry for it,
Mrs. Knight, because I'd do it again; but I've come to your house on
Thanksgiving to tell you what it was, all of it, at your table, if you'll
have me at it. And if you won't, I'll wait in the car. I've done worse."

Diane stood in her doorway.

"Robert," she said, "get another chair."

"There's six."

"Get another one anyway. I want one for the counter. I want to sit where I
can see the door." She wiped her face. "Come in, Mrs. Walker. Wipe your
feet. Addie, honey, what on earth is that."

"It's his," Addie said. "It's for his window."

---

The orchid cactus.

She had repotted it — no. Janet had, on Monday, with her coat on, in the
garden, with the map still on the closet door and a daughter under the city;
and Addie had carried it two miles on the bike this morning in a milk crate
bungeed to the back, with her right hand that did not close held against the
bar, and it sat now in the middle of the Knights' front hall in a pot that
was finally the right size with fresh soil packed around it, its long flat
arms trailing over the rim, five years old, green, absurd, alive, the thing
he had smuggled out of Belize in a sock.

"It's supposed to live where you'd never expect it," Addie said. "You said.
So." She did not look at him. "Your window gets the morning. Mine doesn't.
It'll bloom for you."

"Addie — "

"Don't."

He did not.

---

He told them at the table.

He had not planned how. He had thought about it all night and had not been
able to plan it, and in the end he did it the way Janet did things, in
order, without adjectives, while the turkey sat on its board with Robert's
knife beside it and nobody cut it. The papers. The wax. The necklace at the
table in Ugly Mug, and a waitress who had started that day. The pawn shop.
The lot, and the plaque, and the door under the stair. Down. The town. A
woman on the floor of a room with cloth on the walls. His father, on a cot,
with a piece of iron. The rail. The arrow. The necklace off, on purpose, and
what it had cost, and he put his hands on the good cloth when he said that,
palms up, with the bandage on the left one, and Diane looked at them and did
not understand and Robert did.

He did not say *Cyprien.* He found he could not say it at this table. He
said *she had a name for me,* and Diane said "What was it," and he said "Not
yours," and she nodded like a person being given a gift, and let it go.

Janet told the rest. The church. The year of watching. The three things at
the lit doorway that a man in the rain had said, and why the second one was
*never tell him until he leaves home* — because a boy who knows is a boy who
looks, and a boy who looks can be seen. The ward on her own house and the one
she had laid, Wednesday, on a second-floor apartment four blocks from this
table. A map on a closet door with two columns on it that had, since
yesterday, three fewer names in the second.

"And she's — " Diane said. She could not say it.

"Alive," Janet said. "Up top. She's not coming here. Not because of anything
I did. Because there's a thing under his bed that she doesn't come near, and
because she's got what she wanted, which was to see his face, and because
she's got — " Janet looked at Felix. "Because she said she wouldn't. And she
doesn't lie. It's the one thing I'll say for her."

The kitchen clock ticked. It was the same clock. It had been there when he
was four.

"Then that's that," Robert said.

They all looked at him.

He had said nothing through the whole of it. He had sat at the head of his
own table with the knife beside the bird and his glasses on his forehead and
his big quiet accountant's hands folded on the cloth, and listened, in order,
the way he listened to a client tell him what they had done with the money,
and now he unfolded the hands.

"I'm not going to pretend I understood half of it," he said. "I'm going to
say I heard it. A man handed me a boy in the rain and told me three things
and I did them. I did them for fourteen years and I didn't ask why and I'm
not sorry. I'd have liked to know. I'd have liked to have been told. I'm not
going to say that to Mrs. Walker again because she's said she'd do it the
same and I believe her and I'd probably have done it the same in her chair."
He picked up the knife. "He's my son. He was my son on Wednesday when I
carried the box over and he's my son now with a — with whatever that is under
his bed and his mother across the street and cold hands. If anybody at this
table thinks different they can say it and then they can go, and I'll wrap
them a plate."

Nobody said anything.

"Diane," Robert said, "the rolls are getting cold."

Diane got up, and went to the kitchen, and came back with the rolls in the
good basket, and set them down in front of Felix, and put her hand on the top
of his head, the way you do to a child, and left it there a second, and went
to her chair by the counter where she could see the door, and sat.

Robert cut the turkey.

---

They ate. That was the thing he had not expected, and had, and it was
stranger than the sky had been for his father — that five people who had
that at the table between them could pass a bowl of sweet potatoes and say
*this is good, Diane* and *it's the marshmallows, I used the small ones,* and
mean it; that Robert could ask Janet what a dispatcher's shift ran and Janet
could tell him; that Addie could eat left-handed with her right in her lap
and nobody mention it; that he could be hungry. He was hungry. He had not
been since Saturday. He ate two plates and a roll and Diane watched him do it
from her chair by the counter and did not cry, quite.

Once, passing the green beans, Addie's fingers touched his on the dish.

She did not say anything. She looked at her hand, and then at his, and then
at him, and her face did the thing it had done at a tin mirror and on a
stair, and she took the dish, and he saw her decide to say it later, and
loved her for the deciding and for the later.

At two Diane went to the window.

She had been going to it all through dinner. Not standing; looking. The
dining room's front window gave on the street, and the live oak, and the
houses across the way with their own cars in their own drives and their own
Thanksgivings going on behind their own glass, and she had looked at it every
few minutes with her fork in her hand the way a woman looks at a door she has
been told will not open, and now she got up and went and stood at it with her
arms crossed.

"There's a car," she said.

Janet did not get up. "Gray."

"Yes."

"A man in it. Sunglasses."

"How did you — "

"He's been on our street since Wednesday night," Janet said, and ate a bite
of turkey. "He'll be on this one now. He doesn't get out. He's not going to.
He's got a job and it's to sit in a car where I can see him so I know she's
keeping her word, and I'd rather see him than not." She swallowed. "His name
is Armand. He's got a brother who kept a door for forty years. He's nobody,
Mrs. Knight. He's a man who wanted the sun. Sit down and eat your pie."

Diane did not sit down. She stood at the window with her arms crossed and
looked at a gray car that nobody in the neighborhood was going to remember,
and Felix watched her look, and understood that this was what it would be
now, for her, for all of them: a window, and something in it, and dinner
anyway.

---

He walked home at three with Addie and the cactus.

Four blocks. Diane had wanted to drive him; Robert had said *let him walk,*
and given him a plate wrapped in foil, and stood on the porch with his hand
up while they went down the walk, the way he had stood on it at every
departure of Felix's life, and would not go in till they turned the corner.
Janet had stayed. She had things to say to Diane that were not for the
table, and things to say to Robert that were about the second-floor apartment
and the sills and a smell of sage he was not to ask about, and she had looked
at Addie once, at the door, and Addie had said *I'm walking him home,* and
Janet had said *I know,* and that had been the whole of it.

The gray car did not follow. He looked. It sat under the oak with the man in
it looking straight ahead and did not move, and they went past it on the far
sidewalk, Addie with the pot in her arms and her hand held against it, and he
felt the man's not-looking on his back all the way to the corner.

Cold and bright. The kind of afternoon the whole week had been building to,
with the sky gone hard and blue and the live oaks throwing shadows across the
street like ladders and every yard with a car in it and a smell of somebody
else's turkey coming out of somebody else's kitchen. An ordinary street. He
had walked it ten thousand times. He walked it now with a girl beside him
carrying a cactus and his hands cold in his pockets and a piece of iron four
blocks ahead of him going quiet in a box under his bed, and it looked exactly
the way it had always looked, and he could see it, and it did not look down.

"Natchitoches," Addie said, at the second corner.

He had not told her. He had not told anyone. She said it the way she had said
*Cyprien* on the stair, as a thing she had already decided to be able to
say, and did not look at him, and shifted the pot.

"He told you this morning. I could see it on you at the table. Robert could.
Diane couldn't; she was looking at the window." Step. Step. "Fifty miles.
It's the old French town. We drove through it once for the Christmas lights
and you said you wanted to come back with the camera in the daytime, and you
never did. There's a fort. They rebuilt it. It's a state park."

"Addie — "

"I'm coming."

He stopped on the sidewalk.

"You haven't asked," she said. "You weren't going to. You were going to lie
under that box for a month and think about it and then tell me you'd
decided, and I'd have said yes, and you'd have thought it was your idea." She
turned. Copper hair all the way loose, freckles, the pot against her chest
with its arms hanging down, her right hand curled against the clay in a way
it would not uncurl from, and her face, which he had looked at for four years
and knew better than his own. "So I'm telling you first. I'm coming to
Natchitoches. On the bike. You're getting on it. I don't care what your hands
do. I don't care what your eyes do in a room with the light off. I feel sick
when she's near and I'm going to keep feeling sick and I'm going to keep
telling you, and that's what I've got, and it's more than she's got, because
I can *touch* you." She said the last word like a thing she had waited a week
to say. "Now take your plant. My hand hurts."

He took the plant.

---

His window got the morning. She had been right about that. At three in the
afternoon it got the other thing, the long flat gold that came through a
window with no curtains and lay across the kitchen table and the folder on it
— papers, and an opened letter, and two envelopes still sealed in red — and
across the floor to the bed, and under it, where a box sat with a piece of
iron in it and the room was quiet the way no room he had ever slept in had
been quiet.

Addie put the cactus on the sill.

She did it left-handed, carefully, the way she did everything in the garden
and nothing else, turning the pot so the longest arm hung toward the light,
pressing the soil down once with two fingers, and stepped back, and looked at
it, and the thing sat in the gold in a window on the second floor of a house
cut into apartments in the Garden District of Alexandria, Louisiana, and was
exactly where you would never expect it.

"There," she said.

He came and stood beside her.

Below the window, the side street. His street. A dozen houses, live oaks,
the sidewalk buckled where the roots came up, a dog somewhere, a smell of
smoke from a grill. The far sidewalk, under the biggest of the oaks, where
the shade lay across the concrete in a hard-edged line, and the gold light
stopped at the line, and did not go in.

She was standing in it.

Tall. A gray coat, plain, the kind you bought secondhand for eleven dollars
and wore because it was warm and nobody looked at it. Her hair down over her
ears. Her hands at her sides. Not in a doorway; not at a corner; not behind
anything. On a sidewalk, in the daylight, on Thanksgiving, across a street
from a window, at the exact place where the quiet from a box under a bed ran
out and the shade began, as if someone had drawn a line on the concrete and
she had come and put her toes on it.

She was looking at the window.

Not the way you look at a window. The way you look at a face.

"Felix," Addie said. Very quietly. Her hand had gone to her stomach.

"I see her."

"Nobody else does." A car went by between them, and a man walking a dog, and
the man's eye went across the tall woman on the sidewalk and off her the way
a hand goes off a hot stove, and he did not turn his head. "Look at them.
They're walking right — Felix, nobody *sees* her."

"I know."

"She's not hiding."

"No."

"Then why can't they — "

"Because she's never once in her life been looked at," he said. "And I'm
looking."

He was. He had been since the sill. He stood at his own window with his
hands cold at his sides and his father's ring and a black stone on a cord at
his throat under the hoodie, and he did the thing the prologue of his life
had said you never did — the thing a man with a boy on his shoulder had known
better than to do at a grate in the rain — and looked straight across a
street into his mother's face in the daylight, and she looked back, and
neither of them slid.

She did not smile. She did not lift a hand. She stood at the line with her
old hands at her sides and her brown eyes on his, and it went on, the
looking, long enough that the man with the dog reached the corner and turned
it, long enough that Addie's hand on her stomach went white at the knuckles,
long enough that he understood she was not going to be the one to stop it.

He did not take it off.

He felt where it was. He could always feel where it was now; that was the
cost and the gift of it, a cord on the back of the neck and a stone at the
collarbone and a ring beside the stone, the necklace his mother had had made
so she could go unseen and his father had taken off her in her sleep and put
on a child, and he stood in the gold light with it on and did not touch it,
and did not think about the sky over a lot or a table with a leaf in it or a
fort on a river fifty miles north; he thought about nothing; he looked at
her; and he let it be enough that she could not hide from him either.

Then she turned, and walked away up the sidewalk, in the shade, toward
Jackson Street, unhurried, like a person, and nobody on the street turned
their head, and at the corner a gray car pulled out from under a live oak
and went the same way.

---

The Blade of Tyr was in Natchitoches. His father had said so, with the light
on, in the only words he had: that it went up the river when the Hilt came
down it, that it lay under the old fort on the Cane, that the people who kept
it had written one letter, once, a long time ago, and then had not written
again. That was all anyone in Alexandria knew. It was fifty miles. It was a
state park. A girl with a motorcycle had already said she was coming, and he
was going to get on.

In a second-floor apartment in the Garden District, on Thanksgiving, with the
gold going out of a window and an orchid cactus on the sill and a piece of
iron under the bed, Felix Knight stood with his cold hands at his sides and
his mother's name in a box he would not open and watched the empty place on
the sidewalk where she had been, and the city went on around him — cars,
dogs, other people's dinners, the long ordinary afternoon — and did not look
down. It never had.

She did.

She walked up Jackson Street in a secondhand coat in the last of the light
with a car behind her and a son behind her and the ache in her chest that had
been there for fourteen years and was there still and would always be, and
she did not look back, because she did not need to.

She knew where he was.
