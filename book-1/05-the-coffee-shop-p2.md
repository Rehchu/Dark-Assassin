# The Coffee Shop (part 2)

By half past ten she had been through every alphabet the internet would show
her and three it would not, and she had a word for the stone and a guess for
the mark and no idea at all what any of it was for, and the necklace was still
lying in the lamplight beside her keyboard, warm, two miles from the boy it
belonged to.

Despite her efforts, Addie decided to FaceTime Felix, and give him an update
about what she found out.

He picked up on the first ring. His face came up in the little window with the
streetlight behind it, orange, coming through a window that had no curtains
yet, and he was at the kitchen table in the hoodie with the folder open in front
of him and his hand at his throat.

"Hey," he said. "Hey. Is it —"

She held it up to the camera.

She let him know she had accidentally taken the necklace and that she wanted to
give it back to him.

"I know you do," he said. "I know. It's okay. I figured it out about twenty
minutes after you left, I put my hand up and it was just — hoodie." He laughed,
not much. "I walked all the way home like that. Touching my neck like an idiot."

"I'm sorry."

"You said." His eyes went off her, to the folder, and back. "It's fine. You've
got it. That's what the note said, right? Keep it on you. It's on you."

"It's on my desk."

"Close enough."

It was not close enough and they both knew it and neither of them said so.

"What did you find out?" Asked Felix.

She told him. She held the stone up to the camera again and turned it and told
him *obsidian,* and what obsidian was, a glass that had been fire, and what the
purple pages said it did — a shield, a mirror, a thing for not being found by
the wrong kind of looking — and she watched his face while she said *not being
found* and saw nothing move in it, because he was tired, and because he did not
have the sidewalk in him the way she did. She told him about the mark. She held
the phone close so he could see the two tines. She told him what the one
alphabet had said about it. Protection. Against enemies. On a door, on a
shield, on the thing you carried.

"Belt and suspenders," she said.

"What?"

"Two locks on one door. Whoever made this wanted it to work. Bad."

"Or somebody read the same pages you did and bought it at a fair."

"Somebody sealed it in wax, Felix. Somebody wrote you a note in capital
letters. Somebody gave it to a four-year-old."

"I almost called my mom tonight," he said. "I had the phone in my hand. I had
her name up."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because she'd tell me." He said it to the folder. "Whatever it is. She'd cry
and then she'd tell me, all of it, in order, and then I'd know. And I'd have to
have known it in my kitchen at ten o'clock at night by myself." He looked up.
"I want to know it with you. Is that stupid?"

"No," she said. "That's not stupid."

He did not have anything for that. He turned the folder around on the table so
she could see it through the camera, the envelopes fanned out, the three sealed
ones and the one with the wax broken, and he held them up one at a time to the
light of his phone and she saw the hidden writing again and it was no more
readable at ten-thirty than it had been at seven.

They discussed everything that Addie had learned, and realized they had gotten
nowhere.

Obsidian was a rock. A rune was a letter. Protection was a word people put on
things. None of it told them who had written the note, or sealed the letters,
or in what, or why the Knights had kept a boy's life in a closet for fourteen
years and labeled the cabinet doors, or what a waitress on her first day wanted
with a black stone at a stranger's throat. Every answer they had found was a
door with another door behind it.

They were back at the beginning with even more questions.

"Bring it tomorrow," he said, at the end, when they had run out. "Ugly Mug.
Eleven-ish. I'll bring the folder."

"You're going back there?"

"It's a coffee shop, Addie. Where else am I going to go. I go there every
morning." He rubbed his face. "I want to see it again in the daylight. All of
it. The letters. I want to see if I'm crazy."

"You're not crazy."

"Then I want to see that." He looked at the camera, at her, and for a second
the face he was holding together came apart a little and she saw how scared he
was, and then he had it back. "Keep it on you. Okay? Not on the desk. On you."

"Okay."

She did not put it on. She sat looking at it after the call ended, and she
turned the lamp off and lay down in her clothes, and it stayed on the desk in
the dark where she could not see it, and she could feel exactly where it was
anyway, the way you can feel a person in a room.

She did not sleep much. When she did she dreamed of the sidewalk.

---

Friday came in warm.

It happened like that in November here — the front went through in the night
and took the cold with it and left the air standing still and thick and wrong
for the season, seventy by ten in the morning, not a leaf moving. Her mother's
car was in the drive when Addie came down, and her mother was asleep, door
closed, the way she slept after a shift, and there had been no shift. The sage
was gone from the kitchen table. The saucer was gone from the sill.

Addie did not ask. Her mother was asleep. Later.

She rode in with her visor up because the air was warm enough for it, and the
warmth was the strangest part of the morning — Jackson Street in November with
car windows down and a man in shirtsleeves washing his truck and the live oaks
not moving, not one leaf, the whole town standing in a held breath under a sky
gone the color of a sheet. She looked across the street when she left the drive.
The sidewalk between the two streetlights was a sidewalk with a hedge behind it
and the Guidrys' cat asleep on their step. In daylight it was nothing. She looked
at it anyway until the corner took it away.

The next day, Addie convened with Felix at the coffee shop, and returned the
necklace to him.

He was inside this time, at the same table in the corner, with two cups already
sweating on the wood and the folder under his hand. He looked like he had slept
about as much as she had. She sat down across from him with the helmet by her
boot and took the necklace out of her left pocket, where it had ridden in
against the last of her mother's money, and set it on the table between the
cups.

"Sorry I ran off with it, I had forgotten I had it." Stated Addie.

"I know."

He picked it up. He held it a second, the beads across his palm, the stone hung
down between two fingers, and she watched him decide something and then not
decide it, and then he lifted the loop over his head and pulled his hair out
from under the cord the way he had last night and let the stone drop into the
hollow of his throat.

"How does it look?"

"Same as yesterday."

"Good," he said. "I was worried it had changed."

He tucked it down inside the collar of his shirt so only the cord showed, and
put his hand over it once, flat, and took the hand away.

The two ordered some drinks, and sat down at a table and waited for the barista
to bring them their drinks.

The manager took the order at the register. Two things Addie did not usually get
— hot, both of them, because the iced one had been the joke and the joke was
over. The bearded man rang them up and said somebody would bring them out and
went to the machine himself, and Addie went back to the table and sat, and
looked at the room.

It was different in daylight. Smaller. The string lights were off and the big
windows let in the flat white glare of a still warm day and everything that had
been gold last night was just a coffee shop — chipped tables, a chalkboard, a
tip jar with a joke on it. A man on a laptop. Two nurses in scrubs. The girl
with the ponytail was not on the machine. Nobody with black hair was anywhere
Addie could see.

She let out a breath she had not known she was holding.

They kept talking about the necklace and the rest of the items in the box until
drinks arrived.

The rest of the box, it turned out, was nothing. That was the thing that got
her. A box of old things, Robert Knight had said when he brought it over, and
old things was what it was — a baby blanket with the satin edge chewed, a
plastic dinosaur, two school pictures, a shoebox of crayon drawings with his
name printed on the back in Diane Knight's hand. And under all of it the folder.
Nothing else that was strange. Nothing else that was old the way the envelopes
were old. Just a life, boxed, and one folder in it that did not belong to that
life at all.

"They put it at the bottom," Felix said. "On purpose. You'd have to take
everything out to get to it. Like they wanted me to see the dinosaur first."

"Maybe they did."

"Why?"

"So you'd know they loved you before you got to the part where they lied."

He looked at her. She had not known she was going to say it until it was out,
and it sat on the table between them with the cups, and he did not argue with
it.

He opened the folder instead. He took the three sealed envelopes out and laid
them side by side on the table in the white light from the window, and the
opened one beside them with the note folded back inside it, and they both
looked. It was what he had come for. The hidden writing was no different in
daylight — the same slanting hand on all three fronts, a line each, letters that
were not letters. The wax on each was the same dark red and the same mark was
pressed into each, a mark like a thumbprint that was not a thumbprint. Three
letters, one hand, one seal.

"I'm not crazy," he said quietly.

"You're not crazy."

He put them back in the folder, in order, the way they had come.

"Back again are we".

Stated the barista while bringing the two their drinks.

Addie's stomach turned over. Once, hard, the way it had on the sidewalk and the
way it had at this table twenty hours ago, and she put her hand flat on the wood
before she looked up.

Two mugs, set down without a sound. The flour-handprint apron. The name tag,
LEE. The hair down over the ears and the shoulders. Brown eyes. The same
low slow warm voice.

"I'm sorry I was listening in, But I might know someone who can help you".

Addie looked at the barista.

It was the same lady as yesterday with the black hair.

Of course it was. Addie had looked for her when she came in and had not seen
her and had let herself think that meant something, and it had meant that she
was in the back, and now she was here, standing at the corner of the table with
one hand resting on the back of Felix's chair, easy, the way you rest a hand on
a piece of furniture in your own house.

She had not looked at Felix yet. She was looking at Addie, and her eyes were on
Addie's face the way they had been last night, steady and direct and somehow
pleased, the way you look at a thing you can see all the way to the back of.

Addie immediately replied "That would be a great help! We have been trying to
figure this out."

It came out bright. Too bright; she heard it. But it was true, they had been
trying, and the woman had said *help,* and Addie had spent all night in a locked
room finding out that there was nobody to ask.

"I thought y'all might have been," the barista said. "Yesterday. You had the
look."

Addie turns away for a brief moment, and takes a sip of her coffee.

It was hot and she did not care. She needed a second with her face pointed
somewhere else. Out the window Third Street lay in the white glare with nothing
moving on it, not a leaf, not a bag, a truck going by slow with its windows
down, and she held the cup in both hands and drank and counted three and let
her stomach settle.

The barista leans in to Felix's ear and whispers something before Addie could
notice.

"I'll have to check it out" Said Felix back to the barista.

Addie turns back, and looks at Felix with a charming smile.

The woman was straightening up from beside him. That was all Addie saw — the
end of it, the tall body coming back upright, one hand leaving the back of his
chair — and Felix's face. His face had gone the color of the mug. And under the
white, coming up the side of his neck from inside the collar where the cord
went, the red. The same red as last night when her eyes had gone to his ears.

He was looking at the table. He was not looking at Addie and he was not looking
at the woman.

Addie kept the smile where it was. She did not know why. It was the one she
used on her mother on the stairs.

"This is my favorite place to come. The atmosphere is always welcoming, the
decor is spot on, and I even appreciate the little details on the latte art."
thought Addie with a smile.

It was a thing she had thought a hundred times in this chair. She thought it now
on purpose, the whole of it, word by word, like a person putting a hand on a
railing, and it held her up.

"Where is the place that you mentioned might help?"

The barista smiled, and responded "Gold And Silver Pawn shop, It's located on
Main Street."

"A pawn shop," Addie said.

"Old man runs it. Been there forever. He knows about" — the smallest pause, and
her eyes went for the first time to the cord at Felix's collar and did not stay
there, slid off it and away like water off glass, and came back to Addie — "old
things. Where they come from. Who made them. That kind of thing." She said it
to Addie. She had said all of it to Addie except the part she had said to Felix.
"You take that stone to him. He'll tell you what it is."

"Thank you"

The couple said simultaneously as they rushed to finish their drinks.

They did not look at each other while they said it. Addie drank hers too fast
and burned her tongue and did not care about that either. Felix drank his like
a person drinking water after a run, and set the mug down, and had the folder
under his arm before she had her helmet off the floor.

"What did she say to you?"

"Nothing. The pawn shop. Same thing she said to you."

"She said the pawn shop to me *after.* What did she say to you before?"

"Addie." He was already up. "It was nothing. Come on."

It was not nothing. She had seen his face. She let it go the way her mother let
things go, and she filed it, and she stood.

The woman was behind the counter. Not looking at them. Wiping down the steam
wand with a white cloth, slowly, the way you do a thing you have done ten
thousand times, her hair down over her ears. When they passed the counter she
did not look up, and when they reached the door she said, to the steam wand, in
that same warm slow voice, "Y'all come back," and the bell rang.

The heat outside was like a hand.

Not a breath moving. The sky white. Third Street two blocks from the levee and
nothing on it stirring, not the flag on the bank, not the paper cup in the
gutter, as if the whole town had been told to hold still. Addie unhooked the bag
from behind the seat of the bike and held it open and Felix put the folder in it
and she zipped it and hooked it back on, and pulled the cord twice, and then she
unstrapped the spare helmet from the seat and stood there holding it in both
hands.

Main Street was four blocks. She could see the corner from here.

Felix stood beside her on the curb with his hand flat at his collar over the
place where the cord went under, and the color had not come back into his face.
He was looking at the corner too. She almost asked him again, and did not,
because she could see that whatever the woman had said to him was still being
said, somewhere behind his eyes, and he had not finished hearing it.

She looked back once through the big window at the corner table, empty now,
two mugs on it, and at the counter behind it, where nobody was standing at all.
