# The Door Goes Down

It had rained all night and it was still raining, and Diane had called three
times.

He had watched the phone light up on the kitchen table each time with her
name on it and had let it go dark. The first had been at nine, which was
church. The second at half past twelve, which was lunch — the roast, his
father's roast, the table with the leaf in it and the spice rack in its
alphabet over the stove. The third at two, which was nothing, which was a
woman standing in her kitchen after the dishes with the phone in both hands.
He had not answered that one either. He did not know what he would have said.
*I'm not coming because there's a stair under the place you got me from and I
heard something at the bottom of it move.*

The rain came down the window with no curtains in sheets.

The folder was not on the table. That was the thing he kept noticing. It had
been on the table for four days and now it was in a backpack two miles away
and the table was a table, and he sat at it in his hoodie with the necklace
under his shirt and his hand going to it, and going to it, and outside the
oaks roared and the gutters ran and the whole Garden District lay under the
water like something being washed.

He had said no. He had said it in a basement with his voice shaking and he had
meant it, and he had said it again in his head all night and meant it less
each time, and now it was two o'clock on Sunday and he was sitting by the door
with his shoes on.

The bike came up the side street at ten past.

He heard it before he saw it — the engine, low, taking the wet street careful
— and then it was at the curb under his window with the rain bouncing off the
tank and Addie on it in a yellow rain shell he had never seen, and the spare
helmet already unstrapped and hanging from the handlebar, waiting.

She did not look up. She did not honk. She sat there.

He went down the stairs and out the door and across the sidewalk through the
rain, and he took the helmet off the handlebar, and put it on, and got on the
bike behind her, and put his arms around the yellow shell, and did not say a
word.

She kicked the engine over.

---

The river road was a river.

Not all of it. Most of it was only wet, the levee on their left gone dark green
and streaming, the water coming down its grass face in a hundred little
threads and pooling along the foot of it and running in the gutter beside them
brown and fast. But at the low place — where the road dipped, where the drains
sat in the curb with their mouths open, where she had shouted *that floods*
over her shoulder yesterday and he had looked at a grate — the road was gone.

A sheet of black water lay across it curb to curb, a hundred feet of it, with
the rain hammering the surface into a boil and a current in it, a real
current, all of it sliding one way toward the drain at the bottom of the dip.
The grate was under there somewhere. He could see the place where the water
went down — a swirl, a dimple, leaves and a plastic bottle and something white
circling it and going under — and the sound it made was the sound of a thing
being poured out that would not fill back up.

She slowed. She did not stop.

"Hold on," she said, and he did, and she took the bike into it.

The water came up over the footpegs. It came up over his sneakers, cold, and
pulled at them, and he felt the bike want to go with it, sideways, toward the
swirl, and he felt her not let it — her whole body set against it, her boots
down, walking the bike through the current with the engine growling low — and
for ten seconds there was nothing in the world but the pull of the water and
the drain at the bottom of it waiting, and then the front wheel found the far
slope and climbed, and they came up out of it streaming, and the road was a
road.

He did not let go of her for a long time after.

---

Riverbend on a Sunday in the rain was a parking lot with nobody in it.

The leasing office dark. The flag wrapped around its pole. Every balcony empty,
every car parked, every window with a light on and a curtain drawn against the
weather. She parked the bike on the Building C side in the exact space she had
parked it yesterday, and locked the helmets to it, and got off, and stood in
the rain looking at it.

"It'll be fine," she said. To the bike. "It's been rained on before."

Then she took the backpack off and opened it on the seat under the shelter of
her own body, and he saw what she had brought.

Two headlamps, the kind with the elastic bands, that she used for the trails.
A coil of rope, thin, yellow, the kind climbers used. A hand lens on a cord —
her jeweler's loupe, the one she always forgot, the one that lived in a drawer
at home and had never once made it out to a trail with her in four years. Two
bottles of water. A bag of the peanut things she liked. Her phone in a plastic
sleeve. And under all of it, flat against the back panel, the folder.

"You packed," he said.

"I told you I was going."

"You packed a *rope.*"

"I don't know what's down there." She put a headlamp in his hand. "Neither do
you. Put that on."

He put it on. The band was tight on his forehead over the hair. She put hers on
and clicked it and a white circle appeared on the wet asphalt between her
boots, and she looked at it, and looked at him, and swung the pack onto her
shoulders and pulled the straps tight.

"Okay," she said.

"Okay."

They went in under the stair.

---

The padlock hung open on the staple where she had left it. She lifted it off
and put it in her pocket again without a word. The steel door. The fourteen
steps. The smell coming up — wet, and burnt, and sweet, and stronger, he
thought, than yesterday, or he was more afraid than yesterday, which was the
same thing. The basement at the bottom of the steps with the water heater
humming and a new stain around the drain where last night's water had come in
and gone down, and the far wall, forty feet off, old brick to the floor, and
the door in it.

They crossed to it with the two white circles of the headlamps going ahead of
them over the rough concrete.

The line of ash at the sill had been swept away and laid again. He saw that.
Fresh gray, a clean line, and dry leaves on it silver-green, not curled, and
the saucer in the corner with its sweet smear was a new saucer, white, unchipped.
Somebody had come here since yesterday and done this. Somebody had opened this
door from the other side, and stepped over the line, and swept it, and laid it
again, and gone back through, and shut it.

"Addie —"

"I see it."

She put her hand on the iron ring.

"Wait." He said it before he knew he was going to. "Wait. Just — a second."

She waited. She stood with her hand on the ring and the headlamp throwing her
shadow up the old brick wall behind her huge and crooked, and she waited, and
did not push, and he stood there in the smell of sage with the rain coming
down on the building over their heads and made himself think it through, all
the way, the way he thought things through on a bus in a country where he did
not speak the language.

A stair under an orphanage. Something at the bottom of it that moved. A man
in a pawn shop who said *go in the daytime* and locked three locks behind
them. A woman in a coffee shop with her mouth at his ear. A necklace that made
him hard to find, and under it, right now, against his skin, the stone.

He touched it through his shirt.

"It stays on," he said. "Whatever's down there. It stays on. Say okay."

"Okay."

"And the door stays open. We don't close it behind us. Say okay."

"Okay. Felix —"

"Open it."

She opened it.

---

It dragged the way it had yesterday, the bottom edge scraping over the sill and
through the fresh ash, and then it went, all at once, the way a heavy door goes
when it has decided, and she did not stop it this time. It swung back against
the brick with a sound that went down the stair ahead of them and came back
smaller, twice, three times, going away.

The cold came through.

He had felt it yesterday through a gap the width of a hand. Now it came through
a door standing wide, a slow steady river of it, and it went past them and out
into the basement and he felt it go down the back of his neck and into his
shirt and settle, and it smelled of the river, and of stone, and of a place
that had never once in its life been in the sun.

The headlamps went down the stair.

Brick, the first steps, worn into dips. Then stone, gray, cut square, turning
to the right around a core of the same stone, going down. It was a bell tower.
He saw that now, with two lights instead of one: the stair went around and
around a central column the way the stair in a church tower did, except that
it went the wrong way, and the tower was under the ground instead of over it,
as if somebody had built a steeple and then buried it, or as if the ground had
come up around it, year by year, flood by flood, until the top of the tower was
the floor of a basement and the bell, if there had ever been a bell, was
somewhere far below in the dark with the river.

"Somebody built a tower," he said, "and the town came up over it."

"You don't know that."

"That's a bell stair. Look at it. That's a bell stair going down." His voice
was doing a thing he could not stop, going up at the ends. "I've been up a
hundred of these. Cork. Belfast. There's one in — that's a *bell stair,*
Addie, that's a church, there's a church under here, there's a —"

"Felix."

"— whole *church* —"

"*Felix.* Breathe."

He breathed.

"Ninety-four," she said. She had been counting under her breath and he had not
heard her. "I'm going to count. You count too. When we get to the bottom we'll
know how far it is to come back up."

They went down.

---

He counted. He got to thirty and lost it and got it back from her — she said
the numbers out loud, low, every tenth one, *forty, fifty* — and the stair
went around and around, and the brick walls of the tower core went by under his
hand, cold and wet, and the light from the door above them got smaller each time
they came around to the side where it was, a gray square, a gray slot, a gray
line, and then nothing.

At sixty the stone changed. It got older. He could not have said how he knew;
it was cut differently, the edges softer, the treads dished deeper by more
feet. At seventy there was water on the steps, a thin sheet of it running down
from somewhere above, from the basement floor maybe, from the rain, and their
sneakers went through it and it was cold enough to hurt.

At eighty he stopped.

"What."

"Listen."

They listened. Water, thin, running. Their breathing. The rain, if it was the
rain, a far dim roar through a great deal of stone. And under all of it —
not a sound. A pressure. The feeling of a big room somewhere below with air
moving in it, the feeling a house has at night when someone is awake in
another part of it.

"Keep going," she said. Her voice had gone flat. He looked at her in the light
of his lamp and her face was white under the freckles and her hand was pressed
to her stomach, and she saw him look, and took the hand away.

"You're sick."

"I'm fine. It's the — it's the dark. It's the cold. Keep going."

*Ninety, ninety-one, ninety-two.*

Ninety-four, and the stair ended, and the floor under his sneaker was not a
step, and his light went out ahead of him into a space that swallowed it.

---

Sage. Right there at the foot of the stair, a line of it across the last step,
ash and leaves, fresh, and a saucer, and beside the saucer a lamp — an actual
lamp, glass and brass, the kind you filled with oil, unlit, set on the bottom
step by somebody who had come this far with it and gone on without it.

He crouched and looked at it without touching it. The glass was clean. The
brass was clean. The wick was trimmed square and the oil in the well came
halfway up the glass, fresh, with no dust on it, and the whole lamp sat on the
old stone the way a thing sits that is put there every day by the same hand.
It was not left. It was *kept.* Somebody kept a lamp at the foot of this stair
the way you keep a porch light on, for whoever might come down it — or for
whoever went up it, in the dark, and needed to find their way home.

Addie made a sound. Not a word. She had her hand on her stomach again and she
did not take it away this time.

He put his light up.

A street.

He did not have another word for it and he did not try to find one. A street.
Wider than the stair had led him to expect, wide enough for a cart, and paved —
paved with brick, old brick laid in a herringbone that he could see under the
silt and the wet and the hundred and fifty years — running away from the foot
of the tower in both directions into a dark his lamp could not get to the end
of. And on either side of it, walls. Not tunnel walls. Building walls. Brick,
and cypress beams gone black, and doorways, real doorways with lintels over
them, and windows, real windows with the glass long gone and the frames still
there, square holes of deeper dark looking out on a street where nobody had
walked in the sun since before the war that had burned the town on top of it.

The ceiling was low. Ten feet, twelve, a rough vault of brick and packed earth
and roots, roots coming down through it in places like the fingers of a hand,
and in one place, far down the street on the left, a square of light that was
not his — faint, yellow, the color of a lamp behind a curtain.

He turned his own light off his hand and put it on the nearest doorway.

A cypress lintel, thick as his thigh, gone silver where the wet had not gotten
it and black where it had. And on the lintel, cut into the wood in capitals a
hand high, the letters worn but there, the way letters are on a gravestone
you have to tilt your head to read:

L E M O I N E

And under it, smaller, in the same hand: *marchand.*

He said it out loud. He did not know he was going to. "Lemoine. Merchant.
That's — that's a shop. That was a shop. There was a street here and there
were shops on it and a man named Lemoine sold things out of this door and the
river came up and — " He stopped. He could hear himself. He could hear the
thing his voice was doing, the thing it did on a good day on a bus in a
country he loved, the thing that was joy, and he heard it now, here, at the
bottom of ninety-four steps under an orphanage in a town where a woman had put
her mouth to his ear, and he did not know how it could be joy and it was.
"Addie. The city's built on top of this. The whole city. They just — they
built on top of it."

"I know."

"Nobody knows this is here."

"Somebody knows."

She was looking at the lamp on the bottom step. At the saucer. At the sage.

He looked at his phone. One bar, at the top of the stair, when he had checked
it in the basement. Now, in the plastic sleeve she had given him, at the foot
of a buried street: nothing. Not one bar. Not the word *searching.* A phone
that did not know where it was.

The rain was a rumor. The street went off into the dark both ways. Far down it
on the left the square of yellow light did not move, and did not go out, and
the cold came along the brick past his ankles from the direction of the river,
and Addie stood beside him with her hand flat on her stomach and her face gone
the color of the ash on the step, and said, in the flat voice, so quietly that
he almost did not hear it over the sound of his own heart:

"There's somebody coming."
