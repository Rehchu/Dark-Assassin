# Followed

She had felt it at eighty steps and told him it was the cold.

It was not the cold. She knew what the cold felt like; the cold was on her
skin. This was under it — the same turn beneath the breastbone that had put her
hand on the bike seat Thursday and on the table at Ugly Mug and on the brick
beside the wooden door, the wet mouth, the horizon going wrong — and it was not
coming from anywhere she could point to. It was coming from the dark. From
down the street, to the right, away from the little square of yellow light
that was the only thing in the world that looked like somebody lived there.

"There's somebody coming," she said, and Felix said "Where?" and she could not
tell him, because she could not hear anything, and he could not hear anything,
and she stood at the foot of ninety-four steps under the city with her hand on
her stomach and her headlamp pointed down a street nobody had walked in the sun
for a hundred and fifty years, and *knew,* the way you know a stove is on
before you touch it.

"Which way," Felix said.

"That way." Right. Into the dark, away from the light. "Don't go that way."

"I wasn't going to go *that* way, I wasn't going to go any way, we're at the
bottom of a —" He stopped. His light was on the lintel with the name cut in
it. LEMOINE. His hand was at his collar. "Okay. Okay. Left. Toward the light.
Somebody lit a lamp. Lamps are people. People are — people are better than
whatever you're feeling."

"You don't know that."

"I don't know anything. Come on."

He went left. She went with him, because the alternative was standing still,
and the thing under her breastbone said that standing still was the wrong
thing to do with whatever was coming down the street behind them.

---

The street was a street the whole way.

That was the part she could not get her head around. She had expected a
tunnel, a sewer, a cave — the things a person expected under a town. This was
a street. The brick under her boots was laid in a pattern. There were curbs.
There was a doorway every twenty feet on both sides, and in the doorways her
lamp found the insides of rooms — a counter, a fallen shelf, a stair going up
into packed earth where a second floor had been before the ground ate it. A
window with the frame still in it and, on the sill, a bottle, green, with a
neck like a swan's, standing where somebody had set it down and walked away in
another century. The ceiling came and went overhead, brick vault and then
earth and then vault again, with roots hanging down through it in the earth
places like the fringe on a shawl, and water dripping from the roots, and the
smell — river, stone, sage, and under those, faintly, everywhere, woodsmoke.

Somebody was burning wood down here. Somebody was cooking.

Felix's light went from doorway to doorway and his mouth was going the whole
time, low, under his breath, the way it went on a good day: *look at that,
that's a shop, that's a house, look at the lintel, that's cypress, that's a
hundred and fifty years old, look —* and she let him, because it was the sound
of him not being afraid, and because as long as he was reading lintels he was
not listening to her breathe.

The light ahead got bigger. It was a doorway. A real one, with a curtain across
it, heavy cloth gone the color of the street, and the yellow light came around
the edges of the curtain and lay on the brick outside it in a shape like a
welcome mat.

Behind them, the sickness came down the street like weather.

She stopped.

"Felix."

"What."

"It's closer."

"What is?"

She turned. She put her lamp down the street the way they had come, back
toward the tower, and there was nothing — brick, doorways, the dark, the swan
bottle catching the light on its sill — and the nothing was worse than a
thing would have been, because the nothing was where it was coming from. She
could feel it the way you feel a hand behind your head in the dark. Closer.
Moving. Not fast. Not hurrying.

*Hurry was seen.* She did not know where the thought came from.

"Addie, there's nothing —"

"*Listen.*"

He listened. She watched his face change. It took him a full minute after her
— she would remember that, later, the length of that minute — and then he
heard it too, and she saw him hear it: a step, far off, on brick. Not one step.
Several. Careful. Coming along the street from the direction of the tower and
the dark beyond the tower, where she had said *don't go that way.*

And a light.

Small. Bobbing. Yellow-white, the light of a lamp carried by someone walking,
far down the street behind them and coming on, and in the edge of its glow as
it swung — just once, for the length of a step — something caught it and
flashed. Two flashes. Small, close together, at the height of a man's face.

Glass. Sunglasses.

*Sunglasses,* she thought, stupidly, *under the ground,* and the sickness came
up her throat all the way and she turned to Felix to say *run* —

— and the net came down.

---

It came from the doorway. From the light.

She did not see it; she felt it, a weight of wet cord dropping over her from
above and behind, rope, knotted, a mesh of it, the way a cast net drops on
water — heavy at the edges with something, weights, lead, so that it fell
around them and pulled tight at the bottom in the same second, and she was
inside it with Felix and the backpack and both headlamps swinging wild, and she
did the only thing there was to do, which was fight it.

She got her hand up through a hole in the mesh. The cord burned across her
wrist as somebody pulled the net tight, a hot line drawn over the skin from the
bone to the heel of her hand, and she yelled, and Felix yelled, and his light
went spinning across a ceiling of roots and came down and she heard his knee
hit the brick — heard it, a sound like a dropped melon — and heard the breath go
out of him with it.

"Don't fight — don't fight it, *cher,* don't, you'll hurt yourself —"

A voice. A woman's. Close, and low, and frightened. Not of them.

"Get them up. Get them *up,* they're coming, get them off the street —"

Hands.

A lot of hands, all at once, through the mesh and under her arms and around
her, hauling — and they were not cruel. That was the thing she would keep. She
had been grabbed before, in a bar in Baton Rouge once, by a man who had wanted
her to know he could, and she knew what cruel hands felt like, and these were
not those. These were hands that shook. Hands that held her the way you hold a
thing you are afraid will break, or bite, or both, and hauled her up off the
brick and through the curtain into the light, and she came through it with the
net still over her and the rope burning her wrist and her lamp pointed at the
floor, and the floor was swept.

Swept brick. A room. Lamps on the walls. And faces.

She got one look at them before the hood came down — a dozen, more, pale the
way things are pale that have been kept from the sun, men and women in layers
of patched cloth, clean, and every one of those faces was doing the same thing,
which was looking past her, at the curtain, at the street beyond it, with the
same look she had seen on the woman across the counter at the pawn shop, on
the man at the pawn shop, on her mother on the stairs looking at the blinds.

They were afraid. Not of her. Of what was coming down the street.

Then the cloth came over her head and the world went brown and close and
smelled of smoke.

---

"Bar it. *Bar it.* Étienne, the far door — go, go —"

Feet. A lot of feet on brick. A door somewhere, heavy, and a sound she knew
from the basement, the sound of a wooden door meeting its frame like a word,
and then another sound after it, a scrape and a thud, wood on wood, a bar
dropping into brackets.

Above them. Far above. She could not have said how she knew it was above.

"Felix." Her voice came out muffled through the cloth. "Felix, are you —"

"I'm here. My knee. I'm — I'm here."

He was close. Somebody had put them side by side on the floor with their backs
against something that felt like a wall and the net gone off them now and the
hoods on, and she could feel his shoulder against hers through the cloth and
the yellow shell, and she leaned into it.

"Are they —"

"I don't know."

"Shh," said the woman's voice. Not unkind. "Shh, now. Nobody's going to hurt
you. Nobody here. Just — stay down. Stay quiet. Let them go by."

*Let them go by.*

She stayed quiet. She could feel her wrist bleeding a little into her sleeve
and her stomach gone to water and the smoke-smell of the hood, and she could
feel the room around her holding its breath, twenty people, thirty, not
moving, and on the other side of the curtain — she knew it the way she had
known the stove — the sickness came along the street, and slowed at the
doorway, and stood there.

Nobody in the room breathed.

Then it went on. Down the street, the way she and Felix had been going, toward
whatever was at the end of it, taking the sickness with it a step at a time,
and the room let its breath out in one long shaking sigh that she felt against
her skin through the cloth like wind.

"Missed us," somebody said. A man. "By a minute. By one *minute,* Odile —"

"I know how long it was."

"If he'd been a minute —"

"He wasn't. Get the lamps up. Get the boy's leg looked at. And get the pack
off her — careful — and take it in to Anatole, and *don't open it,* you hear
me, not one of you."

Hands at her shoulders. The straps. The pack going off her back, the weight of
the folder going with it, and she made a sound and reached for it blind
through the hood and a hand caught her hand — a small hand, a child's — and
held it, and did not let go.

"It's okay." A boy. Ten, eleven. High and quick and right next to her ear.
"It's okay, they're just gonna hold it. Anatole holds things. He'll give it
back. He gives everything back, that's — that's the whole thing about him."
A pause. The small hand tightened. "You feel it too, huh. When they come. You
went white like a sheet out there, I saw you, you felt them a whole minute
before he did." Another pause, and then, with something in it that she had
not heard in a voice since her mother's on the stairs: "It's okay. I feel it
worse than anybody. That's why they make me run."

She could not say anything. She held the hand.

"You're Miss Jan's girl," the boy said. "Aren't you. You've got her face."

---

They took the hoods off in a smaller room.

A side vault, brick, with a lamp on a shelf and two stools and a bucket of
water and a rag, and the woman — Odile, the others had called her, Odile,
somewhere near fifty, gray in a braid, a face that was kind and had decided to
be kind on purpose a long time ago — kneeling in front of Felix with his jeans
rolled up over a knee that had already gone the color of a plum.

"That's going to be ugly," Odile said. "It's not broke. You'll walk on it.
You'll hate it for a week." She wrapped it. She wrapped it the way a person
does who has wrapped a lot of knees. "You. Let me see that wrist."

Addie held it out. Odile turned it to the lamp, and cleaned it with the rag,
and looked at Addie's face while she did it, and Addie looked back.

"You're afraid of them," Addie said. "The ones with the — the ones who went
by."

Odile did not answer. She wrapped the wrist.

"Who are you?"

"We live here," Odile said. "That's who we are. We've always lived here." She
tied off the cloth. "Anatole will talk to you. Anatole talks. I don't." She
stood. "The boy's phone's dead, and yours is nearly. There's no signal down
here and there never will be. The door at the top of the stair is barred from
this side and it stays barred tonight. Nobody's going up tonight. Not you. Not
us. Not with them on the street." She picked up the lamp. "I'm sorry. I am. I
know what it is to want up."

She went out.

Addie got her phone out of the plastic sleeve. Nine percent. No bars, no
*searching,* the little clock still going in the corner as if it had somewhere
to be. The last thing on the screen was the photograph she had taken behind
Building C on Saturday — old brick in the grass, her boot in the frame for
scale — and she looked at it for a second, the sky in the top of the picture
gray and ordinary and a hundred and fifty years away, and then she turned the
phone off to keep what was left, and put it back in the sleeve, and the sleeve
in her jacket, against the place where the necklace had ridden home on Thursday.

The boy was in the doorway. He had been there the whole time, Addie realized,
small and quiet against the frame with a lamp of his own, watching her with
enormous eyes. Ten. Eleven. Pale as the rest of them, with a mouth that wanted
to say things and had been told not to.

"Ti-Jean," he said. "That's me. I run." He looked at Felix. He looked at
Felix's face, at the sides of it, at the hair over the ears, the way the man
in the pawn shop had looked, the way the woman at Ugly Mug had looked, the way
everyone in the world apparently looked and nobody said. And then he said it.
The thing nobody said. He said it the way a child says a thing, because it was
true and nobody had told him not to.

"You're the one," Ti-Jean said. "You're the one she's been waiting for."

Then he was gone, quick, down the vault, and his lamp with him.

---

The outer room of the hall had a door, and the door had a gap under it, and
light came through the gap, and voices.

They sat against the wall beside it, the two of them, on the swept brick, with
the lamp on the shelf and the folder gone and the phones dead and the door
above them barred from below. His knee was up on the stool. Her wrist was in
her lap. Somewhere behind her, four hundred yards of dark and ninety-four
steps and a cypress door and a basement and a lot and a bike in the rain and
the river road and two miles and a house that smelled of sage with her mother
in it, who had said *don't go in the basement,* who was known down here by her
first name, who had a face that a boy under the city had seen before.

*You're Miss Jan's girl.*

"He said you're the one," Felix said. Flat. To the wall.

"I heard him."

"The one *she's* been waiting for."

"I heard him, Felix."

"Who's *she?*"

Addie did not answer. She was listening to the voices through the door.

One of them was low and slow and tired, a man's, with something under the
English that was not English, saying a thing over and over in different words
— *they're ours, they came to us, they're in my hall* — and the other was
quicker, and lighter, and angrier, and it cut across the first one like a knife
across a rope, and it was saying a thing it wanted the whole room to hear.

"— the truce is the truce. You made me say it in front of everybody so I'll
say it in front of everybody. *Whoever comes down the door uninvited is
presented to her.* That's the third term. That's Grandmère's own words. They
came down the door. They're presented. You don't get to keep them because
you're scared, Anatole, and you don't get to keep them because the old man
behind the rail asked you to — yeah, I know he asked you, everybody knows,
Ti-Jean can't keep his mouth shut — you *present* them. Tonight. Or you tell
all forty of us why the truce holds for everybody but you."

Silence on the other side of the door.

Addie put her good hand flat on the brick beside her. It was cold. The lamp
on the shelf burned steady. Beside her Felix had gone very still, with his
hand at his collar, over the cord, over the stone, and she could hear him
breathing, and she could hear, through the wall, the rain — the rumor of it,
far up, coming down on a town that did not look down, and never had, and was
not going to start tonight.
