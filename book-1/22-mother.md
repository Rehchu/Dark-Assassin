# Mother

Alcide Fontenot came over the rail at ten o'clock with a crowbar in one hand
and a flashlight in the other and stopped on the far side of the iron like a
man who has walked into a church in the middle of a funeral.

He had come down ninety-four steps on a heart the parish doctor had told him
not to take up a flight of stairs with. He had come along two hundred yards of
a street with faces turned to the wall and past a hall where a boy with a lamp
had pointed him at the river way without a word. He had come four hundred
yards of brick in the dark with the flashlight going yellow, and he had a
prayer in his mouth that he had not said since 1986, and Felix watched him
stand there at the rail and look at what he had come down to, and watched the
prayer stop.

"Jan," Alcide said.

Janet was sitting up. She had gotten herself against the altar stone with her
hand on her side and her left eye shut and swollen, and she looked at him
across the crypt with the other one.

"You came to the door," she said.

"I said I would."

"You said you couldn't come down it."

"I said a lot of things." He looked at the man on the cot. He looked at him a
long time. He had not seen Darneil Delvaux since a night of rain when a
younger man had come through the back door of a pawn shop with a boy asleep
on his shoulder and a necklace in his fist and had asked for nothing but a
towel. "Delvaux."

Darneil's eyes came round to him. The mouth worked on its good side.

"Fontenot." Thick. "You're old."

"You're worse."

Nobody laughed. It was not that kind of room. But something went out of the
air that had been in it since the arrow, and Alcide came over the iron with
the crowbar held away from himself like a thing he was ashamed of and sat down
on the crate by the cot, and put the flashlight on the floor, and put his
hands on his knees, and Felix saw that they were shaking the way his own had
shaken at the rail last night, and that the old man was letting them.

---

They made a plan. He heard it made.

He was on the floor with his back against the brick and Addie beside him, not
touching, her right hand in her lap where she kept looking at it, and Janet
and Alcide and Anatole made a plan over his father's cot the way three
frightened people make a plan, in short sentences, each one checking the last.
First light. The door, because the door was Anatole's again and the stair was
clear. The truck at the top of it with the engine running. The river road.
Rapides Regional, the emergency entrance, a man found wandering by the levee
with his right side gone; no name till they had to give one. Janet would give
it. Janet would give a lot of things.

"He can't be carried four hundred yards," Odile said.

"He can," Anatole said. "I'll carry him."

"With that hand?"

"With the other one."

Felix listened to it. He did not say anything. He had a thing to say and it
was not to any of them, and the longer he sat against the brick with the cold
in his hands and the weight in his pocket the more certain he was of it, until
it was not a thing he was thinking about anymore but a thing he was doing, and
he stood up.

Addie's head came round.

"No," she said.

"I haven't said anything."

"You don't have to. You stood up." She was on her feet. It hurt her; he saw
her hand not work on the brick when she pushed off it. "Felix. *No.*"

"She's got three people on stretchers who are somebody's," he said. "Loretta
Broussard. Curtis Dupre. A boy waiting for a ride. She said she'd give them
back. She said it was up to me."

"She said *come out with it off* —"

"I did that." He said it quietly. "I already did that, Addie. Out there. It's
done. She's seen me. There's nothing left she can take off me that she hasn't
already got." He touched his pocket. "Except this. And I'm not giving her
this. I'm going to go down there and I'm going to get those three people and
I'm going to come back, and that's all."

"That's not all."

"No," he said. "It's not."

Janet had turned. Alcide had turned. His father, on the cot, had turned the
half of his face that could turn, and it was his father he looked at, and his
father who said it, thick, from the pillow:

"She won't hurt you."

"I know."

"That's not — " The tongue fought him. "Boy. That's not the danger."

"I know what the danger is," Felix said. "I've known since the letter."

Addie put herself in front of him. Not touching. She had learned in a room
with cloth on the walls what it did when she touched him, and she stood in
front of him with her ruined hand at her side and her face gone white under
the freckles, and she said, very low, so that it was for him: "Then take me."

"She can see you."

"I don't care."

"I do." He looked at her. "She said *you I can see just fine.* She meant it.
She'll put a hand on you to make me do a thing, and I'll do it. Whatever it
is. You know I will. I can hide you from her when I've got this off and the
shadow up, and I'm not taking it off again, so I can't, so you can't come. If
you come I'll do whatever she says. That's the truth. I'm telling you the
truth."

She stood there. He watched her know it.

"Ti-Jean will walk me to the square," he said. "He knows the crack. He'll
wait at the top of it. If I'm not up by one — " He did not know what came
after that. "Then go up at first light like you said. All of you. That's the
plan. It's a good plan. Do it without me."

"Felix — "

"I love you," he said. It was the first time he had said it in front of
anyone. He had not planned it; it came out of him plain, the way things came
out of him when he was scared. "I'm coming back. I'm going to go tell my
mother no to her face, and I'm going to come back, and you can hit me then."

He stepped past her, and over the rail, and the quiet came off him like a coat
coming off, and his hands, which had been cold inside it, were colder.

---

Ti-Jean walked ahead with the lamp and did not look at him.

The river way was empty. Whatever the town had done with Armand's two who had
gone down under the Dauzat girls, it had done, and the brick was wet and dark
and only brick. At the outfall bend the lamp threw Felix's shadow up the
curved wall ahead of him, big, bent at the shoulders, and he watched it go
along the brick and it was only a shadow, only the lamp, only his own shape
with the light behind it, and he thought: *that's what it looks like from
outside. That's all it is.* He did not believe it. He watched it anyway.

"Your hands," Ti-Jean said, at the bend. Not turning.

"Yes."

"They stay like that?"

"I don't know."

The boy walked a while.

"My mama's stayed like that," he said. "After. She held the door for a woman
once, on the old street, when the woman was walking it. Twenty years ago.
Just held it. Her hands went cold and they never came back." He shifted the
lamp. "She's fine. She just wears gloves."

Felix did not know what to say to that, and said nothing, and they came out
of the river way into the old street.

It was lit. That was the thing he had not expected. Every lamp in the town
was lit, on every sill and in every doorway of the buried street, the way you
light a house after a death, and people stood in the doors, and they did not
turn their faces to the wall. They looked at him. A boy from up top with cold
hands and blood on his sleeve and a lamp-carrying child in front of him,
walking the length of their street toward the square where the crack was, and
they looked, and one of them — an old woman in a doorway with a baby on her
hip that was not hers — said "Delvaux" as he passed, not loud, and he did not
know if it was a curse or a blessing or only his name, and he kept walking.

At the square Ti-Jean stopped.

"Here."

The well. The lifted brick where Addie had gone down on her knees. The far
side, past the well, where the brick floor of the square was cracked open like
a thing that had been cracked before anyone laid a brick, a black mouth with
the cold coming up out of it, and cloth, far down, at the end of it, gray.

"I'll wait," the boy said. "Till one. Like you said."

"Ti-Jean."

"What."

"If I come up and I'm — " He did not know how to say it. "If I come up and
I'm not right. If I come up and you feel it. The way you feel her."

The boy looked at him for the first time since the rail. Eleven years old,
with the lamp in both hands, and the face of a person who has been the runner
for the whole of his life because he feels the weather worst.

"Then I'll run," Ti-Jean said. "I'm good at it."

"Good."

He went down the crack.

---

He had been down it before with a lantern ahead of him and men behind. He went
down it now alone, in the dark, with one hand on the wet stone, and found he
could see.

Not well. Not the way you see with a lamp. The way you see a room you have
been in before with the light off — the shapes of it, where the floor drops,
where the stone leans. He had never been able to do that. He had walked into
door frames in his own apartment at night for three weeks because he would not
buy curtains and could not find the switch. He went down a hundred feet of a
crack in the earth below the water table with no light at all and did not put
a foot wrong, and he knew what it was, and he did not stop to be afraid of it.

The cloth. Gray, at the end. He put his hand on it, and it was cold and dry
and heavier than cloth, and he held it back, and went in.

The room was the room.

Candle in a dish of black glass. Cloth on cloth on cloth, gray on gray, going
up into a dark that was the only ceiling. The low table. On it, the
tea-colored vial, stoppered; the bone-handled knife, folded; a length of cord;
the little brass box. And on the floor on the far side of it, sitting with her
back straight and her hands open on her knees, in black jeans and a gray shirt
with no apron, with her hair down over her ears, the woman from the counter at
Ugly Mug Coffee, who had started on Thursday.

Off to the right, through a second hanging of cloth, he could see the ends of
three stretchers. Two men sat against the wall beside them with their eyes on
the floor. Neither one looked up.

Armand was not in the room. Felix looked, and he was not, and then he saw the
shape by the far cloth, low, sitting on his heels with his back against the
gray, sunglasses pushed up into his hair, and the shape was Armand, and he was
not looking at anything.

"Cyprien," his mother said.

---

She did not get up.

He had thought she would. He had thought — he had thought a lot of things, in
the crack, and none of them had been her sitting on the floor of her room with
her hands open, saying a word he did not know, and not getting up.

"That's your name," she said. "I wrote it on a paper the day you were born
and put the paper in that box with a piece of your hair, and I've said it
once in eighteen years, out loud, in this room, to that candle, on a night I
was very tired. Cyprien. It was a sorcerer's name before it was a saint's. I
gave you the sorcerer and let the saint be somebody else's trouble." Her eyes
were brown. They were only brown. "Your father gave you *Felix* on a doorstep
in the rain because he thought you'd need the luck. He was right. You did."

"I'm not — "

"You don't have to be. It's a name in a box." She tipped her head at the
table. "Open it, if you want. It's yours. I'm not going to touch you, baby.
Sit down."

He did not sit down. He stood inside the cloth with his cold hands at his
sides and the stretchers in the corner of his eye, and he looked at her, and
she let him.

She was not what she had been at the rail. Whatever she had been there — the
red, the shadow lying along the brick like a tide, the thing she had said to
his father across the iron — was gone down into her, and what sat on the floor
was a tall woman with old hands who looked tired in a way he had not seen in
a face before and could not stop seeing. Not the tired of a shift. The tired
of a thing carried a long way and set down.

"You put it on," she said.

"What?"

"The not-being-seen. Over the girl. Out there." Something happened at the
side of her mouth. "I taught it to your father in a year when I was teaching
him things and he never once got it to take. You did it the first time,
without a word, with a mouthful of shadow that didn't want you. You did it
*angry.*" She looked at him. "You were very good."

"I don't want to be good at it."

"No. You want to be a boy with a laptop in a coffee shop who's kind to
waitresses." She said it without any cruelty at all. "I know what you want. I
stood at your table five mornings and I heard what you want. You want to see
the world before you're fifty and help people and be left alone. Those are
good wants, Cyprien. They're mine. I wanted every one of them, at your age,
in this room. Except the last."

"Don't call me that."

"All right."

She meant it. He saw that she meant it, and it was the worst thing in the
room.

"Felix," she said. "Sit down. I'm not going to touch you and I'm not going to
lie to you and I'm going to say some things, and then you're going to say a
thing, and I know what it is, and I'm going to let you say it. Then you can
take those three and go. That's what's going to happen. I'm telling you
because nobody has ever told you what's going to happen before it happened,
in your whole life, and I'm your mother, and it's the one thing I can do for
you tonight that nobody else can."

He sat down.

Across the low table from her. On the floor, with the candle between them and
the brass box at his knee and the knife folded by her hand, and he put his
cold hands on his knees the way hers were on hers, and did not know he had
done it until he saw her see it.

---

"I want you," she said.

Plain. Like the letter.

"Down here. With me. Not tonight; I'm not a fool. But I want you to know
what's on the table, so that when you say no you know what you said no to,
and I'm going to lay it out the way I'd lay out a bargain, because it is one,
and you deserve a bargain and not a story.

"I want you to learn to let it. What you did in the tunnel — you *forced* it,
and it came badly, and it took your hands, and that's the only way you'll ever
have it up top with that piece of iron under your bed and a girl in the next
room who feels sick when you breathe. Down here I can teach you to let it. A
year. Two. It comes to you like a dog to a hand. It doesn't cost you
anything. Your hands come back."

"And in exchange."

"In exchange, nothing." She let him hear that. "The three go up tonight either
way. The town gets left alone. Anatole keeps his door and his people and his
little school; I've got no more use for it. The girl — " Her eyes did not
move off him. "The girl goes home to her mother's house that smells like
sage, and I never stand across from it again, and she grows old. I mean that.
I've never had to lie to get what I wanted and I'm not going to start on my
son."

"Then what do you want."

"I told you. You." She looked at the candle. "A son who can look at me."

The candle burned. Somewhere off to the right, one of the men against the
wall breathed out, long, and did not look up.

"Do you know what it is," she said, "to have a thing on you your whole life
that makes an eye slide? Every eye. Your mother's. The people in this town
turn their faces to the wall — that's not fear, baby, they think it's fear,
it's the *lie.* It comes up on its own. I can't put it down. I have not been
looked at, straight, by a person who saw me, since I was younger than you,
and then only by your father, and only for a year, and he was — " She
stopped. It was the first time she had stopped. "He was a man who'd just lost
his own father and needed something, and it came to him the way it comes to
the ones who need it, and then he had you, and he needed *you* more, and it
left him. That's all that happened to your father. Nobody ever tells him
that."

"He knows."

"He doesn't. He thinks he chose."

Felix said nothing.

"You looked at me," she said. "Through a rail, with your hands on the iron,
with the red in me, and you didn't slide. Then in the tunnel with the red in
you, and you didn't. Then just now, through that cloth, with your mother
sitting on the floor with her hands open, and you *don't.* You're the only
thing in the world that's ever done it. I made you to." She said it quietly,
and it was the truest thing she had said, and she knew it, and she let it sit
on the table between them next to the knife. "I'm not going to pretend I
didn't."

---

"There's a third letter," he said.

She looked at him.

"Three. He read me the first one. First blood. The second's first shadow, he
said, and the third's — he didn't say the third. He said *the third one.*" He
did not look at the folded knife. "You wrote them the week I was born. In the
hand nobody up top can read. What does the third one say."

"It's sealed."

"You wrote it."

"I did."

"Then you know."

She was quiet a moment. Then she closed her eyes.

He had not seen her close them. Not at the counter, not in the room, not at
the rail. She closed them the way a person does who is going to say a thing
from memory and does not want to see the room while she says it, and she said
it. Low. In the voice she used on a Tuesday to a boy at a table with a
laptop. In English, for him, translating as she went out of the hand her
mother had taught her, and she did not stumble on a word.

"*One day you will end a thing that was alive.*

"*It will not be what you think. It will not be a fight and it will not be
in the dark and you will not have wanted it. You will have your hand on a
shoulder and you will be looking at a face and there will be a moment when
you could stop, and you will feel the line lean into your hand the way water
leans into a low place, and you will let it, because letting it is easier
than holding it back, and that is the whole of what we are. Afterward you
will sit down, because that is what the light does when it leaves a body, and
you will not know if the one who sat was you or them.*

"*That is the third turning. After it, there is no going up top. Not because
anyone will stop you. Because you will not want to go.*

"*I am writing this to you on the seventh day of your life with your hair in
a box beside me and I am not sorry. I am telling you so that when it comes
you will not think you are the first, or the worst, or alone. You are the
last. You are the last of a thing that came across the water in the bottom
of a boat and outlived every god it was ever given. You are not alone. I am
in the dark and I am looking back.*

"*When it is done, come and find me. I will hear you. I have always heard
you.*"

She opened her eyes.

"*Cyprien,*" she said. "That's the line under it. Same as the first."

The candle burned.

He found that he was crying. Not the way he had cried in the sleeping room
with the tin mirror, with Addie holding him; the other way, the way you cry
when your face does it without asking, and he let it, because there was
nothing in the room that could see him do it that did not already know, and
his mother sat across the low table from him with her hands open and did not
move to touch him, and that was the tenderest thing anyone had ever done for
him, and it was a hunter's letter, and she had written it to a baby, and she
had meant every word.

"That's what I want," she said. "For you not to be alone when it comes. That
was all I ever wanted. Ask your father what I said to him the night you were
born. Ask him if I said anything else."

"He said you didn't hold me."

"I held you with my eyes open," she said. "The whole first night. He was
asleep. Somebody had to be."

---

He put his hand in his pocket.

She saw him do it. He watched her see. Something came up in her face and went
down again, fast, the way the red went down, and she did not move, and he
took the necklace out of his jeans.

The beads. The waxed cord with the knot in it his father had tied in the rain.
The black stone with her mother's mothers' mark on it, a stem and two tines.
And the ring, gold, old, soft at the edges, threaded on the same cord now
where he had put it in the hold on Monday with his father watching. He held
it in his cold hand in the candlelight where she could see every bead of it,
and he thought about the first time — a coffee shop, a Thursday, a folder, a
joke, *how does it look,* Addie's face going strange across the table, a
waitress who had started today coming to the table and saying *what a lovely
stone* — and he did not laugh, and he did not joke, and he lifted the cord in
both hands and put it over his head.

It settled. The stone found the place at his collarbone where it had lain
since Friday, five days, after fourteen years in wax in a box waiting for
him, and the ring lay beside it, and the cord
was cold on the back of his neck, and his hands were cold, and nothing in him
changed at all.

He looked at her.

"How does it look," he said.

She did not answer for a long moment.

"Like a thing I made a man make for me," she said finally, "twenty years ago,
so I could go up top and not be looked at." Her voice had not changed. "It
doesn't hide you. Not from me. You know that. He told you, and you did it
anyway, and it was the first thing you ever decided on your own in your life
and you decided it standing in a sewer with no light. It'll hide you from
everything else. There are other things. There'll be other things, baby, in
other towns, and they won't be your mother, and they won't want to sit across
a table from you and not touch you. Keep it on. Not for the reason he'll give
you."

"I know why I'm keeping it on."

"Tell me."

"So that you can find me," Felix said, "and I can still say no."

---

She laughed.

He had never heard it. Nobody in the town had heard it; the two men by the
wall lifted their heads and put them down again like men who had heard a
thing they would not tell anyone. It was not a big laugh. It was one breath,
out through the nose, with her head going back a little, and it was the laugh
of a woman who has spent forty years unable to be looked at and has just been
told, by the one person who can, exactly why he is going to keep doing it.

"Oh, you're *mine,*" she said. "Take them. Go on. They're a gift."

"A gift."

"A mother's gift. You don't get to refuse it; that's the rule; it's the only
rule I know that isn't mine." She turned her head. "Armand."

The shape by the far cloth moved.

"Your two. Up. They'll carry. They'll carry the three to the rail and they'll
come back down and they will not touch a hair on anyone in that crypt, and
if they do, you and I will have a conversation, and you know what my
conversations are." She had not raised her voice. Armand stood up. "The boy's
walking home. Nobody follows him. Nobody follows the girl. Nobody follows the
woman with the door on her wall. You go up with me tomorrow, in a car, and
that's the last time anyone in this town says your name."

"Ma'am," Armand said.

"Go on."

He went. He passed Felix without a glance, sunglasses down, and went through
the second cloth, and the two men by the wall stood, and there were sounds of
lifting, and the ends of the stretchers moved, and Felix sat where he was on
the floor with the necklace on and his mother across the table and did not
watch them go.

"You'll let them," he said. "Really."

"I said."

"And the town."

"I said."

"And Addie."

"I said, baby." She looked at him. "I don't need any of it. I have what I came
for. I know what you look like. I know what you sound like when you say no.
I'll know where you are every minute you're alive, from now on, the way you
know where your own hand is in the dark, and you'll wear that thing and it
won't make a bit of difference, and you know it, and you're wearing it so you
can say no to me for the rest of your life, and that's the only thing you've
ever done that I'd have done myself." She stood.

He had not seen her stand. She was up, and tall, and the candle was below her
now, throwing her shadow up the cloth, and it was only a shadow, only the
candle, and she looked down at him across the table and the brass box and the
knife.

"I told you I'd hear you," she said.

He got up.

He stood across the low table from her with the necklace on and the ring on
the same cord and his cold hands at his sides, a short heavy boy with green
eyes and pointed ears under his hair, and looked at his mother, and did not
slide off, and she looked back, and neither of them said anything, and then
he turned and went out through the cloth into the crack, and up.

---

She sat down again after he was gone.

The candle had burned down a finger's width since she lit it. The cloth
breathed the way it always breathed. She put her hands on her knees and
looked at the brass box, which she had not opened in eighteen years and which
he had not opened either, and she thought that she would not open it now,
because there was nothing in it she needed. His hair was on his head. His name
was in his mouth, refused, which was the same as kept.

The ache was there. She had expected it. It had been there since the tunnel,
since the red went down in him and his face came up out of it and did not
slide; it was the old ache, the one the shadow left when it came back with a
little less quiet than it had gone out with, and it was another one under
that, that she had no practice with at all, that had come up in her when he
put his hands on his knees the way hers were and did not know he had done
it.

She let it. That was the whole of what she was.

He had said *so that you can find me.* He had said it with the stone at his
throat and his father's ring beside it and the knot in the cord that a
frightened man had tied in the rain, and he had meant it, and he had meant
the rest of it too, and she turned it over in the candlelight the way she had
turned the smudge over in the dark of a coffee shop for four mornings, and
found that she did not mind. She had come up out of this room five days ago
to find a face. She had one. She could close her eyes in a room with cloth on
the walls a hundred feet below a town and see it — green eyes, her ears under
his hair, the fat gone off his father's jaw and put on his — and it did not
slide, and it never would.

He was walking the old street. She could feel him do it. Every lamp lit. A
runner ahead of him. Two men behind with a woman in scrubs and a man who had
stopped his truck for a girl and a boy who had been waiting for a ride, and
all three of them going to walk the levee again and be afraid of nothing,
because her son had asked, and she had said yes, because it cost her nothing
she wanted.

She had told him the truth. She always did. She had left one thing out,
which was not a lie: that a boy who keeps a thing on so he can say no has
already learned to let it — the small gray *nothing here* that took his girl
out of two men's hands; the seeing in the dark of a crack with no lamp; the
hands on the knees — and that the letting comes on its own, once it has
begun, the way water finds the low place, and does not ask. He would find that
out. He would find it out up top, in a warded apartment with a piece of iron
under his bed, in a town fifty miles north where a thing was rumored, in a
city at the mouth of the river where her mother's mothers had come ashore,
and every time he found a little more of it out, he would know where she was
without turning his head, and he would say no, and she would hear him.

That was a bargain. She had made worse.

She reached across the low table and pinched the candle out.

In the dark, with the ache in her and the cloth breathing, Leila Chattox sat
a while longer on her own floor, and thought about a gray coat she had bought
up top at a Goodwill on MacArthur Drive for eleven dollars in the first week
she had felt him, and where she had left it, and what time it got light.

"Up top, then," she said, to nobody, and got up.
