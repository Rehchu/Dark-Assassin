# The Coffee Shop (part 1)

The place was fuller than it looked from the street. Every table along the
windows was taken, coats on the backs of chairs, a laptop open at one, two women
with their heads together at another. The line at the counter was three deep.
The bearded manager had come out from behind the register to bus a table
himself, which he only did when he was short, and he did it fast and badly,
stacking cups in the crook of his arm and looking at the door between every
stack.

Addie and Felix sit at the next available table.

It was the one in the corner by the front window, wedged between the glass and
the wall, with a pendant lamp over it that threw a small hard circle of light on
the tabletop and nothing much beyond. Felix took the chair with its back to the
room. He always did. Addie took the one facing the door, because she always did,
and set her helmet on the floor by her boot and her drink on the table and
looked at him.

He set the folder down in front of him. He put both hands flat on top of it.

He did not open it.

She is still curious as to what is going on with. With him, with the folder,
with the Knights, with all of it. She could feel the questions lined up behind
her teeth like people at the counter, and she made herself wait, because he had
asked her to come and he had not asked her to start.

"Why hasn't he said anything about the adoption?" "Why is he waiting?" Addie
thought to herself. He had said the word on the phone. He had read her the
header in big letters. And now he was sitting on top of it like a hen and
talking about nothing, and she knew him well enough to know that this was
Felix working up to a thing, and she knew him well enough to know it could take
a while.

With her mind puzzled, she kept running through every scenario in her head as
to why his parents never told him.

The Knights were not people who kept things. That was the trouble with every
scenario she ran. She had eaten at their table a hundred times. She had seen
Diane Knight label the inside of a cabinet door. She had watched Robert Knight
walk back into a restaurant because the tip had come out to an odd number and
it bothered him. They were the kindest and most orderly people she had ever met,
and she could not make a version of them that put a boy's whole life in a box in
a closet and never said a word about it — and yet here was the box, or the
folder that had come out of the box, under Felix's hands.

Unless they had been told to. Unless someone had asked them to, and they were
the kind of people who did what they were asked. That one she did not like, so
she put it at the back of the line.

"How's the drink? Did they make it right?" Asked Felix.

Addie shook her head up and down, and gave the biggest, goofiest grin to show
her appreciation. "It's absolutely fantastic." Addie says.

It was, too. She held it up to him like a toast, and he almost smiled, and she
saw how much it cost him to almost do that, and she put the drink down.

A tall, black haired, dark complexioned waitress with a flour coated apron came
up to the table.

She had come from the back. Addie saw her come through the swinging door beside
the counter with the apron already on and her hair down loose over her shoulders
and over her ears, a woman late for a shift who was not hurrying, and the
manager had said something to her that Addie could not hear and had gone back
to looking relieved instead of looking at the door. The name tag on the apron
said LEE in the coffee shop's curly letters, hand-printed, new.

She was tall. That was the first thing. Taller than the manager, taller than
most of the men in the room, and she carried it the way tall women do when they
stopped minding it a long time ago. Black hair so black it did not shine under
the string lights the way hair should. Skin the color of the coffee in Addie's
cup before the milk went in. Flour on the apron in a handprint, as if she had
wiped her palms on it once, deliberately, to make it look used.

"Is there anything I can get for you guys today?" the waitress continued to ask
"Do you need any refills or pastries?"

Her voice was low, and slow, and warm in a way that made you want to say yes to
it. It had this town in it, the long vowels, the *y'all* that she did not say
but that lived in the shape of the words anyway.

Felix politely declined the offer.

He said "No, ma'am, we're good, thank you," in the voice he used on other
people's mothers, and he did not look up from the folder to say it, and the
waitress looked at him. Then at the top of his head. Then, for just a second,
at Addie, and her eyes stayed there.

Addie's stomach started to feel like it was being twisted into knots.

It was the sidewalk again. The cold hand under the ribs, the wet mouth, the
horizon going wrong. It came up out of nowhere with a woman standing three feet
away asking about pastries, and Addie had to put her hand flat on the table and
her drink went cold and sweet and wrong in her stomach and she thought, clearly,
*not here.*

And under the sick, something else. The height. The way she stood.

"Do I know you?" She asks "are you just coming on your shift?"

She heard herself say it and did not know why she had. It was not a thing she
said to waitresses. But the bell was ringing again, far back, the same bell as
the sidewalk, and she could not find the rope of it, and it came out of her
mouth before she could decide not to let it.

The waitress promptly replies "I just started today. I'm still learning the
ropes, and just want to make sure everyone is satisfied with my performance."

She said it with a little smile, easy, the new girl making a joke about being
the new girl. Her eyes did not move off Addie while she said it. Brown eyes.
Ordinary. And then she did move them, over to Felix, and Addie watched
something odd happen that she would not have a word for until much later: the
eyes went to him and did not stay. They went to the folder, and the cup, and
the window behind him, and the top of his head, and back to Addie, the way your
eyes go to the one thing in a room that is in focus.

"Y'all holler if you need me," the waitress said, and she went.

As the waitress walks away to another table, Felix pulls out a folder full of
paperwork.

He had had it under his hands the whole time. Now he opened it, and turned it
around on the table so it faced her, and slid it into the circle of the lamp,
and his hands came off it and went into his lap, and she understood that this
was the thing he had walked two miles in the cold to do.

"This is why I wanted you to come. Look at all of this." Felix said with a
worried tone. "There are more than just adoption papers in here."

The papers were on top. She saw the header before she saw anything else —
ALEXANDRIA ORPHANAGE, in bold, the way he had read it to her, and under it a
form, typed, with his name typed into it, *Felix Robert Knight,* and a date
that made him four years old and a signature at the bottom in blue ink that
had gone the color of a bruise. She did not read the rest. She could not, right
then. Her eyes went off it the way you take your hand off a hot pan.

Under the papers, envelopes.

"I found some letters. They appear to be written in a foreign language, but I
don't recognize it."

Four of them. Old, the paper gone soft and brown at the corners, thin — all but
one, which was thicker and had something in it that made a shape through the
paper. Each one had writing on the front, a line of it, and it was not any
alphabet Addie had ever seen. Not Spanish. Not the French on the signs in the
old part of town. Not anything. It looked like handwriting that had been taught
to hide.

Addie reaches for one of the envelopes in the folder.

The thick one. Her hand went to it on its own.

It's sealed with a red wax ring.

A circle of it, dark red, gone brittle, pressed with a mark she could not read
any better than the writing. She turned the envelope over. She turned it back.

"What's this?" She asks Felix as she inspects it further. "It looks like
there's something inside."

Felix snatches the envelope out of Addie's hands, and opens it with caution.

He did it fast, and then he did the rest of it slow. He worked a thumbnail under
the edge of the wax, and the wax cracked, and he peeled the flap back with the
care of someone opening a thing that might not be his to open. He reaches
inside, and pulls out a necklace.

"This is peculiar, I've never seen anything like this before."

It came out in a loop over his fingers. Wooden beads, brown, worn, each one a
little different from the last, on a cord that had gone dark with handling. And
in the middle of them a bead that was not wood. It looked like a black rock with
some sort of symbol on it. Glassy. Black all the way through, the way a thing is
black when it has never had any color to lose. About the size of the end of her
thumb, and hung on the cord so it would sit in the hollow of a throat.

The lamp found it and could not get in.

"Is there anything else in there?" Addie asks Felix as she continues going
through the different notes.

She had the other three envelopes fanned in her hand. Same wax. Same mark. Same
writing that was not writing on the front of each one. She held one up to the
lamp and the light came through the paper and showed her a page inside, folded,
covered edge to edge in the same hidden hand, and she put it down. She had the
strong and stupid feeling that she was being watched read it.

"It looks like there is a note with it."

He drew it out of the envelope. A half-sheet of plain paper, folded once, not
old the way the envelopes were old. Blue ballpoint. Block capitals, pressed hard
enough that she could see them from across the table before she could read
them.

"It just says:

do not take this off under any circumstance. Keep it on you at all times."

He read it flat, the way you read a receipt. Then he read it again to himself
with his lips not moving. Then he looked at her.

"That's all," he said. "That's the whole note. No name. Nothing."

"Whose writing?"

"Not my dad's." He turned the note so she could see it. "I know my dad's. I've
seen it on every check he ever wrote me. That's not it."

Giving Addie strange looks, he decides to put on the necklace.

He put the loop over his head. It caught on his hair and he pulled the hair out
from under the cord, all of it, so it lay on the outside, and the beads settled
against the neck of his hoodie and the black stone dropped into the hollow of
his throat like it had been measured for it.

"How does it look?" He asks jokingly.

"Like you're about to sell me a timeshare."

That got the smile. The real one, half of it, for a second. She wanted to reach
across and hold it there.

The waitress comes back to the table, and checks up on everyone, and notices
Felix's necklace.

She came from the side, out of the room, so that neither of them saw her until
she was there. A carafe of water in one hand that nobody had asked for. She
topped off a glass that did not need it. And her eyes went to the black stone at
his throat and stayed there, and stayed, and Addie counted — one, two, three —
and it was too long, it was the kind of look you gave a thing in a shop window
with a price on it, and then the look went up. To his face. To the sides of his
face, under the hair, where his ears were.

"What a lovely stone, wherever did you get it?" the waitress asked.

"I was just going through some old things, and there it was."

Felix said it to the table. His neck had gone red under the hair. Addie saw the
red come up the side of his throat and over his jaw, and she knew what it was —
she had seen it happen every time a stranger's eyes went to his ears, every
time, for four years, and she had never once said a word about it and neither
had he.

The waitress smiled with her mouth and said "Well, it suits you," and her hands,
setting the carafe down on the table, were older than the rest of her. Addie
noticed it and did not know she had. The knuckles. A vein standing up on the
back of one like a root.

Then she was gone again, to the laptop table, the water going into somebody
else's glass, and Addie's stomach let go a little, and she breathed.

"Take it off," she said. "Let me see it."

"The note said —"

"The note said keep it on you. I'm not going to run off with it." She held her
hand out across the table, palm up. "I want to see the mark on it. Under the
light. Come on. Two seconds."

He looked at the counter. The waitress was not at the counter. He looked at the
door. Then he lifted the loop back over his head, hair and all, and put it in
her hand, warm from his throat, heavier than it looked.

She held the stone under the pendant lamp and turned it.

Up close it was not a rock. It was glass, or something like glass — black, and
polished, and full of tiny lines under the surface like frozen smoke. And cut
into the face of it, so fine she had to tilt it to catch it, a mark. A line,
with two short lines coming off the top of it at an angle. A stem with two
tines.

"It's a fork," she said.

"It's not a fork."

"It's a little fork. Look."

"Why would somebody carve a fork on a rock and put it in an envelope with a
note that says never take it off?"

She did not have an answer for that. She turned it again. The lamp slid off the
black surface and could not find a way in, and she thought, without deciding
to, *it's like looking at the door at home tonight. The black glass with nothing
behind it.*

The two were finishing up their drinks, and Addie remembered she needed to run
to the store for her mom.

It came up out of nowhere, the way it does — sage, butter, honey, *the bunches,
get two, not the bear* — and she looked at the clock over the counter and it
was ten past seven and the store closed at eight and she had the necklace in her
right hand and her helmet by her boot and her mother's *text me* still on her
phone.

"Shoot," she said. She stood up. "I have to go. The store. She'll kill me."

"Now?"

"It closes at eight. I'll come back. Or I'll — I'll text you. Don't go anywhere
without me. Don't read those." She jerked her chin at the envelopes. "Not
without me."

"I can't read them."

"Then don't try."

Her hand had closed on the necklace when she stood, the way your hand closes on
whatever is in it when you get up fast, and it was still closed. She had the
helmet in the other. She shoved her right hand into her left jacket pocket, the
one with the zipper, the one with the twenty and the ten in it, and the hand
came out empty and she did not notice, because she was already looking at the
door and at the clock and at Felix, who was gathering the envelopes back into
the folder with his head down and did not look up.

"I'll text you," she said again.

"Go," he said. "Go, it's fine. Get your mom her — whatever it is."

She went.

Past the laptop table. Past the two women with their heads together. Past the
counter, where the girl with the ponytail was wiping the steam wand and the
bearded manager was ringing somebody up, and where the waitress with the black
hair was standing at the far end by the pastry case with her hands at her sides
and her carafe on the counter, doing nothing.

Not wiping. Not pouring. Not watching the door, the way everybody else in a
coffee shop watches the door.

She was standing very still, and she was looking at the table in the corner by
the window, where a short heavy boy with his hair over his ears was closing a
folder, and she was looking at it the way you look at a place where something
was a second ago and is not now.

Addie saw it. She did not know what she had seen. She pushed the door open with
her shoulder and the bell rang and the cold took her, and the sickness she had
been carrying since the woman first came to the table went out of her all at
once on the sidewalk, like a hand letting go, and she put her helmet on and got
on the bike and did not think about it again for almost an hour.
