# Prologue

It was raining the night the man carried the boy down to the river, the kind of
rain the low country saves up all summer and lets go of all at once, warm and
loud and total, coming down so hard the streetlights wore halos and the gutters
had turned to little brown rivers of their own, racing curbside toward the drains
with a sound like something being poured out that would never be filled back up.

The man kept to the alleys. He had a coat and the coat had a hood and under the
coat, held against his chest with one arm the way you hold a thing you have
decided to die for, was the boy. The boy was four. He was not crying, which was
the strange part, the part the man kept coming back to even now with the water
running into his eyes — a four-year-old carried out of his bed in the middle of
the night into a storm like the end of the world, and he was not crying. He only
watched. He had his mother's eyes and he watched the way she watched, steady and
dark and taking everything in and giving nothing back, and it broke something in
the man to see it, because it was the one thing of hers he could not cut out of
the child no matter how far he ran.

"Almost there," the man said, to the boy or to himself. The rain ate it.

He did not run. Running was seen. He had learned that from her, the way he had
learned everything that was going to save the boy's life tonight, all of it hers,
all of it turned now against her — you did not run, you did not hurry, you moved
through a city like a thing the city had always contained, and the city looked
right past you. So he walked. He walked with his heart going like a fist on a
door and the boy's small heat soaking through his shirt and the rain coming down
in ropes, and he made himself walk, because somewhere behind him or below him or
inside the very stone of the streets was a woman who could feel a heartbeat like
his across a mile of noise if he let it get loud enough, and the only thing in
the world that was keeping the boy's heartbeat quiet was the thing around the
boy's neck.

It was not much to look at. That was the point of it. Wooden beads, hand-carved,
strung on a waxed cord, and one bead among them of black obsidian with a mark cut
into it that looked, if you looked at all, like a little two-tined fork. An old
man in a pawn shop on Main Street had made it, years and years ago, for a woman
who came to him near closing and did not want a ring fixed or a watch sized, who
wanted only to be gone from the world's sight — and he had made her a thing that
would do it, the old kind of protection, his grandmother's kind, and she had worn
it and no one had found her, not once, not ever, until she had wanted them to.

The man had taken it off her while she slept. It was the bravest thing he had
ever done and it had taken him eleven days to work up to, eleven days of lying
beside a woman whose eyes went red in the dark and pretending his hands were
steady, and when he finally did it — one bead at a time, the cord lifted a
hair's width off her skin, his breath held so long the room swam — he had been
so sure she would wake that he had a story ready and a knife he knew was useless.
She had not woken. That was the mercy and the horror of it both. She trusted him
that much. She had let the one man in the world down her guard, and he had used
it to steal the charm off her throat and put it on their son and take the son
away into the rain, and he would carry the shame of how he did it for the rest
of whatever life this bought him, and he would do it again a thousand times.
Because the alternative was that she would raise the boy. And the man had seen
what her mother's raising had made of her, and he would drown the child in the
river with his own two hands before he let that happen to him.

He would not have to. There were people waiting. There was a house he would not
let himself picture yet, and a name the boy would take that was not the boy's
name, and a pair of good ordinary strangers who did not know the half of what
they were being handed and had said yes anyway, because that was the kind of
people the protectors found when they needed them — plain, and kind, and brave
in the small unremarkable way that the whole world runs on and never thanks. The
boy would grow up in a house that alphabetized its spices. He would never know
what he was. That was the plan, and the plan was good, and the man had given
fourteen years of the life he might have had to make it real, and he would give
fourteen more.

He had one thing left to do tonight, and then the boy was gone from him too.

He came out of the alley at the low place where the street dipped toward the
storm drain, and the water there was over the curb, a wide black sheet of it
sliding fast toward the grate, carrying leaves and a lost shoe and the whole
night's ruin down into the dark under the city. And it was there, at the worst
possible moment, in the worst possible place, that the cord broke.

He never knew why. Rot in the wax, maybe. A snag on his own coat. It did not
matter. One second the necklace was at the boy's throat and the next it was
not — it slid, all at once, off the small wet neck and out of the collar of the
coat and it was gone, into the running water, into the black sheet racing for
the grate, and the man made a sound he had never made before in his life and
went down onto his knees in the flood with the boy still crushed to his chest
and his free hand plunged wrist-deep into the cold rushing dark.

For one endless instant there was nothing. Water and grit and the edge of the
iron grate under his fingers and the certain knowledge, colder than the flood,
that it had gone through, that it was down in the sewers now and rolling toward
the river and the boy's light was already blazing up naked into the storm like a
struck match in a dark house, and that somewhere below the streets a woman had
just gone very still.

Then his fingers closed on it.

It had caught. One bead had hooked the lip of the grate, one bead of a dozen, the
whole small weight of the thing hanging by a single carved knuckle of wood over
the throat of the drain, and he drew it up out of the water so fast he skinned
his knuckles bloody on the iron and he did not feel it, would not feel it for an
hour, and he knelt there in the middle of the flooded street with the rain
pounding his hood and his son against his heart and the necklace streaming in his
fist and he wept, once, hard, the way a man weeps who has looked over an edge and
been let step back.

Then he got the cord knotted with shaking hands. Then he lifted the wet beads
over the boy's dark hair and settled them at his throat and tucked them down
inside the collar where they would stay, and he put his forehead against the
boy's forehead in the rain and he said the only thing he had to give him, the
thing he had said to the woman once and would never say to her again:

"Don't take it off. Not to shower. Not to sleep. Not ever. You keep it on."

The boy looked at him with his mother's eyes.

"You keep it on," the man said again, and his voice cracked all the way through,
"and I will find a way to be a man you'd have been proud of. I promise you that.
I don't get to keep you. I get to keep you *safe.* It's the worst trade in the
world and I'd make it a hundred times."

The boy did not understand a word of it. He was four. But something in the man's
face must have reached him anyway, some weight coming off it, because the boy
lifted one small hand out of the coat and laid it flat against the man's wet
cheek, patient, the way you comfort a thing that is upset for reasons you cannot
see, and the man broke entirely, and held him, and let the rain hide it.

Then he stood up, and he walked the rest of the way to the house without
hurrying, and he handed the boy through a lit doorway into a pair of ordinary
arms, and he did not look back, because looking back was how you were followed,
and he had one more thing than his own grief left to protect.

---

Far below the drowning streets, in a room that no map had ever held, a woman sat
bolt upright in the dark.

She had felt it. For one breath — one single breath, in the whole endless flat
gray of fourteen years to come — she had felt her light flare up somewhere over
her head, near, so near, a warmth in the cold miles of the parish like a hand
laid suddenly on the back of her neck. She was on her feet before she knew she
had moved, the shadow boiling up off the floor around her, her eyes gone the red
of a coal at the center of a dying fire, her whole starved being turned upward
toward that one impossible breath of warmth —

— and it was gone.

Snuffed, between one heartbeat and the next, the way a candle goes out when a
door you did not know was open swings shut. She stood in the dark with the rain
a far dim roar through a hundred feet of stone and she reached up after it with
everything she was and she found nothing. Cold. Noise. The huge indifferent
weight of the sleeping city. Nothing.

She did not scream. She had never in her life made a sound she did not choose to
make. She stood there a long time while the shadow settled and her eyes cooled
and the ache took up its place in her chest, the ache that would not leave again
for fourteen years, and at last she understood, in the wordless certain way she
understood the moment before a kill, exactly what had happened.

Someone had put out her light and hidden it in a bushel.

Someone clever. Someone close. Someone who had loved it as much as she did.

She sat back down at the low table in the dark, and she folded her ruined-looking
hands, and she began, with the patience of a bloodline older than the city over
her head, to wait.

Above her, the rain came down and down, and washed the blood from an iron grate,
and filled the gutters, and ran on toward the river, carrying the whole night's
ruin away into the dark — and did not carry away the one small thing it had been
sent for, which was even then going quiet and warm and hidden down a lit street
she would spend the rest of her life unable to find.
