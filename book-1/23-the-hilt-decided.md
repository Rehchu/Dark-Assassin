# The Hilt Decided

His father could not hold it.

That was the first thing, at seven in the morning by Janet's watch, with the
lamp burned down to its last inch and the crypt full of people who had not
slept. Anatole lifted the Hilt off its cloth on the altar stone — carefully,
with his left hand, the right one wrapped in Odile's strips and held against
his chest — and carried it the four steps to the cot and laid it in Darneil's
right hand, the way you would give a man a thing he had asked for, and the
hand lay on the blanket with the black iron across the palm and did not
close.

His father looked at it.

Felix watched him try. He watched it go down the arm from the shoulder, the
wanting, and stop at the elbow the way water stops at a wall, and the fingers
lay open on the pewter-colored wire and did not move, and his father's face
did a thing on the side that could still do things, and then he reached
across himself with the left hand and picked the Hilt up out of the right
one, and held it in the left, and looked at it there.

"No," he said. Thick. "Not this one."

"Darneil — " Janet said.

"It's not — " The tongue fought. He waited it out. "It's not a hand for
holding. It's a hand for the lamp. My father held it left-handed the last
year because the right was gone with the damp, and it *quieted,* Jan, it
quieted the room fine, and it didn't bind. He told me. You have to hold it in
the hand you'd hold a sword in or it doesn't take." He looked at the thing in
his left fist. "I've held it fourteen years in the right. I felt it go still.
It's not still now. It's just heavy."

Nobody said anything. The lamp hissed.

"There's one hand of the line in this room," his father said, "that isn't on
a cot."

---

He had known. He had known since the river way, since the cold came into his
hands and did not leave; since the low table, since a woman with her eyes
closed said *the third turning* in a voice she used for waitressing; since
the crack, coming up it in the dark, able to see. He had known it the way you
know the last step of a stair you have gone up a hundred times. And still,
when his father held the Hilt out to him across the blanket, in the left
hand, with the right lying dead beside it, he did not take it, and stood
there, and his father let him.

"It'll go still in you," his father said. "Same as me. It'll go still and it
won't wake while you've got it. That's what it's for. It doesn't make you
anything. It doesn't fix your hands. It keeps her a stone's throw off
wherever it is, and it keeps *that* — " a look, at Felix's chest, at the
place under the hoodie where the cord was — "asleep in you. That's all. It's
a piece of a sword. Some Frenchman carried it up the river three hundred
years ago and lost it in a flood, and my father's father's people found it in
the mud and knew what it was, and they've kept it in this hole ever since for
a boy who'd need it, and you're the boy."

"I'm not a protector."

"Nobody's *born* one." His father almost laughed; it came out of the good
side of his mouth as a breath. "It's an oath, not blood. That's the whole
point of it. Your grandfather swore it to a man named Lemoine who swore it to
a woman whose name's on that wall under the damp, and I swore it to my
father the year before you were born, and it's three sentences, and you say
them holding it, and that's all there is. Anybody could. Nobody does. You're
going to."

Felix looked at the Hilt.

Grip and crossguard. Iron gone black, wrapped in wire worn the color of
pewter, the stub of a tang broken square. No blade. It had sat on a stone in
this room for longer than the town above it had had electric light, and it
had kept his mother off a man for fourteen years, and it was a piece of iron,
and he had seen it lie on its cloth while six men came over a rail, and do
nothing.

"What if I can't," he said.

"Then you can't." His father held it out. "Find out."

He took it.

---

It was heavier than it should have been. Everyone had said that — Anatole,
his father, the book gone soft on the shelf that Darneil had read him a page
of on Monday — and it was true, and it was not the thing. The thing was that
it *fit.* His hand, a short heavy boy's hand, a hand that had held a laptop
and a coffee cup and a spare helmet and a wax envelope, closed on the grip
and found that the grip had been made for it — not for a hand like it; for
*it,* for the width of the palm and the length of the fingers and the place
the thumb wanted to lie — and he stood in the crypt with the Hilt of Tyr in
his right fist and felt something under the floor of himself lift its head,
and look at the iron, and lie back down.

That was all. It was everything.

He had not known it was awake. He had thought — he had thought it was gone
down into him at the rail last night the way the red went down, that it had
gone quiet on its own when he came over the iron with his father's weight on
his arm. It had not. It had been up, and awake, under everything, the whole
night, the way it had been in the crack coming up in the dark, the way it had
been in the room with cloth on the walls when he put his hands on his knees;
and now it was not. It lay down in him like a dog in a doorway, and the room
was quiet, and his hands were still cold.

"Say it," his father said.

"I don't know it."

"*I keep the light.*"

"I keep the light."

"*I keep it from her.*"

He looked at his father. His father looked back at him from the pillow with
half a face and the ring gone off his throat and fourteen years of charcoal
on the wall behind him, and did not say *her name,* and did not have to.

"I keep it from her," Felix said.

"*I keep it until it's whole.*"

"I keep it until it's whole."

The Hilt did not do anything. He had known it would not. It lay in his fist
the way it had lain, heavy and black and cold as his hand, and the lamp
hissed, and Anatole's people stood against the brick and looked at the floor,
and Addie, on the crate, with her bad hand in her lap, looked at *him* and
did not look away, and something in the room was decided that had been
undecided since a night of rain, and that was all, and he lowered his arm.

"That's it?" he said.

"That's it."

"Nothing happened."

"Nothing's supposed to happen." His father closed his eyes. "It's yours now.
God help you. Put it in the box."

---

They went up at nine.

It took that long. It took an hour to get his father off the cot and onto the
door Anatole's people had brought from the hall — one of the doors off the
long table, unhinged, with a blanket on it and rope at the corners — and to
get the rope right, and to get six people who had been afraid of Darneil
Delvaux for fourteen years to put their hands on him. They did it. Felix
watched them do it. Odile first, at the head, because she had crossed the rail
a thousand times with a plate and was not afraid of him; then a Dauzat girl at
the foot; then, one at a time, four men from the hall who had stood behind
Armand on Tuesday morning and behind Anatole on Tuesday night and did not
look at each other, and took the rope, and lifted.

"Four hundred yards," Anatole said. "Then the stair. Ninety-four."

"I know how many," Darneil said, from the door.

"I know you know."

The Hilt was in Felix's hoodie pocket. He could feel it against his stomach
through the cloth, cold, heavy, decided, and every step he took the quiet
went with him and the six carrying his father walked inside it, and he
understood, at the outfall bend, that this was what it would be now. Not a
room. Him. Wherever he stood, a stone's throw of a thing she would not come
into, and it would go with him up a stair and into a truck and down a river
road and into a second-floor apartment with no curtains, and she would stand
at the edge of it in daylight, and not come in, and not go away.

Behind the door, Janet walked with her hand on her side and her eye shut.
Behind Janet, Alcide, with the crowbar and the dead flashlight and a face like
a man walking out of his own funeral. Behind Alcide, Ti-Jean with the lamp.
And beside Felix, not touching him, Addie, with her right hand held against
her chest in the front of her jacket as if it belonged to somebody else and
she was carrying it for them.

At the mouth of the river way the old street was lit again. Or still. Every
lamp. And the people stood in their doors the way they had stood at midnight,
and looked, and this time the thing they looked at was a gaunt gray man on a
door, going by at the height of their faces with his eyes open, and not one
of them turned to the wall, and not one of them said anything, and an old
woman with a baby on her hip that was not hers came out of her doorway and
walked beside the door for twenty steps with her hand on Darneil's dead one,
and went back.

"That's a Lemoine," his father said, to the ceiling. "That's Ti-Jean's
grandmother. Her mother held the door for — " He stopped. "Never mind who
for."

At the foot of the bell-stair Anatole stopped them.

He had walked at the front the whole way with his wrapped hand against his
chest and had said nothing since the river way, and he stopped now at the
bottom step with the sage crushed under his boot and turned around, and Felix
saw that he had been counting.

"Fourteen," Anatole said. "In the hall, that wouldn't look at me this
morning. Fourteen who stood behind him Tuesday and want to be somewhere else
now and aren't. And twenty-six who will." He said it to Darneil, not to
anyone else. "Forty. Same as before. Two of his under the brick at the
outfall that nobody's going to talk about up top. That's my town. That's
what I've got, Delvaux. A door with fewer keepers and a table with a leg off
it and a school."

"It's more than she left the last one," Darneil said.

"It is."

"Keep it."

"I'm going to." Anatole looked up the stair. "I'll hold the door. Not like
before. Like now. If Jan comes, it opens. If the boy comes, it opens. If
anyone else comes down it that I didn't send for — " He did not finish.
"Nobody's getting *presented* anymore. That's done. I said it to her face
and she didn't argue. That's the one thing I got out of this."

"You got your people," Odile said, from the head of the door.

"I got my people."

"Then shut up and lift."

They went up.

---

Ninety-four steps.

The town carried his father up them. That was the sentence, and he would say
it later exactly that way, to Diane, at a table, and she would not understand
it and would cry anyway. Six people who had lived their whole lives a
hundred feet under a city and had been told since they could walk that the
man in the hold was a thing you did not look at carried him up a bell tower
that went the wrong way, brick and then stone, on a door with a blanket on
it, and at the fortieth step a Dauzat girl's hands gave out and Alcide
Fontenot put down the crowbar and took the rope from her without a word and
carried, seventy-one years old, on a heart the parish doctor had told him
about, all the way to the top.

Felix went last. With Addie.

She had not asked. He had known she would not ask; he had known it on the
floor of the crypt at one in the morning when he came back over the rail with
the necklace on and three stretchers behind him and she had stood up and hit
him, once, in the chest, with her good hand, not hard, and then put her face
against him and not asked. She did not ask now. She went up the stair beside
him with her hand held in her jacket and the lamps going away above them and
the sage smell fading, and somewhere around the sixtieth step, in the stone
part, where the shaft was narrow and there was nobody near enough to hear, he
told her anyway.

"She had a name for me."

Addie did not stop climbing.

"In the box. The brass one. With — there's a piece of hair. Mine. She said
she wrote it the day I was born and put it in with the hair." Step. Step.
"Cyprien."

"Cyprien," Addie said.

"It's a sorcerer's name. She said. Before it was a saint's." He heard how it
sounded. "I don't know what that means. I didn't ask. She said my father gave
me *Felix* on the doorstep because he thought I'd need the luck."

"You did."

"Yeah."

They climbed.

"Is that what you are," she said. Quietly. Not looking at him; looking at the
step in front of her. "Cyprien."

"No."

"Is it what she thinks you are."

"No. I don't — " He put his cold hand on the stone. "She said it once and then
she said *all right* when I told her not to. I think she'd already decided
about it before I got there. I think she'd decided about all of it. She told
me what was going to happen and then it happened. She told me I'd say no and
I said it." Step. "Addie, she *let me.* That's what I can't — she sat on the
floor and she never once touched me and she let me say no and she meant it.
And I keep thinking that's the worst thing she could've done and I keep not
knowing why."

"Because you wanted her to try," Addie said.

He stopped on the stair.

"You wanted her to grab you. Or lie. Or do the thing with the eyes. So you
could hate her." She had stopped too, a step above him, and turned, with the
lamps small above her and her copper hair come all the way loose and her face
white and set. "And she didn't, and you can't, and now you've got a mother
who's a — who's what she is — and you don't get to hate her, and you've got
to just *know* it, for the rest of your life, with her across the street.
That's the worst thing. That's what she did to you. She let you see her."

He did not say anything.

"I'm a psychology student," Addie said. "I've had one semester. Get up the
stairs."

He got up the stairs.

---

The concrete steps. The cellar. The steel door with MAINTENANCE on it at an
angle. The stairwell of Building C, ordinary, with the mailboxes and the
notice about the pool closing for the season, and the door at the end of it
with the push bar, and beyond the push bar, light.

---

He had not seen it in fourteen years.

He had thought about it. He had thought about it in the way a man thinks about
a thing he has decided not to think about, at the edge of every day, at the
hour when the charcoal on the wall said it would be coming up over the levee
and the lamp said nothing at all. He had thought he knew what it would be. A
door. A parking lot. Cars. He had lived twenty-eight years in it before the
rain and there was nothing about daylight a man forgets.

The door opened and Odile went through it backward with the head of the
stretcher and the light came in over her shoulder and put itself on his face,
and Darneil Delvaux found out that there was.

It was *white.* That was the whole of it. Not the yellow of a lamp, not the
gray of the cloth, not the brown of the candle in her room that he had lived
in for a year — white, and cold, and everywhere, coming down out of a sky he
could not look at and off the tops of three brick buildings and off the
windshields of a lot full of cars and off the frost on the landscaped strip
and off a woman's copper hair coming through the door behind him, and it did
not stop at the edge of anything, it went *on,* and he lay on a door from a
table in a buried hall with a blanket on him and half of him gone and looked
straight up into it, and it looked back, and he wept.

Once. Hard. The way he had not on the night of the rain, or at the lit
doorway, or any night after with the charcoal in his hand. It came up out of
the good side of him and the dead side too, all at once, without asking, and
the six carrying him stopped in the middle of the Riverbend parking lot on a
cold bright Wednesday morning with the frost still on the grass, and did not
put him down, and did not say anything, and waited.

Then it was done. He wiped his face with his left hand.

"All right," he said.

They carried him on.

Fontenot's truck was at the curb with the engine running and the tailgate
down. An old red truck, the same one, God help them all, that the man had
driven twenty years ago with a case of trinkets in the bed. Fontenot had laid
a mattress in the back of it and a quilt on the mattress and a bundle of sage
under the quilt at each corner, and Darneil smelled it as they lifted him,
and looked at the old man, and the old man looked at the tailgate.

"For the ride," Fontenot said.

"It doesn't work in a truck."

"It doesn't hurt."

"No." He let his head go back on the mattress. "No, it doesn't."

The boy climbed in beside him. Jan's girl climbed in on the other side with
her hand in her jacket. Jan got in the cab. Anatole's six stood on the curb
in the white light with their hands at their sides and their eyes screwed up
against it, six people who had never in their lives been in a parking lot at
nine in the morning, and looked at him, and he looked at them, and Odile
lifted her hand, and the tailgate went up.

"Back," Anatole said, behind them. "All of you. Before somebody in a window —
back. *Go.*"

They went. Through a door under a stairwell. He heard it shut.

The truck moved.

---

The river road.

He knew it. He knew every yard of it, from underneath: the low place where it
dipped and the drain ran under, the culvert, the levee wall with its stains.
From on top, at nine in the morning in November, lying on a mattress in the
bed of a pawnbroker's truck with the sky going by overhead, it was a road.
Two lanes. A gull. A man on a bicycle. A sign for a boat launch. It was the
most ordinary thing he had ever seen and he could not stop looking at it, and
beside him the boy sat with his back against the cab and the Hilt in the
front of his hoodie, and the quiet of it went out over the tailgate and along
the road behind them, and somewhere in it, a stone's throw off, keeping pace,
in the daylight where she was nearly nothing, his wife walked.

He did not see her. He felt the boy feel her.

"She's there," the boy said.

"Yes."

"She's not doing anything."

"No." He looked at the sky. "She's not going to, today. She's walking her
son home."

The truck slowed at the bench.

It was Jan's idea. Everything up top was Jan's idea; she had sat on the crypt
floor at two in the morning with her eye shut and made a list on her wrist in
pen, and this was the third thing on it. A bench by the river road, at the
turnout by the boat launch, where the parish had put a trash can and a sign
about the bald cypress. Fontenot stopped the truck on the shoulder. Jan's
girl got out, and Fontenot, and the boy, and they went to the back of the
truck where the three lay on the mattress beside him under the other quilt —
a woman in scrubs, a man in a work shirt, a boy in a letter jacket, with their
eyes open — and lifted them out one at a time, and carried them to the bench,
and set them on it in a row, sitting, leaning on each other, in the sun, like
three people who had stopped to rest.

The woman in scrubs was looking at the river.

"Miss Broussard," the boy said. He had crouched down in front of her. "You're
on the river road. By the boat launch. Somebody's going to come by in about
five minutes and find you, and they're going to call it in, and my — Miss
Walker's going to take the call. You're going to be fine. It wears off. It's
already wearing off. You're going to walk the levee again. I promised
somebody."

The woman's eyes moved. Down, to his face. She could not do anything else.

"I'm sorry," the boy said. "For the part that was me."

He stood up. He put his cold hand, for a second, on the top of the boy's head
in the letter jacket, the one who had been waiting for a ride, and then they
got back in the truck, and Fontenot pulled onto the road, and Darneil watched
the bench go away behind them with three people on it in the sun until the
road bent, and thought about a man named Terry Guidry with a wedding ring on
his hand, at the flat line, whom nobody was going to leave on a bench.

"Four," he said, to the sky.

"What?"

"She had four. She gave back three." He closed his eyes. "That's the count.
Tell Jan. For the wall."

---

Rapides Regional took him in at ten-fifteen.

He heard it done. He heard Jan do it, at the emergency entrance, through the
open window of the cab, in the dispatcher's voice that he had heard over a
phone once, fourteen years ago, from the back room of a pawn shop, saying *I
have a couple. They're good. They'll do what they're told:* a man found by
the river road, by the boat launch, wandering, right side gone, no ID, no
wallet, confused; she was a parish dispatcher and she'd been driving by and
she knew a stroke when she saw one. No, she didn't know him. No, no next of
kin that she — 

"Delvaux," Darneil said.

The orderly with the gurney looked down at him.

"Darneil Delvaux." He said it as clearly as the tongue would let him.
"D-E-L-V-A-U-X. Born here. Forty-six. I've got — " The thing in his chest
went over. "I've got a son. He's right there. That's him."

He had not said it in fourteen years. Not to Anatole, not to the wall, not to
the boy's face on Monday when he was told to say anything and said the wrong
things. He said it to an orderly at a hospital door in the white light with a
name tag that said TREVOR, and the orderly wrote it down, and it went into a
computer, and somewhere in the parish a record came alive that had been dead
since he was thirty, and the boy stood at the tailgate with his cold hands in
his pockets and heard his father say it, and did not say anything, and did
not have to.

They wheeled him in. At the door, in the cold, he heard Fontenot's boots stop
on the concrete, and the old man's voice, once, not loud, not to anyone in
particular:

"Jan."

That was all. He did not hear what she said back. The doors closed.

---

Room 412.

They said *stroke.* They said it in the hallway and they said it in the room
and a doctor with a tablet said it to him slowly as if he were deaf and asked
him to smile and to lift his arms and to say *the sky is blue in Louisiana,*
and he did the first two on the one side and the third with a thick tongue,
and they said *stroke* again and wrote it down. They took blood. They took
pictures of the inside of his head. A nurse asked him if there was anyone she
should call, and he said his son knew, and she asked where he'd been living,
and he said *around,* and she looked at his hands and did not ask again.

The window looked at a parking garage.

He looked at it anyway. Concrete, and cars, and the sky above the top deck
going from white to blue to the deep gold of a November afternoon, and he
could not get enough of it and he did not try to. The right side of him lay in
the bed like a thing that had been left there. The left side held the rail of
the bed. There was a television up in the corner that somebody had turned on
and he had not asked them to turn off, and at six it said the news.

*Three Alexandria residents missing for as long as six weeks were found
alive this morning by a passing motorist on the river road near the Cypress
Bend boat launch. Loretta Broussard, forty-one, Curtis Dupre, thirty-three,
and a seventeen-year-old Pineville High School student were transported to
Rapides Regional in stable condition. All three are described as confused
and unable to account for —*

He looked at the ceiling.

Down the hall, a stone's throw off, three people who had lain on stretchers
on the brick of his river way were lying in beds with their families around
them, and a girl in scrubs was sitting up, they said, and had asked for a
Coke. A stone's throw. She would not come here for them. She had said and she
did not lie. But she was somewhere in the gold light out past the parking
garage with her son's face in her and a car and a man in sunglasses, and he
lay in a bed with his own name on the chart at the foot of it and knew, for
the first time since a night of rain, that she knew exactly where he was, and
did not care, and that he was going to have to live in that.

He found he could.

---

The boy came at seven, when the gold was gone. Alone.

He looked — Darneil made himself look, and see — like a boy who had not slept
in two days and had carried a piece of iron up a bell tower and put his mother
behind him. Short, heavy, the hair pushed back so the ears showed, which it
never had been. A bandage on the left palm. The hoodie was empty in front. He
came in and pulled the chair round and sat in it where the good eye could see
him, and put his hands on his knees.

"It's under the bed," he said.

"Which bed."

"Mine. In the box. The one Dad — the one Robert brought over. With the
papers and the letters on top." He looked at the window. "Janet did the
kitchen. The whole thing. Scrubbed it, like — Addie says she did theirs, on
Thursday. Then she burned sage at the windowsills and the door and she put
something in the corners I didn't ask about, and she said it won't hold her.
She said it'll slow her down. She said a ward's a fence, not a wall, and the
Hilt's the wall, and the two together are the best anybody up top's ever
had, and then she went home. Alcide drove her. He hasn't said one word since
the door."

"He said one."

"What?"

"Never mind." Darneil turned his head on the pillow. "A stone's throw. You
felt it. In the apartment."

"Out to the street. Not across it. The sidewalk on the other side's outside
it." The boy's hands did not move on his knees. "She was there. This
afternoon, when Janet was doing the sills. Across the street. Under the live
oak. She didn't do anything. She just stood there in the daylight where
anybody could've seen her and nobody did, and she looked at my window, and I
looked back at her through the glass with the Hilt in my hand, and after a
while she walked off. Toward Jackson. Like a person."

"She'll be back tomorrow."

"I know."

"And the day after."

"I know." He looked at his father. "Diane's called nine times. Since
Saturday. I finally — I called her back from the truck. I said I'd been out
of town with Addie, that my phone died. I said I'd be there tomorrow. It's
Thanksgiving." He said it as if the word were in a language he was
translating out of. "I forgot. I forgot the whole thing. She's doing the
turkey and Robert's putting the leaf in the table and I'm supposed to bring
rolls."

Darneil looked at the ceiling.

Fourteen years ago he had stood at the lit doorway of those people's house in
the rain with the boy asleep on his shoulder and told them three things and
walked away into the dark before the woman could ask him in, and he had never
once let himself think about what the inside of the house looked like. He
thought about it now. A table. A leaf. A boy bringing rolls.

"Bring the rolls," he said.

"I'm going to."

"And bring the girl."

"She's coming. Janet too. Diane asked." The boy stood up. He stood a moment
with his cold hands at his sides and his ears showing and looked down at the
man on the bed, at the right hand lying open on the blanket, at the chart at
the foot with a name on it that had been dead since before he could remember,
and Darneil saw him decide not to say the thing he was going to say, and say
a smaller one instead.

"You told them your name."

"Yes."

"You said *that's my son.*"

"I said it to an orderly named Trevor." The good side of his mouth moved.
"It's a start."

The boy went to the door. Stopped in it, with the hallway bright behind him
and the ears showing, the way a man had stood in another doorway once, in
rain, and turned around.

"I'll come tomorrow," Felix said. "In the morning. Before the table. There's
a thing I need to ask you, and I want to ask it with the light on."

"Ask it now."

"No." He put his hand up, to his collar, where the stone was and the ring,
and touched them through the cloth, not knowing he did it. "Tomorrow. I want
one night where I've got it and I don't know what's next. I've never had
one. I want to see what it's like."

He went.

Darneil lay in the bed with the parking garage going dark outside the window
and the television saying something about the weather, cold and bright
through the holiday, and did not sleep, and did not need to, and for the
first time in fourteen years there was nothing at all he had to keep, and it
was the strangest quiet he had ever lain in, and it was not the Hilt's, and
he let it.
