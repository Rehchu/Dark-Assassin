# Red Eyes

She had never walked the river way.

Not once, in forty years of a life spent under this town. It was theirs — the
frightened forty's, the truce's, the brick tunnel that ran from their hall to
the one room in the parish she could not enter — and she had kept the second
term the way she had kept all of them, exactly, for as long as they served her,
and they had stopped serving her at about nine o'clock on Monday night when
Armand came down her crack and said the hall was quiet and the watchers were
his and the way was open.

So she walked it.

Armand went ahead with a lamp, and four of his men behind him with lamps and
cudgels, and behind them the two who carried the first stretcher, and the two
who carried the second, and the two who carried the third, and the last two
with the fourth, and she came after all of them with no light at all, the
shadow up around her like a coat, the bow across her back, the belt at her
waist, her hands empty. She did not need a light. The brick went by under her
palm cold and wet and she had walked worse in the dark, and she had time.

The shadow came with her.

It had been up since she left the room and it did not go down. It rose off the
wet floor of the tunnel and pooled at her wrists and lay along her forearms
and came along the brick behind her like water following a channel, filling
the river way from wall to wall as she went, so that the men ahead of her with
their lamps walked in a yellow light and she walked in nothing, and the four on
the stretchers, who could not turn their heads, stared up at a ceiling that had
gone black behind the lamps and did not know why.

Four in six weeks. She had chosen them the way she chose — not the easy ones.
The ones who had already done half her work. A man who checked drains. A woman
who walked home from a night shift by the levee because she was not afraid of
the levee. A boy waiting for a ride. A man in a truck who stopped for a girl
by the road and found she was not a girl. Armand had brought them down her
crack one at a time, and she had put her hand on them, and had not taken the
light — had done the other thing, the new thing, the *holding,* the brew she
had spent a year perfecting for the night she needed a body kept and not
ended. They lay on the stretchers with their eyes open. They breathed. They
saw the black ceiling going by. Somewhere in each of them a person was awake
and could not move a finger, and she had learned a great deal from them, and
she was going to learn one thing more tonight, and then she was going to give
three of them back.

The tunnel bent at the outfall. The lamps went round it. She went round it.

And there, at the end of four hundred yards of dark, a light that was not
theirs.

---

An iron rail.

She had known it was there the way you know a wall is there in a house you
have never once gone into the back of. Waist high, black, across the mouth of
a vaulted room with a lamp in it, and behind the rail a man with a thing in
his hand, standing, and behind the man a stone, and on the stone — nothing she
could see. Nothing she could see was the point of it.

Armand stopped ten feet short. His men stopped. The stretchers went down on
the wet brick with the four faces turned up.

She kept walking.

The shadow came with her. It came along the floor of the river way toward the
rail the way it had come along the whole tunnel, slow, patient, a tide going
in, and she watched it go — watched it lie along the brick, and reach the
lamplight from the crypt, and thin in the light the way it always thinned, and
go on anyway, a gray suggestion on the wet floor, toward the iron —

— and stop.

It stopped at the rail. Not at the man. Not at the stone. At the *iron,* a foot
short of it, along a line as straight as if somebody had drawn it, the shadow
went flat. It lay down on the brick and went still and did not go one inch
further, the way water lies at a sill and will not cross it, the way a dog
lies down at a door and will not go into the room. She felt it stop. She felt
it the way you feel a hand you are holding out in the dark meet a wall.

Fourteen years. Every night of them she had known this rail was here and had
not come. She had told herself it was the truce. She had told the town it was
the truce. She had let the town believe the fourth term was a thing she *gave*
them, and she stood at the flat edge of her own shadow now in the lamplight
with her hands empty and looked at the thing behind the rail that the shadow
would not go near, and knew what she had always known, and let herself know
it.

She let her eyes go red.

It was not a choice, by then. The shadow was up as high as it had ever been
and the red came with it, the whites going, the deep red at the center of a
coal deciding, and she felt it lay its color on her hands and on the brick and
on the iron of the rail, and behind the rail the girl with the copper hair made
a sound and put her hand on her stomach, and the woman in the tan coat put
her arm across the girl, and the boy —

The boy stood up.

He had been sitting on a crate. He stood, on a knee that did not want him,
and came to the rail and put both hands on it, on the iron, inside the quiet,
and looked at her.

And she could see him.

Not his face. Not yet. The charm was on him — she could feel it on him the
way you feel a locked door — and his face slid off her eye the way it had at
the table, at the counter, in her own room. But the *shape* of him. The
shoulders. The hair. The hands on the rail. The way he stood, which was the
way she stood. And the eyes — she could not hold his face but she could hold
his eyes, for the first time, because they were looking at her through the
red and they were not sliding, they were *fixed,* green, furious, terrified,
and they were her mother's eyes and her own and she had not seen them since
they were four years old and looking at her over a man's shoulder in the rain.

"Baby," she said.

---

"Someone clever."

She said it to the man. She had not looked at him yet. She looked now.

Gaunt. Gray in the streak. A beard cut with a knife. Her ring gone off his
throat — she saw the cord and the ring not on it and knew where it was
without looking. A thing of black iron in his fist that he held the way a man
holds a thing that has kept him alive and that he hates.

"Someone close," she said. "Someone who loved it as much as I did." She let
the red rest on him. "Darneil."

The man behind the rail did not move.

Fourteen years she had not said it. Not in the room, not to the brass box,
not to the boy in the rain. She said it now, once, at the flat edge of the
shadow, and it cost her what it cost, and she paid it the way she paid for
everything, and the man it belonged to stood behind his iron and heard his
own name in her mouth and his hand tightened on the black thing until the
knuckles went white in their scars.

"You knew where I was," he said. His voice was rust.

"I've always known where you were."

"Then why —"

"Because you weren't what I wanted." She said it plainly. "I could have come
up that tunnel any night of fourteen years and stood where I'm standing and
looked at you through that rail, and you'd have looked back, and it would have
been the two of us and a piece of iron, and I'd have gone home. You weren't
what I wanted, Darneil. You had what I wanted. On your neck. Then in a box in
a house that smelled of sage. And then, last Saturday, in a car, for forty
minutes, out from under every roof that had ever hidden it — and I felt it,
after fourteen years, like a hand on the back of my neck, and I went up top,
and I *waited tables.*"

She almost smiled.

"I have never in my life done a thing so ridiculous. I did it four days
running. I served the boy coffee and I could not see his face and I stood
three feet from him with a carafe and I could not *see his face,* and I would
do it again, and I am done doing it. That's why I'm here. That's why the
truce is over. Not for you. For him."

"You can't cross it," Darneil said.

"No."

"Then there's nothing here for you."

"There's everything here for me. It just has to come out to me." She turned
her head. "Armand."

---

They brought the fourth stretcher forward.

The man on it was fifty, or near, with a parish jacket still on him and a
wedding ring on his hand and his eyes open on the black ceiling — the drain
man. She had not needed him Saturday. She had taken him anyway and had not
liked it, and had gone back for him afterward, and had put the holding in him
because a thing she did not need was still a thing she could use, and she used
him now.

She knelt beside him on the wet brick.

"Watch," she said, to the rail.

She put her hand on his shoulder.

Gently. The way you turn a person who is looking the wrong way. And his eyes,
which had been on the ceiling, came to hers, because she let them — because
with the holding in him he could not turn his head and she turned it for him,
with one hand, so that he was looking up into the red — and she let him see
it. She let him see her. The one mercy, and not for him. The surface broke.
The person under the person looked out at her, unhidden, the way nobody was
unhidden, a big soft man who had spent thirty years checking drains and had
found out at last what was down there and could not close his eyes on it.

The shadow closed over him.

It did it slowly, at the edge of the light, the way still water closes over a
stone. There was no sound. His eyes stayed open. His chest, which had been
moving, stopped. And on the stretcher on the wet brick of the river way he
lay exactly as he had been lying, with his eyes on the ceiling, and was not
there anymore, and behind the rail the girl with the copper hair said "*No,*"
and the woman in the tan coat said a name — *Terry* — and the boy said
nothing at all.

She stood.

"That's the taking," she said. "You've had it described to you by a man who
saw it at nine years old from the end of a street. Now you've seen it. That's
what I do. That's what it is. It's not a wound. It's not the poison. It's the
one thing that came all the way across the water in a woman's blood and never
got lost, and it's in you, and last night a candle bent."

The boy's hands had not moved on the rail.

"These three," she said, "are alive."

She put her hand on the nearest stretcher. A woman in scrubs, forty, with her
eyes open.

"That's Loretta Broussard. She walks home along the levee from the hospital
because she is not afraid of the levee. Six weeks ago. That one's Curtis
Dupre; he stopped his truck for a girl by the road. And the boy at the end is
Terry's — no. The boy at the end is nobody's. He was waiting for a ride." She
looked at the woman in the tan coat. "You've got them on a door. In red. I
know what's on your door, Jan Walker; I've stood across from your house. They
are alive and they are *held,* and I can let them go, or I can not, and that is
the only thing that has ever been up to me in this whole business, and I am
putting it up to him."

She looked at the boy.

"Come out," she said.

---

"Past the rail. To me. Ten feet. With it off."

The red rested on him. He did not move.

"You take it off yourself, with your own hand, and you step over that iron
where I can see you do it, and you stand where I'm standing, and you let me
look at your face. Once. That's all I've ever wanted. Not your father's piece
of junk. Not the town. Your *face,* baby, plain, the way I saw it for one
half of one second under a coffee-shop lamp when you handed my charm across a
table like a rock you found in a box." Her voice did not rise. It never had.
"You do that, and these three go home. Tonight. Up the stair, in the truck
that old fool has waiting at the top of it. They go home to the beds they were
taken out of and their people get them back and the marks come off the door.
Loretta Broussard walks the levee again and isn't afraid of it."

"And if I don't."

It was the first thing he had said. His voice was thin and it did not shake.

"Then they stay mine. And I go on learning from them what I've been learning,
and tomorrow I take the town, because the town is Armand's now and Armand is
mine, and the day after that I come back to this rail with three more, and the
day after that, and you sit in your father's hole behind your father's iron
and you *watch,* the way you watched just now, until you understand that there
is no rail in the world between you and what you are." She let it sit. "You'll
come out. They always come. I've never been wrong about it. I'm only telling
you what it costs to wait."

"You're my mother."

He said it flat. Not a question.

"I carried you in the room under this one," she said. "Eighteen years and
three months. You came at the dark of the moon with your eyes open and you did
not cry. I don't ask you to believe it. You've got my ears."

Nobody behind the rail moved.

"I'm not coming out," the boy said.

The red did not change.

"Not for them. Not for you." His hands were white on the iron. "I'm sorry.
That's — I'm *sorry,* Miss Broussard, I'm sorry, I don't — but I'm not. I know
what it does if I do. He told me. Once you see me you've got me, for good,
worn or not, and I'm not — I'm not giving you that so you can have a *look.*"
His voice broke on the last word and he let it. "Find another way to see me."

She looked at him for a long time.

Then she did a thing she had not done in a room in forty years. She laughed.
Once. Low, and short, and real, and it went along the river way and the men
with the lamps flinched from it.

"He told you," she said. "Of course he did. He always did know the *rules.*"
She stepped back. One step, off the flat edge of the shadow, into the dark
where the lamps did not reach. "That's fine. That's fine, baby. You've told me
no. Nobody's told me no in a very long time and I find I like the sound of it
in your voice. It's mine." She turned her head. "Armand. The three. We're
going."

"And him?" Armand said. He meant the drain man.

"Leave him. He's Jan Walker's. She'll want to write the time."

They lifted the stretchers. The lamps turned. She stood a moment longer at the
edge of the light with her back to them and the red going down out of her eyes
the way a coal goes down, and she said, not loudly, to the rail, to the boy:

"Not long, baby."

Then the dark took her and the shadow went with her along the brick like a
tide going out, and the river way was a tunnel with four lamps going away down
it and a dead man on a stretcher at the flat line where the tide had stopped.

---

He stood at the rail until the lamps were gone.

He could not have said how long. His hands would not come off the iron. Behind
him he heard Addie breathing the way she breathed when she had been sick and
was not going to be sick again, and Janet saying something low to her, and his
father — his father not moving at all, the way he had not moved since the
woman said his name.

The room was quiet. Not the quiet of the Hilt. The other kind.

"Terry Guidry," Janet said behind him. Flat. "That's Terry Guidry. He lives
on our street. He's — he lived four doors down. His daughter waits for the
bus with —" She stopped. "I took the call. Saturday. I wrote it on my wrist. I
thought he was on the wall."

"He was on the wall," Felix said. "She went back for him."

He did not know how he knew that. He knew it.

He took his hands off the rail. They came off it stiff, the fingers not
wanting to open, and he looked at them in the lamplight, and they were his
hands, a short heavy boy's hands, and they were shaking, and he could not stop
them, and he turned around.

His father was looking at him.

"You said no," his father said.

"Yes."

"To her. At the rail. With three people on the ground." His father's face was
doing a thing Felix could not read. "I've known her twenty years and I've
never once heard anybody say it to her face and be standing after."

"I'm standing."

"You are."

Addie had gotten up. She came to him, not fast, with her hand still on her
stomach and her face the color of the ash, and she stood in front of him, and
she did not touch him, and she looked at him the way she had looked at him at
the tin mirror.

"You're going to do it," she said.

"What?"

"Not tonight. Not for her, not the way she said. But you're going to go out
there." Her voice was very quiet. "I saw it. When she said Loretta Broussard's
name. Before you said no. You're going to find another way, because you said
*find another way* and you meant *I will,* and you don't know it yet and I do."

He did not answer her. He did not have one.

Out in the river way, ten feet past the iron, at the place where the shadow
had lain down like a dog and would go no further, Terry Guidry lay on a
stretcher with his eyes open on a ceiling that was only brick now, and his
wedding ring on his hand, and the lamp from the crypt reaching him and not
quite reaching him, and Felix looked at him over the rail for a long time,
and then went and sat down on the crate with his hand at his collar, over the
stone and the ring, and did not sleep, and did not think, and waited for
morning the way you wait for a thing you have already decided about.
