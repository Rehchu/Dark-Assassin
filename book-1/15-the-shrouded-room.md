# The Shrouded Room

The street ran out at a square.

She had not expected that either. A market square, brick like the street,
with a well in the middle of it — a real well, stone, with a wooden lid gone
soft — and the black mouths of doorways all the way around, and a lantern
hung on a hook by one of them that Armand's man lit as they came into it, so
that for a second the whole square jumped up out of the dark and stood there,
old and empty and ordinary, and then went back to being a place they were
walking across.

The sickness had been getting worse the whole way. It had come on at the
curtain of the hall and gotten worse with every doorway, a slow steady turn
under her breastbone that had gone from *sick* to *something is wrong with me*
to a thing she did not have a word for, and by the square she was walking
with one hand on Felix's arm and the other flat on her stomach and her jaw set
so hard her teeth hurt.

Armand did not touch her. She noticed that. He walked a step ahead with the
sunglasses on and the lantern-man beside him and the other man behind, and
when she stumbled on a lifted brick at the edge of the square and went down on
one knee he stopped, and turned, and stood there, and did not put out a hand.
Felix got her up. Armand watched him do it.

"She's fine," Armand said. "She walks. Those are the rules."

"Whose rules?"

He did not answer that. He went to the far side of the square, past the well,
to a doorway that was not a doorway — a place where the brick had come apart,
a long crooked gap in the wall of an old building that went back into the
earth behind it, and then down, the lantern showing rough stone going down at
an angle you could barely walk, wet, with the marks of hands on it.

"Her way," Armand said. "Not ours. You go first. Then him. I'm behind."

She looked at the gap. The sickness came up out of it like heat off a stove.

"I can't," she said.

"You can. You're a Walker. You've got it worse than any of us and you can
still stand up, and that's the only reason you're going in there on your own
feet instead of over my shoulder." Something went across his face under the
glasses, and was gone. "I'm not allowed to carry you. Go."

She went.

---

It was not far. That was the thing she would try to explain, afterward, and
never manage: it was not far, not the way the stair had been far, ninety-four
steps of it. It was a hundred feet of crooked stone going down at a slant, and
then a turn, and then a place where the stone was wet and the air was wet and
the sound of water was close, and then cloth.

A curtain. Gray. Hung across the end of the crack where it opened into
something, and the lantern-man stopped at it and did not go through, and
Armand came past her and put his hand on the cloth and held it back.

"In," he said. "Both of you."

She went in with Felix's hand in hers and the sickness so bad she could not
feel her feet.

The room was dark, and it was not dark. One light — a fat candle in a dish of
black glass, low, on a low table, throwing more shadow than light — and the
walls all around it hung with cloth, cloth on cloth on cloth, gray, the color
gone out of all of it so long ago that gray was the only word, and the cloth
ate the light and ate the sound of their feet and ate, she thought, the sound
of her own heart, which should have been the loudest thing in the room and
was not.

On the table: a small bottle, stoppered, the color of tea. A folding knife
with a pale handle. A length of cord. A little brass box.

And behind the table, sitting on the floor with her legs folded under her and
her hands open on the wood, palms up, the way you sit when you are waiting for
something to be put into them — a woman.

"Back again are we," the woman said.

---

Addie's stomach turned over so hard she made a sound.

It was the voice. Low, and slow, and warm in a way that made you want to say
yes to it — the voice that had said *pastries,* the voice that had said *what a
lovely stone,* the voice that had said *y'all come back* to a steam wand while
they went out the door. She knew it before she knew anything else. She knew it
before her eyes had finished finding the woman in the candlelight, and then
her eyes found her, and she did not know her at all.

No apron. No flour handprint. No name tag with LEE on it in curly letters. A
gray shirt, and black jeans, and a belt at the waist that was not a belt but a
row of small dark shapes, and long black hair down over the shoulders, so
black it took the candle and gave nothing back, and a face —

The face was the face. Tall, even sitting. Dark. Brown eyes. The face from
across the counter. The face from three feet away with a carafe of water in
its hand.

It was the same lady as yesterday with the black hair.

Not yesterday. Friday. Thursday. A coffee shop on Third Street with string
lights and a chalkboard and a manager who looked at the clock — that woman,
this woman, sitting on the floor of a room under a square under a street under
an orphanage with a knife on the table in front of her, and she had come up
to their table and asked if they wanted refills.

*Do I know you?* Addie had said, on Thursday. *Are you just coming on your
shift?*

"You," Addie said. It came out of her like something torn. "You're — you're
from the —"

"I'm from a lot of places, baby." The woman did not move. Her hands stayed open
on the wood. "Ugly Mug's just the one you saw me in."

She was not looking at Addie when she said it. She was looking at Felix.

And Addie watched it happen. She had seen it once before, at a table, under a
lamp, and had not had a word for it then and had one now: the woman's eyes
went to him, and went *off* him. They went to his hoodie and his bandaged knee
and the hand at his collar and the hair on the sides of his face, and slid,
and slid, the way rain slides off a window, and could not stay. Three feet
from him in her own room in her own candlelight, and she could not hold his
face in her eye, and Addie saw her not be able to, and saw something in the
woman's own face that was not anger and not sorrow and was older than both.

Then the eyes came to Addie.

And stayed.

They did not blink. They did not move. They came to her face and settled there
the way they had at the table, the way they had at the counter, steady and
direct and *pleased,* the eyes of a person looking at a thing they can see all
the way to the back of, and Addie felt them go all the way to the back of her,
and could not look away.

"You I can see just fine," the woman said.

---

Felix had gone white.

Not pale. White, the way he had gone at the table on Friday when the woman
straightened up from beside him, the color of the mug, and the red coming up
his neck from under the collar where the cord went. He was standing very
still beside her with her hand crushed in his and his other hand flat at his
throat over the stone and he was looking at the woman on the floor the way you
look at a car coming.

"It was you," he said.

"It was me."

"Friday. At the — you leaned down and you —"

"I did."

"Why."

The woman smiled. It was a small smile and it did not reach anything.

"I said I'd hear you," she said.

Addie did not know what that meant. She looked at Felix and he knew what it
meant. She saw him know it. She saw it go into him and stop somewhere under
his breastbone and stay there, and she understood, standing in the gray room
with the sickness in her and the candle bending in some draft she could not
feel, that whatever the woman had whispered to him at that table on Friday
with Addie's face turned to the window, it was *this* — this was the other end
of it — and he had carried it four days without a word, and he was not going
to say it now.

"Felix."

He did not look at her.

"Felix, what did she —"

"Not now." His voice was gone to nothing. "Addie. Not — not here."

The woman watched them. She watched them the way you watch two people through
a window, from a warm room, with the whole of your attention and none of your
body. Then she lifted one hand off the table and did a small thing with it,
to Armand, a turn of the fingers, and Armand came forward with the backpack.

Addie had not felt it come off her. She reached for it and the hand that was
not holding Felix's closed on nothing.

The woman opened it. She took the folder out and laid it on the low table
beside the knife and opened it, and the candle laid its light across the
papers — ALEXANDRIA ORPHANAGE — and she did not look at them. She turned
them over. Under them, the envelopes, the three sealed and the one broken, the
red wax on each one with its mark, and the woman looked at the wax.

Her face did nothing.

It was the most frightening thing Addie had ever seen a face do. It did not
soften. It did not harden. She looked at the mark pressed into the wax on
three envelopes that had been in a box in a hall closet in the Garden District
for fourteen years, and her face did nothing at all, the way a still pond does
nothing when you look into it, and then she closed the folder and put it back
into the pack and zipped the pack and set it on the floor and slid it across
the brick to Addie's feet with one hand.

She had not said a word about it. She did not now.

"Take your things," she said. "Both of you. Nobody here wants them."

---

The woman's hair moved.

It was the draft — the same draft that bent the candle — coming from the crack
behind them or from somewhere Addie could not see, and it lifted the long black
hair off the side of the woman's face for one second, the way wind lifts a
curtain, and laid it back down.

And Addie saw the ear.

She would not have seen it if she had not spent four years not looking at
Felix's. That was the truth of it. She had trained herself, freshman year, in
a church van with the youth pastor driving, not to look at the sides of his
head, and the training had a shape now, a place in her where the not-looking
lived, and when the hair lifted off the woman's face the not-looking went
straight to it like a tongue to a bad tooth.

Pointed. The top of it. Not much — not a cartoon, not a thing you would see
across a room. The way his were. Exactly the way his were, under the hair he
had worn long since he was twelve, that he pulled out from under the cord of
the necklace every time he put it on.

The hair came down. The woman had not moved. She had not seen the draft do it,
or she had, and did not care, or she had let it.

Addie did not say anything.

She stood there holding Felix's hand with the sickness roaring in her and the
folder at her feet and the thing she had just seen going down into her like a
stone into a well, and she did not say a word, because there was nobody in the
room to say it to who did not already know.

---

"Take them back up."

Armand had not moved from the curtain. Now he did.

"Back up," he said. "You said bring them. I brought them. I brought them down
your way, both of them, on their feet, not a mark on her, the way you said —"

"And now I'm saying take them back up. All the way. To the hall, to your
brother, and you put them in his hands and you tell him I let them go." The
woman had not raised her voice. She never did. "That's the third term,
Armand. She takes some and she lets some go. Tonight she lets go."

"You *said* —"

"I said I wanted him in a room with me." Her eyes had not left Addie's face.
"I've had that. I've waited a long time for it and I've had it, and I know what
I know now, and it's enough for tonight. It isn't going to be enough tomorrow.
But it's tonight." A pause. "Not a mark on her. Not one. She walks up the way
she walked down and if she goes down on a knee you stand there and let her,
the way you did on the square. I saw that. I see everything you do down here,
Armand, and I see a lot of what you do up there."

Armand stood in the curtain with his jaw working. He was furious. Addie could
see it from across the room, the fury coming off him like heat, a man who had
carried a thing down a crack in the dark for a year of nights and been told
to carry it back up — and he was going to obey. That was the other thing she
could see. He was going to obey her the way the room obeyed her, the way the
candle did, the way the cloth on the walls did.

"Yes, ma'am," he said.

"Go on, baby."

She said it to Felix. She said it looking at Addie. Her hands were open on the
wood again, palms up, the way they had been when the curtain lifted.

"Go on up. You'll come back." She said it the way you say the weather. "They
always come back to me. It's the one thing I've never once been wrong about."

Addie picked up the pack. She put it on, both straps, with her wrapped wrist
screaming, and she took Felix by the arm, because he was not moving, and she
turned him toward the curtain, and she walked him out of the room of gray
cloth into the crack with Armand ahead of them and the sickness so bad now that
the stone came up to meet her and she took it on her hands and kept going.

Behind them, through the cloth, no sound at all. Not a breath. The room had
eaten it.

---

She did not remember the crack. She remembered the square, the lantern going
ahead of them across it, the well with its rotten lid, and then the street,
the doorways, the long walk back toward the curtain of the hall with the
sickness draining out of her a doorway at a time, the way it had drained on
the sidewalk Thursday, like water out of a sink, until by the swan bottle on
its sill she could walk without her hand on him.

He had not said a word.

She let him not say it for two hundred yards. Then she stopped, in the middle
of the street, with Armand ten feet ahead and the lantern-man and the other
one closing up behind, and she made him look at her.

"She's the waitress."

"Yes."

"She's the waitress from Ugly Mug. She's *Lee.* She brought us our coffee.
She sent us to the pawn shop. She's been — " Her voice went and she got it
back. "She was across the street from my house. Thursday. Before. That was
her. That's who I —" She stopped. "You knew her voice."

"Yes."

"You knew it *Friday.* Whatever she said to you at that table, you knew it was
her the second she opened her mouth down there, and you didn't — Felix, you let
me walk into that room —"

"I didn't know she'd be *there.* I didn't know she'd be *anywhere.* I thought
she was a — I thought she was a *waitress,* Addie, I thought she was a waitress
who said a weird thing to me and I didn't want to —" He stopped. His hand was
at his collar. "I can't. Not here. Not with them. I'll tell you. I swear to
God I'll tell you all of it, every word, but not on this street with him
listening."

Armand was listening. He had turned around with the sunglasses on and his head
tilted, ten feet up the street, the way a dog listens.

She let go of Felix's arm.

She had seen the ear. She had it in her like a stone in a well. She looked at
the boy she had loved for three years and known for four, at the hair over the
sides of his face, at the hand he could not keep off his throat, and she
thought about a woman on a floor who could not hold his face in her eye and had
looked at Addie instead, all the way to the back of her, and had said *I can
see you just fine,* like a promise.

"Okay," she said. "Not here."

They walked. The hall's curtain came up out of the dark ahead of them with the
light around its edges like a welcome mat, and Anatole's people were standing
in front of it, six of them, with a look on them Addie had not seen before —
not afraid, or not only — and Armand walked up to them with the two of them
behind him and stopped, and said, to the biggest of the six, in a voice that
had all the *no worries* gone out of it:

"She let them go. Tell my brother."

Then he turned and went back down the street the way they had come, fast, with
the lantern-man and the other one, toward the square and the well and the crack
in the wall, and did not look back, and the six let Addie and Felix through
the curtain into the light.
