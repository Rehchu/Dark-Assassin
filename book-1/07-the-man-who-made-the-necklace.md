# The Man Who Made The Necklace


The shopkeeper explained how he created the necklace for a lady some time ago. She wanted to stay hidden from the world, and remain undetectable.

He said it plainly, the way a man reads a receipt. Then he stopped, and the plainness went out of his voice.

*Hidden.* Felix heard the word land in the dim shop among the guitars and the
birdcage and the folded flag and stay there. A woman who wanted to be hidden
from the world. Not lost, not gone. Hidden — which was a thing somebody did on
purpose, to themselves, because of somebody else.

"I know my own work," he said. "I made this string. I carved that stone. I have not seen it in a very long time."

Felix looked at Addie. Addie looked at Felix. Neither of them said anything.

The man came around the counter. He was older than he had looked from across the room, with white in his beard and grease worked into the lines of his hands, and he moved like the shop was a thing he had to be careful in. He put the necklace down on a square of felt and turned the little lamp on it.

"Sit," he said, and pointed at two stools. "You are going to want to sit."

They sat. Felix had his hands in his lap. Addie had hers on the counter, and she kept looking at the door.

The stools were the tall kind, wood, worn pale where a hundred years of people
had sat on them to be told what their grandmother's ring was worth. The counter
came to his chest. Under the glass, in the light of the two small lamps, were
trays of other people's rings, and a tray of pocketknives, and a tray of
military medals on ribbons gone the color of tea, each with a little paper tag.
The shop ticked. Somewhere in the back a refrigerator ran. And behind him, in
the window, he could feel the unicorns turning, five little white horses going
around in air that did not move, though he did not look.

His hand kept going to his collar. There was nothing there. The necklace was
two feet from him on a square of black felt with a lamp on it, and his throat
felt the way it had in the second before he handed it over — open, and cold,
and pointed at.

"A woman came in here a long time ago," the man said. "Near closing, when the light was going. She did not want a ring fixed or a watch sized. She wanted to be gone." He turned the obsidian bead so the little fork-shaped mark caught the lamp. "So I made her a thing that would do it. The beads are ordinary. The stone is not, and neither is what I put in it."

A woman near closing, when the light was going. Felix saw her without wanting
to — no face, because the man had not given her one, just a shape in the door
of this shop with the unicorns turning behind her, wanting to be gone. He
thought of the wax on the envelopes. He thought of the hidden writing, a hand
that had been taught to hide. Everything about the woman had been made to not
be found, and here was the man who had done part of the making, and here was
Felix, with her letters in a bag by his foot.

"What did you put in it?" Addie asked.

"A protection," the man said. "The old kind. My grandmother's kind." He looked at Addie for a second like he was deciding something about her, and then he looked back at the stone. "You want the short of it. As long as this is on you, you are hard to find. A quiet thing in a loud room. Take it off, and you are a candle in a dark house, and anyone who is looking will see you burning."

He stopped. He looked at the necklace on the felt between them, and then at
Felix's collar, and something in his face went hard and fast.

"Put it on."

"What?"

"Put it on. Now." He pushed the necklace across the felt with two fingers, the
way you push a thing you do not want to be holding. "I am not saying another
word to you with that lying on my counter. Put it on."

Felix put it on. His hands were not quite steady with the cord and it caught
in his hair and he pulled the hair free and let the stone drop back into the
hollow of his throat, where it had been an hour ago, and the cold went out of
his neck as if a door had shut. He felt it. He did not know what he felt, but
he felt it, and across the counter he saw the man's shoulders come down a
notch, the way a man's shoulders come down when a pot he was watching finally
stops rattling.

"Good," the man said. "Good."

Felix touched the beads at his throat. He had put the necklace back on at the coffee shop, joking, and he had not taken it off since. It felt heavier now than it had this morning.

"Who's looking?" Felix asked.

"I do not know," the man said. "She did not tell me, and I did not ask. People who come to me at closing do not want questions." He straightened up. "But I will tell you what I told her. Do not take it off. Not to shower. Not to sleep. Not because a girl at a party thinks it is ugly." He almost smiled. "You keep it on."

"I have been," Felix said. "There was a note. It said the same thing."

"Then whoever gave it to you did right by you." The man's eyes went to Felix's face and held there a moment too long. To the shape of his ears, and away again, fast, like a man who has looked at the sun. He did not say anything about it. Felix felt his neck go hot anyway.

*He'll tell you —*

The woman's voice from this morning came back at his ear, warm and low and
close, and he shut it out before it could finish. He did not want it in this
room. But it had gotten one thing in before he closed the door on it: she had
known. Whatever else she had whispered over his shoulder with Addie turned
away, she had known exactly what this man would say when the stone came under
his lamp. She had known it before Felix knew this man was alive.

And she had looked at his ears too. Everyone looked at his ears. Nobody said
the word. He had lived eighteen years inside that silence and he had never
once, until this week, felt it looking back.

Addie reached into the folder Felix had brought and pulled out the envelopes. "There was more," she said. "These came with it."

She spread them on the counter. Three of them, thin, the paper gone soft at the corners, and each one closed with a circle of red wax pressed with a mark none of them could read.

The man went still.

He did not pick them up. He looked at the seal the way you look at a snake you were not expecting on a path, and for a second Felix thought he was going to tell them to put the letters away and get out. His hand came up halfway and stopped.

"Where did you get these," the man said. It was not a question this time.

"Same box," Felix said. "My parents left me a box when I moved out. The necklace was in it. So were the letters. I can't read them. They're not in any language I know."

"No," the man said quietly. "They would not be." He touched the edge of one envelope with one finger, just the paper, not the wax, and drew his hand back. "I have seen that seal one other time in my life. On a paper the woman signed before she left. I did not read that one either." He looked up, and there was something in his face that had not been there when they walked in. Not curiosity. Closer to fear. "You are the boy who had the necklace, and you have her letters, and you have her look. Do you understand what I am telling you? You are hers."

*Hers.*

The word went through him and out the other side and left a hole the shape of
itself. He had been somebody's for eighteen years — Robert and Diane's, the boy
in the school pictures, the kid who alphabetized his bookshelf because they
alphabetized the spices. Now an old man in a pawn shop was telling him he was
a stranger's, a woman's, a shape in a doorway who had wanted to be gone, and
he had her look, whatever her look was, and he had never seen her face. Under
the counter Addie's hand found his knee and stayed there, and he did not look
at her, because if he looked at her he would have to have a face, and he did
not have one ready.

"Whose?" Addie said. "You keep saying her. Her who?"

"I do not have a name to give you," the man said, "and if I did I would not say it in this room with the door unlocked." He gathered the letters into a stack, careful, and pushed them back across the felt to Felix. "Take these. Keep them with the stone. Do not show them around like you showed me."

Felix folded them into the folder. His hands were not quite steady. "You have to give us something," he said. "You can't just tell me I'm in danger and send me home."

He heard himself. He sounded like a kid at a counter asking for something back.
But it was true. He had come in here with a rock and a note, and he was going
to walk out with a rock and a note and the word *hers,* and a bag full of
letters he could not read from a woman he could not name, and a warning to
keep the thing on that he had already been given by somebody who, it turned
out, was wrong or was right and there was no way to know which. He needed a
door. One door. Anywhere.

The man was quiet for a moment. Then he wiped his hands on the rag at his belt, the way he had when they came in, and Felix understood it was a thing he did when he was thinking.

"She told me where she had been," he said. "Not where she was going. Where she had been. She said it like it still had a hold on her." He looked at Felix. "There was a place in this town for children who had no people. It is not there anymore. They tore it down and built apartments on the lot, on the east side, off the river road. If she left anything, she left it there. That is all I have, and it is more than I should have said."

Felix knew the place. He had a folder full of paper that started with the words ALEXANDRIA ORPHANAGE in bold at the top. He had been trying not to think about it since the phone call at his kitchen table, and now a stranger in a pawn shop was pointing him straight at it.

"We know it," Addie said. "It's the Riverbend complex now. My mom looked at a unit there once."

Something crossed the man's face when she said mom, and it was gone before Felix could name it.

Felix saw it and had nowhere to put it. Addie's mother. Janet Walker, who
worked nights answering the phone for the whole parish and looked at you when
you came in the door like she was writing you down; who had never, in four
years, said one unkind word to him and had never quite said a kind one either.
Something in this man knew her. Felix filed it the way Addie filed things, and
did not know he had learned that from her.

"Then you know the way," the man said. "Go in the daytime. Do not go making a lot of noise. And you." He looked at Felix. "The necklace stays on you the whole time. Do you hear me? If you feel like taking it off, that is the exact moment you do not."

"I hear you," Felix said.

The man nodded once, like a thing was settled, and started turning off the little lamps along the counter. It was the middle of the afternoon. The shop was suddenly a place that wanted them out of it.

They stood. Addie already had a hand on the door.

"Thank you," Felix said, because he did not know what else to say to a man who had just told him his whole life was a lie built around a rock.

The man did not answer. He was looking past them, out the front window, at Main Street, at nothing Felix could see. His jaw had gone tight.

Felix looked where he was looking. Through the unicorns, through the saxophone
and the plates and the gray velvet board of watches, Main Street lay flat and
white in the noon glare with nothing on it. Across the way, the storefronts
had deep doorways, and the sun was straight down, and the doorways were the
only shade on the street. In one of them there was a shape. Tall. Standing the
way you stand when you are not waiting for anything. He blinked, because the
glare made him, and when his eyes opened the doorway was a doorway, and the
cup in the gutter had not moved, and the man behind the counter had not looked
away from it.

"Go home a different way than you came," the man said. "Both of you."

Addie pushed the door open and the bell over it rang, too loud. Felix followed her out into the light. The heat came down on them like a hand. Behind him he heard the lock turn, and then the second lock, and then a chain.

Three sounds, one after another, and each one was the sound of a man deciding
he had been safer before they came. Felix stood on the sidewalk with the heat
on the back of his neck and the stone at the front of it and felt, for the
first time in his life, locked out of somewhere — not kept out, locked out,
which was different, which was a thing a person did with their hands because of
you. Main Street went both ways in the white glare and nothing moved on it.
The doorway across the street was a doorway.

The little unicorn mobile in the window turned slowly on its string. There was no wind. Not a breath of it, not a car passing, not an open door. The street was still as a held breath.

But the unicorn turned, once all the way around, and then it stopped, pointing at Felix.

"Get on," Addie said. She was already on the bike. "I don't like this street."

He looked at the doorway once more. He could not have said why. There was
nothing in it, and there had been nothing in it, and the cup in the gutter sat
where it had sat, and the flag on the building at the corner hung dead, and the
whole of Main Street had the held-breath stillness of a room where somebody has
just stopped talking.

Felix got on. He grabbed her around the waist, and this time he did not think about how she drove. He was thinking about a lot next to a river with apartments on it, and a woman he had never met who had his look, and a rock at his throat he was never supposed to take off.

She went a different way. She went the way the man had said, east instead of
south, down to the levee and along the river road with the water on their left
behind the grass bank and the town on their right, and she did not lane-split,
and she did not zip, and she rode like a person taking somebody home. The road
dipped once, at a low place where the storm drains sat in the curb with their
iron mouths open, and came up again. Then a sign went by on the right — brick
pillars, a brass plaque he could not read at that speed, three new buildings
behind a strip of young trees, and a name in raised letters: RIVERBEND.

She slowed. She did not stop. Neither of them said anything. He felt her look
at it through the back of her helmet and he looked at it too, the three
buildings on their lot, ordinary, with cars in front of them and a woman
carrying groceries up an outside stair, and then the trees took it, and the
road went on toward the Garden District and his apartment and the folder that
would be waiting on his table when he opened the bag.

*Go in the daytime,* the man had said. It was the daytime now. They kept
going.

He did not take it off.
